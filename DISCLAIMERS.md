# Disclaimer & Disclosure Requirements for Distributed (Pay-Per-Call) Local-Service Sites

**THIS IS RESEARCH TO INFORM A BUSINESS DECISION. IT IS NOT LEGAL ADVICE, AND I AM NOT A LAWYER.**
Nothing here creates an attorney-client relationship. Every factual statement below is tied to a page that was fetched while writing this file, with the URL next to it. Where authorities conflict or where I could not confirm something, that is stated plainly rather than smoothed over. Before switching 40 legal-vertical sites to distributed call routing, this needs review by an ethics/advertising lawyer licensed in TX, CA, FL and VA at minimum. State bars, not the FTC, are the near-term risk here.

Scope assumed as given: 83 live sites, one trade + one city each (garage door Fort Worth TX; motorcycle accident lawyers Houston TX; dog bite lawyers San Diego CA; emergency plumbers San Jose CA; AC repair Sacramento CA; criminal defense Tampa FL; car accident lawyers Virginia Beach VA), 43 home services / 40 legal, each with its own domain and unique tracking number, copy written in first person as the provider ("we fix your garage door"), no disclaimer anywhere.

## 1. Plain-English answer

**Yes — distributing calls instead of sending them to one named tenant changes what these sites are, and the sites cannot stay as they are.**

The single-tenant model was defensible because the site could truthfully be an advertisement *for that one business*, whose name and license could appear on it. Once a call is auctioned into a ring tree and can land at any one of several unrelated businesses, the site stops being an advertisement for a provider and becomes a **referral/lead-distribution intermediary that is describing itself as the provider**. That is the exact fact pattern that lawyer-advertising rules, state contractor-licensing statutes, and the FTC's deception framework all treat as a problem.

Concretely:

**Legal-vertical sites (40 sites; here: Houston TX, San Diego CA, Tampa FL, Virginia Beach VA)** — this is the serious one.

- Distributing calls among multiple unrelated firms is very likely operating a **lawyer referral service**, which in **Texas requires a state certificate** and in **California requires State Bar certification** — and both regimes are effectively closed to a for-profit operator (Texas requires a governmental entity or a 501(c)(3)/(4)/(6) nonprofit with at least 25 lawyers from different firms; California requires certification under State Bar minimum standards). See §2.1 and §2.2. So in TX and CA the answer is not "add a disclaimer" — it is that **the distributed-call model in the legal vertical is likely prohibited outright, disclaimer or not**.
- **Florida** does allow a for-profit "qualifying provider," but conditions it on registration with the Bar, a minimum of four lawyers from four different firms, disclosure of the referred lawyer's office location at the moment of connection, and — decisively for your copy — a rule that the provider must **not** use a name or communicate in any way that could lead people to think it is a law firm or provides legal services. Your current copy does exactly that. See §2.3.
- Every one of these sites must, at minimum, carry the **name and contact information / primary practice location of a specific lawyer or law firm responsible for the content** (ABA MR 7.2(d), TX 7.02(a), CA 7.2(c), AZ ER 7.2(c), NC 7.2(d)). A site with only a tracking number and no identified responsible lawyer fails this even today.
- Texas adds an affirmative disclosure duty that maps directly onto ring-tree routing: a communication is misleading if the lawyer knows a responding prospect **is likely to be referred to a lawyer in another firm** and fails to disclose it. Georgia has a formal opinion saying the same thing. See §2.1, §2.5.
- Texas also requires ads to be **filed with the Bar's Advertising Review Committee** (§2.1).

**Home-services sites (43 sites; here: Fort Worth TX, San Jose CA, Sacramento CA)** — fixable, but not by disclaimer alone in California.

- **California makes it a misdemeanor for any person to advertise for construction work covered by the Contractors State License Law unless that person holds a license in the classification advertised**, and "advertise" is defined to include electronic transmission. A first-person "we fix your AC in Sacramento" page run by an unlicensed marketing company is squarely inside that text. Adding "we are a lead generation service" is the right direction but the safest posture is to stop claiming to perform work at all. See §6.1.
- **Nevada** (expansion market) goes further and requires an express negative disclosure: an unlicensed person who advertises to perform construction work **"must state in the advertisement that he or she is not licensed."** That is the strictest single wording requirement found anywhere in this research and is what the home-services draft language below is built around. See §6.5.
- **Florida and Arizona** require the contractor's license/registration number in advertising; Texas requires the affiliated ACR licensee's number in HVAC advertising and requires a licensed RMP behind any plumbing solicitation. If you cannot put a real license number on the page, you must not present the page as an offer to perform the licensed work. See §6.
- Garage door repair in Texas has no state contractor license I could confirm (see "What I could not confirm"), which is why that vertical is the lowest-risk of the three home-services trades.

**Both verticals:** the FTC framework prohibits creating a false net impression about who you are and about affiliation, and FTC staff has said specifically that lead generators should "disclose clearly to consumers who you are and how you will share their information" (§3). Google separately (a) will not give a Business Profile to lead-generation agents, (b) forbids passing or selling Local Services leads to another business, and (c) treats city/trade doorway networks funneling to one destination as spam (§5). Recording calls is fine in TX, but **CA, FL, WA, MA and PA are all-party-consent states**, so recorded ring-tree calls need an announcement (§4.3).

**Minimum change set if you proceed:** (a) do **not** distribute legal calls in TX or CA; (b) rewrite all copy from "we perform the service" to "we connect you with," (c) add an above-the-fold, near-the-phone-number disclosure that the site is a paid advertising/lead-referral service, not the provider, and that calls are routed to third parties who pay for them, (d) add a recording announcement, (e) put a real identified responsible lawyer or a real licensed contractor + license number on any page that still reads as an offer to perform the licensed service.

## 2. Area 1 — Lawyer advertising, referral and lead-generation rules

### 2.0 ABA model baseline

ABA Model Rule 7.2(b) permits paying only for advertising and for "the usual charges of a legal service plan or a not-for-profit or qualified lawyer referral service," and 7.2(d) states: "Any communication made under this Rule must include the name and contact information of at least one lawyer or law firm responsible for its content" ([ABA Model Rule 7.2](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_2_advertising/)).

The controlling comment for lead generation is Comment [5]:

> "A lawyer may pay others for generating client leads, such as Internet-based client leads, as long as the lead generator does not recommend the lawyer, any payment to the lead generator is consistent with Rules 1.5(e) (division of fees) and 5.4 (professional independence of the lawyer), and the lead generator's communications are consistent with Rule 7.1 (communications concerning a lawyer's services). To comply with Rule 7.1, a lawyer must not pay a lead generator that states, implies, or creates a reasonable impression that it is recommending the lawyer, is making the referral without payment from the lawyer, or has analyzed a person's legal problems when determining which lawyer should receive the referral."
> — [ABA Model Rule 7.2, Comment on Rule 7.2](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_2_advertising/comment_on_rule_7_2/)

Read against a bid auction: a site that says "we fix / we represent," carries reviews, and then routes the caller to whoever bid highest is at risk on all three prongs — it implies a recommendation, it does not disclose that the connection is paid for, and site copy implying case evaluation implies analysis of the person's problem. Related rules: [Rule 7.1 (communications concerning a lawyer's services)](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_1_communication_concerning_a_lawyer_s_services/) and [Rule 5.4 (professional independence)](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_5_4_professional_independence_of_a_lawyer/). ABA Formal Opinion 501 addresses a lawyer's responsibility under Rules 8.4(a) and 5.3 for the conduct of lead generators engaged on the lawyer's behalf ([ABA Formal Op. 501 PDF](https://images.law.com/contrib/content/uploads/documents/399/77701/aba-formal-opinion-501.pdf)).

New York State Bar Ethics Opinion 1131 permits participation in lead-generation platforms only where the matching is transparent and mechanical rather than a recommendation, and notes NY Rule 7.1(h) requires the name, principal law office address and telephone number of the lawyer whose services are being offered ([NYSBA Ethics Opinion 1131](https://nysba.org/ethics-opinion-1131/)).

### 2.1 TEXAS (Houston motorcycle accident site; also Fort Worth for the trades section)

**(a)/(c) Disclosure duty triggered by routing to another firm.** Texas Rule 7.01 Comment 4:

> "A communication offering legal services is misleading if, at the time a lawyer makes the communication, the lawyer knows or reasonably should know, but fails to disclose, that a prospective client responding to the communication is likely to be referred to a lawyer in another firm."
> — [Texas Center for Legal Ethics, Rule 7.01](https://legalethicstexas.com/resources/rules/texas-disciplinary-rules-of-professional-conduct/communications-concerning-a-lawyers-services/)

This is the single most on-point sentence found for the distributed-call question in any of your states.

**(c) Identification.** Texas Rule 7.02(a): "An advertisement of legal services shall publish the name of a lawyer who is responsible for the content of the advertisement and identify the lawyer's primary practice location." ([Texas Rule 7.02](https://legalethicstexas.com/resources/rules/texas-disciplinary-rules-of-professional-conduct/advertisements/))

**(a) Payment for referrals.** Texas Rule 7.03(e) bars paying a non-lawyer "for soliciting or referring prospective clients," excepting advertising/PR fees and "usual charges of a lawyer referral service that meets the requirements of Texas law" ([Texas Rule 7.03](https://legalethicstexas.com/resources/rules/texas-disciplinary-rules-of-professional-conduct/solicitation-and-other-prohibited-communications/)).

**(b) Referral service licensing — the hard blocker.** Texas Occupations Code Chapter 952:

> §952.002(1): "'Lawyer referral service' means a person or the service provided by the person that refers potential clients to lawyers regardless of whether the person uses the term 'referral service'"
> §952.101: "A person may not operate a lawyer referral service in this state unless the person holds a certificate issued under this chapter."
> §952.102(a): applicant must be a governmental entity or a nonprofit exempt under §501(c)(3), (4) or (6), and must submit a list of at least 25 lawyers from different firms.
> §952.151(a) required statement in all advertising: "This service is certified as a lawyer referral service as required by the State of Texas under Chapter 952, Occupations Code."
> — [Texas Occupations Code Ch. 952 (PDF)](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/OC/pdf/OC.952.pdf)

§952.003(4) excludes "lawyers who jointly advertise their services in a manner that clearly shows that the advertising is intended solely to solicit clients for those lawyers" — i.e., joint advertising is an escape hatch **only if the participating lawyers are identified on the ad**. A blind bid auction among unnamed firms does not fit that exclusion on its face. §952.203 provides for injunctive relief.

**(c) Filing.** Texas Rule 7.04 requires filing advertisements and solicitation communications with the Advertising Review Committee within 10 days, with a fee ([Texas Rule 7.04](https://legalethicstexas.com/resources/rules/texas-disciplinary-rules-of-professional-conduct/filing-requirements-for-advertisements-and-solicitation-communications/)). The exemptions rule (7.05) page returned a client error and I could not read the exemption list — see "What I could not confirm."

**(d) Trade name / site name.** Texas Rule 7.01(c) permits trade names that are not false or misleading ([Texas Rule 7.01](https://legalethicstexas.com/resources/rules/texas-disciplinary-rules-of-professional-conduct/communications-concerning-a-lawyers-services/)). A domain like a city+practice-area name is not automatically improper; what makes it improper is the combination with first-person "we represent you" copy and undisclosed routing (Comment 4 above).

### 2.2 CALIFORNIA (San Diego dog bite site)

**(b) Certification requirement.** Business & Professions Code §6155(a):

> "An individual, partnership, corporation, association, or any other nongovernmental entity shall not operate for the direct or indirect purpose, in whole or in part, of referring potential clients to attorneys, and no attorney shall accept a referral of such potential clients, unless all of the following requirements are met: (1) The service is certified by the State Bar of California and is operated in conformity with minimum standards for a lawyer referral service established by the State Bar and approved by the Supreme Court. (2) The combined charges to the potential client by the referral service and the attorney to whom the potential client is referred do not exceed the total cost that the client would normally pay if no referral service were involved."
> — [Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §6155](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=6155)

**The line between legal joint advertising and illegal referral is drawn by naming the firms.** §6155(h):

> "(h) This section shall not be construed to prohibit attorneys from jointly advertising their services. (1) Permissible joint advertising, among other things, identifies by name the advertising attorneys or law firms whom the consumer of legal services may select and initiate contact with. (2) Certifiable referral activity involves, among other things, some person or entity other than the consumer and advertising attorney or law firms which, in person, electronically, or otherwise, refers the consumer to an attorney or law firm not identified in the advertising."
> — [Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §6155](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=6155)

A ring-tree auction is definitionally a connection to "an attorney or law firm not identified in the advertising." Note §6155 binds **both** the operator and the participating attorney ("no attorney shall accept a referral").

**(a)/(c) Rules of Professional Conduct.** CRPC 7.2(b) permits paying advertising costs and the usual charges of a "qualified lawyer referral service" meeting State Bar Minimum Standards; 7.2(c): "Any communication made pursuant to this rule shall include the name and address of at least one lawyer or law firm responsible for its content." ([State Bar of California, RPC Chapter 7](https://www.calbar.ca.gov/legal-professionals/rules/rules-professional-conduct/current-rules-professional-conduct/chapter-7-information-about-legal-services))

### 2.3 FLORIDA (Tampa criminal defense site) — the state with an explicit "must not look like a law firm" rule

Rule 4-7.22 governs "qualifying providers," defined to include anyone who receives consideration for directly or indirectly referring prospective clients, including "providing tips or leads for prospective clients" ([The Florida Bar, Qualifying Providers / Lawyer Referral Services](https://www.floridabar.org/ethics/etad/qualifying-providers-lawyer-referral-services/)).

The operative condition, confirmed in the Bar's own 2025 Advertising Handbook:

> "(11) does not use a name or engage in any communication with the public that could lead prospective clients to reasonably conclude that the qualifying provider is a law firm or directly provides legal services to the public;"
> — [The Florida Bar Advertising Handbook 2025 (PDF)](https://www-media.floridabar.org/uploads/2025/12/Handbook-2025-Approved-by-SCA-12-10-25.pdf)

> "(10) affirmatively discloses to the prospective client at the time a referral, match or other connection is made of the location of a bona fide office by city, town or county of the lawyer to whom the referral, match or other connection is being made;"
> — [Rule 4-7.22 text (Florida Justice Association mirror)](https://floridajustice.com/rule/4-7-22/)

The same mirror sets out the rest of the conditions: use of the provider's actual legal or registered fictitious name (d)(9), no fee splitting (d)(2), an annual report to the Bar (d)(5), at least four participating lawyers from four different firms (d)(12), and a 15-day reporting obligation on the participating lawyer (e)(1) ([Rule 4-7.22](https://floridajustice.com/rule/4-7-22/)).

The Bar Handbook also confirms trade-name practice: a lawyer may use a trade name only if it "does not imply that the firm is something other than a private law firm," and "Some trade names may require a disclaimer such as 'a private law firm' so as not to be misleading. The disclaimer must be clear and conspicuous" ([Florida Bar Advertising Handbook 2025](https://www-media.floridabar.org/uploads/2025/12/Handbook-2025-Approved-by-SCA-12-10-25.pdf)).

**Direct answer to "does any state require a lead-gen site to say it is NOT a law firm?"** Florida is the closest thing to a yes, but note the shape of it: Florida does not prescribe the sentence "we are not a law firm." It prohibits the opposite impression — a qualifying provider must not communicate in any way that could lead prospects to conclude it is a law firm. In practice the way operators satisfy it is an affirmative "not a law firm" statement (see the live examples in §7), but the rule as written is a prohibition on the false impression, not a mandated wording. I did not find a state that mandates exact "not a law firm" wording by rule text.

### 2.4 VIRGINIA (Virginia Beach car accident site)

Virginia Rule 7.3(d) permits paying "the reasonable cost of advertisements, including online group advertising" but limits referral payments to "a not-for-profit qualified lawyer referral service." Comment [4] tracks the ABA lead-generator conditions, and Comment [6] confirms only not-for-profit referral services qualify ([Virginia State Bar, Rules of Professional Conduct (complete PDF)](https://vsb.org/common/Uploaded%20files/docs/rules-rpc-complete.pdf)). So a for-profit distributed-call service paid per call by Virginia lawyers is outside the payment exceptions unless it is characterized purely as group advertising — which, per the Texas/California/Georgia authorities above, requires identifying the participating firms.

### 2.5 GEORGIA (expansion state)

Georgia Rule 7.3(c): "a lawyer shall not compensate or give anything of value to a person or organization to recommend or secure the lawyer's employment by a client, or as a reward for having made a recommendation resulting in the lawyer's employment by a client; except that the lawyer may pay for public communications permitted by Rule 7.1," plus the listed referral-service exceptions ([State Bar of Georgia, Lawyer Referral Services](http://www.gabar.org/general-counsel/ethics---discipline/lawyer-referral-services)). The Supreme Court of Georgia order text sets out 7.3(c)(1) (bona fide referral service authorized by law that files an annual report with the State Disciplinary Board) and 7.3(c)(2) (bar-operated nonprofit service, with E&O insurance and fee-neutrality conditions), and Rule 5.4(a)(5) permitting percentage referral fees only to a bar-operated nonprofit service ([Supreme Court of Georgia, June 9, 2004 amendment order](https://www.gasupreme.us/rules/amendments-to-rules/6_9_amend_2004_2/)).

Georgia also has an advisory opinion squarely on the "advertise, then route the call elsewhere" pattern:

> "It is ethically improper for a lawyer to advertise for legal business with the intention of referring a majority of that business out to other lawyers without disclosing that intent in the advertisement and without complying with the disciplinary standards of conduct applicable to lawyer referral services."
> "Assuming that the advertisements at issue offers only the advertising attorneys services and that the attorney accepts cases from the callers, the ad is not false or inherently misleading. However, where a majority of the responding callers are referred out, this becomes a lawyer referral service. The Rules require disclosure of the referral as well as compliance with the Rules applicable to referral services."
> — Georgia Advisory Opinion 05-6, as reproduced at [ezel.ai mirror of GA Ethics Op. 05-6](https://ezel.ai/ethics-opinions/gabar/05-6-advertising-hiding-a-referral-practice) (**secondary mirror — I could not fetch this from gabar.org; treat the wording as unverified against the primary source**)

**Conflict / numbering caveat:** the State Bar's own current rules index lists "Rule 7.2 Communications Concerning a Lawyer's Services: Specific Rules" and reserves 7.5 ([Georgia Rules of Professional Conduct index](https://www.gabar.org/general-counsel/georgia-rules-of-professional-conduct)), while the Bar's referral-services page still cites the payment prohibition as "Rule 7.3(c)" ([Lawyer Referral Services](http://www.gabar.org/general-counsel/ethics---discipline/lawyer-referral-services)). The Bar's rule handbook is a JavaScript application I could not read. So Georgia's **substance** is confirmed but its **current numbering is not**.

### 2.6 NORTH CAROLINA (expansion state) — the most workable regime found

NC Rule 7.2 carries the ABA lead-generator comment and the responsible-lawyer identification requirement ([N.C. Rule 7.2](https://www.ncbar.gov/for-lawyers/ethics-and-governing-rules/rules-of-professional-conduct/71-76-information-about-legal-services/72-communications-concerning-a-lawyers-services-specific-rules/)). Uniquely, NC Rule 7.4 addresses "intermediary organizations" and expressly includes a "lawyer matching service, online marketing platform" in the definition, then conditions participation on, among other things, disclosure of the payments and of the referral arrangement "to the client at the outset," fees limited to a proportional share of administrative and advertising costs, and mandatory withdrawal by the lawyer if non-compliance is not corrected ([N.C. Rule 7.4, Intermediary Organizations](https://www.ncbar.gov/for-lawyers/ethics-and-governing-rules/rules-of-professional-conduct/71-76-information-about-legal-services/74-intermediary-organizations/)). Note the proportional-cost limitation is hostile to per-call auction pricing.

### 2.7 ARIZONA (expansion state) — the definition that catches ring trees

Arizona ER 7.2(c) requires the name and contact information of at least one responsible lawyer, and Comment [6] defines the term in a way that maps precisely onto call distribution:

> "A lawyer referral service, on the other hand, is any organization in which a person or entity receives requests for lawyer services, and allocates such requests to a particular lawyer or lawyers or that holds itself out to the public as a lawyer referral service... Consequently, this ER only permits a lawyer to pay the usual charges of a not-for-profit or qualified lawyer referral service. A qualified lawyer referral service is one that is approved by an appropriate regulatory authority, such as the State Bar of Arizona, as affording adequate protections for the public."
> — [Arizona ER 7.2, Comment 6](https://tools.dev.azbar.org/RulesofProfessionalConduct/ViewRule.aspx?id=18)

Arizona ER 7.2 Comment [5] also mirrors the ABA lead-generator conditions ([same source](https://tools.dev.azbar.org/RulesofProfessionalConduct/ViewRule.aspx?id=18)). **Caveat: the only host that served me this text was `tools.dev.azbar.org`, a development-looking hostname; the production azbar.org URL returned a client error. Verify before relying on it.** Note also that Arizona has otherwise liberalized nonlawyer ownership via ABS licensing — a factor worth exploring with counsel that I did not research here.

## 3. Area 2 — FTC: deception, affiliation/identity, lead generation, reviews

### 3.1 Deception framework

The FTC's deception standard has three elements — a representation, omission or practice likely to mislead; a reasonable consumer; and materiality — and it explicitly covers implied claims and the overall impression:

> the Commission examines "the entire mosaic, rather than each tile separately," and considers the "net general impression" of the advertisement; a representation may be deceptive "if it is likely to mislead consumers acting reasonably under the circumstances"
> — [FTC Policy Statement on Deception (PDF)](https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/attachments/training-materials/policy_deception.pdf)

A first-person "we fix your garage door" page that never performs any work and instead auctions the call is an implied claim about identity, and identity is material to whom the consumer chooses to hire.

### 3.2 Affiliation and identity — the Impersonation Rule

> "§461.3 Prohibited impersonation of businesses. It is a violation of this part, and an unfair or deceptive act or practice to: (a) materially and falsely pose as, directly or by implication, a business or officer thereof... or (b) materially misrepresent, directly or by implication, affiliation with, including endorsement or sponsorship by, a business or officer thereof"
> — [FTC Government and Business Impersonation Rule, 16 CFR Part 461 (PDF)](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/r207000_govt_biz_impersonation_rule.pdf); part index at [Cornell LII, 16 CFR Part 461](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/part-461)

This bites hardest if any site borrows a real local company's name, brand or reviews, or implies affiliation with a manufacturer or franchise. Generic city+trade branding is not "posing as a business," but reusing another firm's identity or reviews is. The proposed means-and-instrumentalities provision (§461.4) was not finalized in the same rule text.

### 3.3 Lead generation specifically

FTC staff's lead-generation workshop report describes ping trees and auctions and then states the disclosure expectation directly:

> "consumers who fill out web forms may not realize they are operated by lead generators and instead assume that they are submitting information directly to a merchant"
> "consumers may not be aware that lead generators sometimes sell their information to the companies willing to pay for it (or pay the most for it), as opposed to those best suited"
> "Companies should disclose this type of information to consumers clearly and conspicuously"
> "Disclose clearly to consumers who you are and how you will share their information."
> — [FTC Staff Perspective, "Follow the Lead" (PDF)](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/reports/staff-perspective-follow-lead/staff_perspective_follow_the_lead_workshop.pdf)

That is essentially a plain-English specification of the disclosure your sites currently lack: **who you are** and **that the call/lead goes to whoever pays**.

### 3.4 Reviews and testimonials (relevant only because the sites display reviews)

> "(a) It is an unfair or deceptive act or practice and a violation of this part for a business to write, create, or sell a consumer review, consumer testimonial, or celebrity testimonial that materially misrepresents, expressly or by implication: (1) That the reviewer or testimonialist exists; (2) That the reviewer or testimonialist used or otherwise had experience with the product, service, or business that is the subject of the review or testimonial; or (3) The reviewer's or testimonialist's experience..."
> "(b) ... for a business to purchase a consumer review, or to disseminate or cause the dissemination of a consumer testimonial or celebrity testimonial, about the business or one of the products or services it sells, which the business knew or should have known materially misrepresented..."
> — [16 CFR §465.2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/465.2)

Critical implication for the distributed model: a review on a site that reads as "our customers" implies the reviewer had experience **with the business the page presents itself as being**. If the reviewer actually dealt with a former tenant, or with one of several rotating buyers, or was invented, that is within §465.2(a)(2)-(3)/(b). The endorsement guides add: "Endorsements must reflect the honest opinions, findings, beliefs, or experience of the endorser... an endorsement may not convey any express or implied representation that would be deceptive if made directly by the advertiser," and advertisers must guide, monitor and remediate endorsers ([16 CFR §255.1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/255.1)). Related FTC guidance: [Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule Q&A](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers) and [The FTC's Endorsement Guides: What People Are Asking](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking). (Per the brief, Negative Option and Made-in-USA rules are excluded as irrelevant.)

## 4. Area 3 — TCPA, one-to-one consent status, and call recording

### 4.1 Inbound consumer-dialed calls

The TCPA restrictions in the FCC's rules are framed around calls that a person or entity **initiates**: "(a) No person or entity may: (1)... initiate any telephone call...", and the do-not-call provision applies to a person who "initiate[s] any telephone solicitation" to a residential subscriber on the registry ([47 CFR §64.1200](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/64.1200)). A consumer dialing your tracking number is not a call you initiated, so the DNC and prior-express-written-consent machinery is not triggered by the inbound call itself. The consent apparatus becomes relevant the moment you (or a buyer) call or text that consumer back, or an IVR/dialer touches them afterward.

The FTC's Telemarketing Sales Rule gives an explicit, quotable exemption in the same shape:

> "Telephone calls initiated by a customer or donor in response to an advertisement through any medium, other than direct mail solicitation, provided, however, that this exemption does not apply to: (i) Calls initiated by a customer or donor in response to an advertisement relating to investment opportunities, debt relief services, technical support services, business opportunities... (ii) The requirements of §310.4(a)(9) or (10); or (iii) Any instances of upselling included in such telephone calls;"
> — [16 CFR §310.6(b)(5)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/310.6)

Note the upsell carve-out: if the answering party pivots the inbound call into selling something else, the exemption stops covering that portion.

### 4.2 One-to-one consent rule — current status

- The Eleventh Circuit vacated the FCC's one-to-one consent requirement: "we grant IMC's petition for review, vacate Part III.D of the 2023 Order, and remand for further proceedings," holding the FCC exceeded its statutory authority ([*Insurance Marketing Coalition Ltd. v. FCC*, 11th Cir., Jan. 24, 2025 (PDF)](https://media.ca11.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/files/202410277.pdf)).
- Immediately before that, the FCC had postponed the rule: "the effective date for the revised Sections 64.1200(f)(9) of the FCC's rules is postponed pending judicial review" ([FCC, order postponing effective date of one-to-one consent rule](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-postpones-effective-date-one-one-consent-rule)).
- The current codified text of the prior-express-written-consent definition at [47 CFR §64.1200](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/64.1200) does not contain a one-to-one / single-seller requirement.
- Secondary reporting states the FCC finalized its consent rules with the one-to-one requirement removed and said in April 2025 it would not challenge the ruling ([Consumer Financial Services Law Monitor, Sept. 2025](https://www.consumerfinancialserviceslawmonitor.com/2025/09/fccs-final-rule-on-consent-kills-one-to-one-consent-requirement/) — link as returned by search; treat as secondary).

**Status as best I can confirm for 2026: the one-to-one consent requirement is NOT in effect.** It was vacated in January 2025, its effective date had already been postponed, and the codified rule text does not contain it. I was not able to fetch a 2026-dated primary FCC statement confirming there has been no reinstatement (fcc.gov document pages returned robots errors) — see "What I could not confirm." Practical read: buyer-side consent discipline for callbacks/texts still matters under ordinary TCPA rules and state mini-TCPAs, but the specific "one consent per identified seller" mandate is not a live constraint on ring-tree distribution.

## 6. Area 5 — Home services: license numbers in advertising, and who the duty falls on

**The recurring answer to "does the obligation fall on a marketing site that is not the contractor?"** The license-number-in-advertising duties are written to bind *licensees* ("Every person licensed pursuant to this chapter shall include his license number in... all forms of advertising"). So a non-contractor marketing site does not violate the number-in-ads rules. But the *other* statute in each state — the prohibition on **advertising to perform the work without a license** — is written to bind "any person," and that is the one a first-person marketing site trips. In short: you do not inherit the licensee's disclosure duty; you inherit the unlicensed-advertising prohibition.

### 6.1 California (San Jose plumbing, Sacramento AC) — misdemeanor exposure

> "(a) It is a misdemeanor for any person to advertise for construction or work of improvement covered by this chapter unless that person holds a valid license under this chapter in the classification so advertised, except that a licensed building or engineering contractor may advertise as a general contractor."
> "(b) 'Advertise,' as used in this section, includes, but not by way of limitation, the issuance of any card, sign, or device to any person, the causing, permitting, or allowing of any sign or marking on or in any building or structure, or in any newspaper, magazine, or by airwave or any electronic transmission, or in any directory under a listing for construction or work of improvement covered by this chapter, with or without any limiting qualifications."
> "(c) A violation of this section is punishable by a fine of not less than seven hundred dollars ($700) and not more than one thousand dollars ($1,000)..."
> — [Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §7027.1](https://california.public.law/codes/ca_bus_and_prof_code_section_7027.1)

Note "with or without any limiting qualifications" — on its face, that phrase weakens the argument that a footer disclaimer cures an advertisement that otherwise advertises for covered work. And the licensee-side rule: "Every person licensed pursuant to this chapter shall include his license number in: (a) all construction contracts; (b) subcontracts and calls for bid; and (c) all forms of advertising, as prescribed by the registrar of contractors, used by such a person." ([Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §7030.5](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-bpc/division-3/chapter-9/article-2/section-7030-5/))

California is therefore the state where the home-services sites need the most rewriting: they should read as an information/connection service, not as an offer to perform plumbing or HVAC work.

### 6.2 Texas (Fort Worth garage door; plus TX HVAC/plumbing if you expand)

HVAC advertising: "All advertising by air conditioning and refrigeration contracting companies designed to solicit air conditioning or refrigeration business must include the affiliated licensee's license number," with narrow exceptions (national TV, bare phone-book listings, telephone solicitations where the solicitor states compliance, nominal promo items, letterhead, signs at the permanent location) ([16 Tex. Admin. Code §75.71(h)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/16-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-75-71)). Proposals and invoices must also carry the licensee number and the TDLR regulatory notice (§75.71(i), same source).

Plumbing: "A person or company shall not advertise, contract for or otherwise offer or agree to perform plumbing work for or provide plumbing services to the public unless the person or company has secured the services of at least one RMP holding a current Master Plumber License." ([22 Tex. Admin. Code §367.9(a)](https://regulations.justia.com/states/texas/title-22/part-17/chapter-367/section-367-9/)) — note this binds "a person or company," not just licensees, and covers advertising. A plumbing site that offers to perform plumbing services without an RMP behind it is within this text.

Garage door: I found no Texas state license requirement for garage door repair (see gaps). That is why the Fort Worth garage door site is your lowest-risk home-services asset.

### 6.3 Florida

> "(5)(b) The registration or certification number of each contractor shall appear in each offer of services, business proposal, bid, contract, or advertisement, regardless of medium, as defined by board rule, used by that contractor or business organization in the practice of contracting."
> "(5)(e) The board shall issue a notice of noncompliance for the first offense, and may assess a fine or issue a citation for failure to correct the offense within 30 days or for any subsequent offense, to any contractor or business organization that fails to include the certification or registration number..."
> "(5)(f) ... a local government may impose a civil fine pursuant to s. 489.127(5) against a person who is not certified or registered under this part if the person: 1. Claims to be licensed in any offer of services, business proposal, bid, contract, or advertisement, but does not possess a valid competency-based license...; or 2. Claims to be insured in any offer of services, business proposal, bid, contract, or advertisement, but whose performance of the subject work is not covered by a general liability or workers' compensation insurance policy."
> — [Fla. Stat. §489.119](http://www.leg.state.fl.us/Statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0400-0499/0489/Sections/0489.119.html)

Florida's trap for lead-gen copy is (5)(f): claiming to be "licensed and insured" on a site that is neither is separately fineable against a non-licensee.

### 6.4 Arizona

> "(B) The license number appearing on any licenses held by the licensee must be preceded by the acronym 'ROC' and shall be posted or placed as follows: ... (3) On all published advertising, letterheads and other documents used by the licensee to correspond with the licensee's customers or potential customers...; (4) On all broadcast, internet or billboard advertising, unless the broadcast, internet or billboard advertising includes a website's uniform resource locator that directly links to a website that prominently displays the licensee's name and license number."
> — [A.R.S. §32-1124](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/32/01124.htm)

Interesting nuance for a marketing site: (B)(4) contemplates satisfying the requirement by linking to a page that prominently displays the licensee's name and number — a structure a lead-gen site could support if it names the actual provider.

### 6.5 Nevada — the strictest wording requirement found in this entire research

> "1. It is unlawful for any person, including a person exempt under the provisions of NRS 624.031, to advertise as a contractor unless the person has a license in the appropriate classification..."
> "2. Notwithstanding any other provision of this chapter, any person not licensed pursuant to the provisions of this chapter who advertises to perform or complete construction work or a work of improvement must state in the advertisement that he or she is not licensed pursuant to this chapter."
> "4. All advertising by a licensed contractor must include the name of the contractor's company and the number of the contractor's license."
> "6. ... the Board may... issue an order to the person to cease and desist the unlawful advertising and to cause any telephone number included in the advertising to be disconnected."
> — [NRS 624.720](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/NRS/NRS-624.html#NRS624Sec720)

Fines run from $1,000 to $50,000 administratively ([NRS 624.710(1)](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/NRS/NRS-624.html#NRS624Sec720)) with escalating criminal penalties ([NRS 624.750(2)](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/NRS/NRS-624.html#NRS624Sec720)). The phone-disconnection remedy is uniquely dangerous to a tracking-number business model. Nevada is the state that drives the affirmative "we are not a licensed contractor" sentence in the draft language below.

## 9. Ready-to-use draft disclaimer wording

These are drafts for a lawyer to review, not vetted language. They are written to the **strictest requirement found in each vertical**, and each clause is annotated with the state that drove it. Placement matters as much as wording: per the network publisher standard, a disclosure "must be unavoidable... visible and unavoidable to the consumer 'above the fold'" and "cannot be used to 'explain away' information or contradict the claim itself" ([Advertising and Marketing Requirements for Publishers](http://network.affmine.com/notice/RZ-Advertising-Marketing-Requirements.pdf)). So the copy must change too — a disclaimer under contradictory "we fix it" copy is the weakest possible position.

### (a) LEGAL-VERTICAL SITES

**Threshold warning before any wording is used:** in **Texas** and **California** the problem is not disclosure, it is authorization. Texas requires a Ch. 952 certificate available only to governmental entities and 501(c)(3)/(4)/(6) nonprofits with 25+ lawyers from different firms ([Occ. Code Ch. 952](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/OC/pdf/OC.952.pdf)); California requires State Bar certification and forbids attorneys from accepting non-certified referrals ([B&P §6155(a)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=6155)). No disclaimer cures an uncertified for-profit referral operation in those states. The only structurally defensible version of a distributed legal site I found in the source material is **named joint advertising** — publishing the participating firms so the consumer can "select and initiate contact with" them ([§6155(h)(1)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=6155); [Occ. Code §952.003(4)](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/OC/pdf/OC.952.pdf)) — which is incompatible with a blind bid auction among rotating buyers.

Draft, for a named-participants joint-advertising structure (placed above the fold, adjacent to the phone number, plus repeated in full on a linked "Legal Notices" page):

> **ATTORNEY ADVERTISING — PAID ADVERTISEMENT. THIS WEBSITE IS NOT A LAW FIRM AND DOES NOT PROVIDE LEGAL SERVICES OR LEGAL ADVICE.**
>
> [SITE NAME] is a paid advertising service operated by [LEGAL ENTITY NAME], [CITY, STATE], which is not a law firm and is not a lawyer referral service. Calling the number on this site connects you with a participating law firm that pays a fee to advertise here. Participating firms are listed at [LINK]. The firm responsible for the content of this advertisement is [FIRM NAME], [STREET ADDRESS, CITY, STATE], [PHONE]. *(Driven by: FL 4-7.22(d)(11) — must not create the impression of being a law firm; TX 7.02(a) and CA CRPC 7.2(c) and AZ ER 7.2(c) — named responsible lawyer/firm with location; FTC "Follow the Lead" — disclose who you are.)*
>
> You may be connected with, or your inquiry may be forwarded to, a lawyer at a law firm other than the one named above. Inclusion of any firm is based solely on that firm's payment of an advertising fee. [SITE NAME] does not recommend, endorse, evaluate or rank any lawyer or law firm, does not evaluate your legal problem, and does not select a lawyer based on the facts of your matter. *(Driven by: TX 7.01 cmt 4 — must disclose likely referral to another firm; GA Adv. Op. 05-6 — must disclose intent to refer out; ABA MR 7.2 cmt 5 / AZ ER 7.2 cmt 5 — no implied recommendation, no implied case analysis, no concealment of payment.)*
>
> The office location of any firm you are connected with is [CITY/TOWN/COUNTY] — disclosed to you at the time of connection. *(Driven by: FL 4-7.22(d)(10).)*
>
> Contacting us or a participating firm does not create an attorney-client relationship. No representation is made that the quality of legal services to be performed is greater than the quality of legal services performed by other lawyers. Prior results do not guarantee a similar outcome. The choice of a lawyer is an important decision and should not be based solely upon advertisements. *(Driven by: state-specific advertising disclaimers as used by [The Injury Solution's state disclosures](https://theinjurysolution.com/disclaimers-by-state); confirm the required wording state-by-state before use — I did not verify the AL/MO/NY/WY rule texts myself.)*
>
> Calls to and from this number may be recorded or monitored. *(Driven by: CA Penal §632/632.7, FL §934.03(2)(d) all-party consent — must also be announced audibly at the start of the call and the announcement itself recorded, per the [RCW 9.73.030(3)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=9.73.030) model.)*

Additional non-wording steps for legal sites: file the advertisement with the Texas Advertising Review Committee ([TX 7.04](https://legalethicstexas.com/resources/rules/texas-disciplinary-rules-of-professional-conduct/filing-requirements-for-advertisements-and-solicitation-communications/)); register as a Florida qualifying provider and confirm ≥4 firms from 4 different firms ([Rule 4-7.22](https://floridajustice.com/rule/4-7-22/)); confirm no percentage-of-fee or fee-split component anywhere in the payment structure ([FL 4-7.22(d)(2)](https://floridajustice.com/rule/4-7-22/); [ABA MR 5.4](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_5_4_professional_independence_of_a_lawyer/)).

### (b) HOME-SERVICES SITES

Draft (placed above the fold, adjacent to the phone number, plus in full on a linked "Disclaimer" page):

> **THIS SITE IS AN ADVERTISING AND CALL-CONNECTION SERVICE. WE ARE NOT A CONTRACTOR AND WE DO NOT PERFORM ANY WORK.**
>
> [SITE NAME] is operated by [LEGAL ENTITY NAME], [CITY, STATE], a marketing company. **We are not a licensed contractor and we are not licensed under [state contractor licensing law].** We do not perform garage door, plumbing, HVAC or any other repair or construction work. *(Driven by: NRS 624.720(2) — an unlicensed advertiser "must state in the advertisement that he or she is not licensed"; CA B&P §7027.1 — misdemeanor to advertise for covered construction work without a license, "with or without any limiting qualifications," which is why the copy on this site must not offer to perform the work at all.)*
>
> When you call the number on this site, your call is routed to an independent local service company that pays a fee for the call. We may be paid for connecting you. The company that answers may change from call to call, and different companies may receive calls from this site. *(Driven by: FTC "Follow the Lead" — "Disclose clearly to consumers who you are and how you will share their information," and consumers "may not be aware that lead generators sometimes sell their information to the companies willing to pay for it (or pay the most for it)"; FTC Deception Statement — net impression; 16 CFR §461.3 — no implied misrepresentation of affiliation.)*
>
> We do not employ, supervise, endorse, recommend, guarantee, or verify the companies that receive these calls. Any agreement, price, warranty or dispute is solely between you and that company. Ask the company that answers for its name, its license number, and proof of insurance before authorizing any work. *(Driven by: FL §489.119(5)(f) — non-licensees may be fined for claiming to be licensed or insured; Google LSA "Do not use the license of a person who does not work for your business.")*
>
> We are not affiliated with, and do not represent, any manufacturer, brand, franchise or other company named on this site. Any trademarks are the property of their owners and are used only to describe services that independent companies may provide. *(Driven by: 16 CFR §461.3(b); network publisher requirements — "Do not state or imply any representation or affiliation with any third-party businesses"; "Creative may not use actual third-party product logos.")*
>
> Reviews shown on this site describe experiences with [SPECIFIC NAMED COMPANY / the individual companies identified beside each review] and do not describe [SITE NAME], which performs no work. *(Driven by: 16 CFR §465.2(a)(2)-(3) and (b) — reviews must not misrepresent the reviewer's experience with the business that is the subject of the review; if you cannot attribute each review accurately, remove the reviews.)*
>
> Where a licensed provider is named on this site, its license number is: [NAME, LICENSE #]. *(Driven by: A.R.S. §32-1124(B)(4) — internet advertising may satisfy the ROC-number requirement by linking to a page prominently displaying the licensee's name and number; 16 TAC §75.71(h) — TX HVAC advertising must carry the affiliated licensee's number; Fla. Stat. §489.119(5)(b); NRS 624.720(4).)*
>
> Calls may be recorded or monitored. *(Driven by: CA Penal §632/632.7 and FL §934.03(2)(d); announce audibly at call start per the [RCW 9.73.030(3)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=9.73.030) model.)*

**Copy rewrite requirement (both verticals):** replace every first-person service claim ("we fix your garage door," "our technicians," "we represent you") with connection language ("connect with a local [trade] company," "get a quote from an independent local provider"). This is the single highest-value change, because it removes the underlying false claim rather than trying to explain it away — and both the FTC's net-impression framework and the network publisher standard say a disclosure cannot contradict the claim it sits under ([FTC Deception Statement](https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/attachments/training-materials/policy_deception.pdf); [publisher requirements](http://network.affmine.com/notice/RZ-Advertising-Marketing-Requirements.pdf)).

## 11. Risk ranking — ordered by likelihood of actually causing a problem

**1. California legal sites: uncertified referral activity (highest).** §6155(a) is a statutory prohibition that binds both operator and attorney, §6155(h)(2) defines the violation as connecting a consumer to "an attorney or law firm not identified in the advertising" — which is literally what a ring tree does — and §6155(e) makes it enjoinable by any person. There is no disclosure cure and the participating attorneys have their own exposure, which means your buyers' bar counsel will flag it. ([§6155](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=6155))

**2. Texas legal site: uncertified lawyer referral service + undisclosed referral.** Chapter 952 defines a referral service to include anyone who refers potential clients "regardless of whether the person uses the term 'referral service,'" bars operation without a certificate, and provides for injunctive relief; simultaneously Rule 7.01 cmt 4 makes non-disclosure of likely out-of-firm referral misleading and Rule 7.03(e) bars paying non-lawyers for referrals. Two independent hooks, one statutory and one disciplinary, plus a filing requirement you may currently be missing. ([Ch. 952](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/OC/pdf/OC.952.pdf); [TX 7.01](https://legalethicstexas.com/resources/rules/texas-disciplinary-rules-of-professional-conduct/communications-concerning-a-lawyers-services/))

**3. California home-services sites advertising as the provider.** §7027.1 is a **misdemeanor** aimed at "any person" who advertises for covered work without a license, and the phrase "with or without any limiting qualifications" undercuts a disclaimer defense. Two of your three home-services markets (San Jose plumbing, Sacramento AC) are California, and both trades are squarely licensed work. This is the highest-likelihood *home services* risk and the cheapest to fix, by rewriting copy. ([§7027.1](https://california.public.law/codes/ca_bus_and_prof_code_section_7027.1))

**4. Florida legal site: "looks like a law firm."** Rule 4-7.22(d)(11) is the one rule in this research that your current copy violates by its own terms, and Florida's Bar actively administers a qualifying-provider registration regime with annual reporting — meaning there is a live administrative process, not just theoretical enforcement. A competitor in this exact vertical publicly concedes that a common number plus referral to participating firms is defined as a referral service in Florida. ([Florida Bar Handbook 2025](https://www-media.floridabar.org/uploads/2025/12/Handbook-2025-Approved-by-SCA-12-10-25.pdf); [theinjurysolution.com](https://theinjurysolution.com/disclaimers-by-state))

**5. Participating lawyers' own discipline risk, which becomes your commercial risk.** Every legal-vertical rule reviewed binds the *lawyer*: ABA/AZ cmt 5 (may not pay a lead generator that implies recommendation or conceals payment), CA §6155(a) ("no attorney shall accept a referral"), VA 7.3(d)(2) (not-for-profit services only), NC 7.4(c) (lawyer must withdraw if the intermediary's non-compliance is uncorrected), FL 4-7.22(e)(1) (15-day reporting). Practical consequence: sophisticated firms decline to bid, and unsophisticated firms churn out after their bar counsel reviews it. This degrades the auction economics even where nobody enforces anything against you. ([ABA cmt 5](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_2_advertising/comment_on_rule_7_2/); [NC 7.4](https://www.ncbar.gov/for-lawyers/ethics-and-governing-rules/rules-of-professional-conduct/71-76-information-about-legal-services/74-intermediary-organizations/))

**6. Call-recording exposure in California and Florida.** Recording without an announcement in an all-party state is a defined statutory violation with an easy fact pattern (the recording itself is the evidence), and California §632/§632.7 claims are a well-known plaintiff's-bar target. Mitigation is trivial — an announcement — which is why this ranks below the structural issues but above everything platform-related. ([§632](https://california.public.law/codes/ca_penal_code_section_632); [§934.03](http://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0900-0999/0934/Sections/0934.03.html))

**7. Reviews that no longer correspond to any real provider.** Once calls rotate, any review presented as "our work" misrepresents the reviewer's experience with the business that is the subject of the review — §465.2(a)(2)-(3) and (b) — and this rule is new enough that the FTC has an active interest in it. The trigger is usually a competitor complaint, not a sweep. Cheap to fix (attribute or remove). ([16 CFR §465.2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/465.2))

**8. Google Business Profile suspension / doorway-abuse demotion.** High probability of *happening*, low probability of being catastrophic per site, and entirely within Google's discretion rather than a legal process: lead generation agents are ineligible for profiles, and Google reserves the right to suspend ([GBP guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177)). Doorway-abuse risk rises with template similarity across 83 domains funneling to one call endpoint ([spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies)). Rank this by revenue impact rather than legal impact.

**9. Texas HVAC / plumbing advertising rules and Florida/Arizona/Nevada number-in-ads rules (expansion risk).** Real requirements with real fines, but they mostly bind licensees, and your current live footprint in these trades is California and Texas. They become first-order the moment you add Nevada — where the Board can order the advertised phone number disconnected, which is existential for a tracking-number business. ([NRS 624.720(6)](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/NRS/NRS-624.html#NRS624Sec720))

**10. FTC enforcement directly against you (lowest likelihood, highest severity).** The deception framework and the Impersonation Rule clearly cover this conduct, and FTC staff has expressly told lead generators to disclose who they are and how leads are shared. But the FTC brings comparatively few actions and prioritizes large-scale consumer injury; an 83-site local-services network is far more likely to be reached first by a state bar, a state contractor board, a state UDAP claim, or a competitor complaint. Treat FTC principles as the *drafting standard* rather than the *likely enforcer*. ([FTC Deception Statement](https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/attachments/training-materials/policy_deception.pdf); [FTC "Follow the Lead"](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/reports/staff-perspective-follow-lead/staff_perspective_follow_the_lead_workshop.pdf))

**Not on this list because it is not a live constraint:** the FCC one-to-one consent rule, which was vacated in January 2025 and does not appear in the current codified rule text ([11th Cir. opinion](https://media.ca11.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/files/202410277.pdf); [47 CFR §64.1200](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/64.1200)). Inbound consumer-dialed calls are also not the TCPA/TSR problem; outbound callbacks, texts and upsells on those calls are ([16 CFR §310.6(b)(5)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/310.6)).

<p>The description barely varies. A crack loud enough to bring somebody out of bed, then a door that will not lift. Push the wall button and the motor groans against a slab of steel that suddenly weighs its true one hundred and forty to two hundred and fifty pounds instead of the almost nothing it normally feels like. Walk over and look at the shaft above the opening. On a torsion setup you will find a coil interrupted by a clean gap roughly two inches wide, the two halves relaxed and slightly unwound. On an older extension setup you will find a slack spring drooping alongside the horizontal rail, sometimes with its safety cable the only thing keeping it from whipping loose.</p>
<h2>Which system your garage has, and why it varies here</h2>
<p>Census estimates put the median Fort Worth housing unit at 1998, so torsion is the default assumption on most streets. About a third of the city was built before 1980 though, and that changes everything. Wedgwood, developed from the 1950s through the 1970s, and the 1920s bungalow blocks around Arlington Heights frequently have extension springs and shallow headroom above the header, occasionally on original one-piece doors. Newer areas out west, Walsh among them, are all sectional-and-torsion. <h2>Do not put a screwdriver in the winding cone</h2>
<p>Even snapped, a torsion spring holds serious stored energy, because the surviving half is still partially loaded. Winding bars are the only correct tool for this and no household object substitutes for them. Screwdrivers and rebar slip out of the cone under load and become projectiles. If a spring has gone: unplug the opener so nobody presses the button out of habit, leave the door flat on the slab, and keep vehicles and people out from under it.</p>
<h2>Two springs means two springs get replaced</h2>
<p>When a double-spring door loses one, the survivor has logged identical cycles under identical Texas conditions and is months from going the same way. Fitting the pair at once costs a little more in steel and saves an entire second service call. Anyone quoting a single spring on a two-spring door is either not looking or is banking on the return trip. Standard residential springs are rated near ten thousand cycles, roughly seven years at four openings a day, and high-cycle springs about double that for a modest premium on the door your family uses as its front entrance.</p>
<h2>Heat, dust and lubricant</h2>
<p>North Texas is hard on everything surrounding a spring. Summer heat in an unconditioned garage thins the lubricant on the shaft and bearings until it runs off or bakes into varnish, and dust blowing under the door sticks to whatever remains. Dry bearing plates and gummed drums force the spring to work harder than it was sized for, quietly eating its cycle life. Lithium or silicone garage door lubricant on bearings, hinges and the spring twice a year is the cheapest maintenance on the property.</p>
<h2>What the visit looks like</h2>
<p>The technician reads wire gauge, inside diameter and length off the failed spring, or the stamped numbers on the cone if they are still legible, and matches replacements to the door's actual weight. Both springs come off, drums get reset, new springs go on and are wound to the exact turn count for your door height. Cables get inspected while the tension is off, because a spring letting go often frays a cable on the way down. Then the door is balanced by hand: released from the opener, it should sit still at waist height instead of sinking or springing upward. Ask to watch that test. It is the proof the job was done properly.</p>
<h2>What it costs</h2>
<p>You get a firm figure before any tool comes out. Nobody can responsibly quote it without knowing door height and spring configuration, but you should absolutely have the total in hand before work starts.</p>

<h2>Down a foot, then back up</h2>
<p>That is the safety circuit doing exactly what federal rules require of it, correctly or otherwise. Two photo eyes sit within about six inches of the slab on either side of the opening and must see each other for the door to travel down. Late-day sun coming straight into a west-facing Fort Worth garage can blind one of them. So can a bike tire, a trash bin, a bag of mulch leaned against the bracket, or the fine grit that coats everything here by August. Brackets also drift over years of the door shaking the jamb. Check the indicator LED on each eye: on most brands both should burn steady, and a dark or flickering light means that eye has lost its partner.</p>
<p>Once the eyes are clean, square and lit, suspicion moves to the close-force setting in the motor head. As a door stiffens with age the opener needs more push, and when it hits its programmed ceiling it interprets the resistance as an obstruction and retreats. Cranking the force up is the wrong answer. It papers over a mechanical problem in the door and disables the protection that caused the reversal in the first place.</p>
<h2>Texas heat and opener electronics</h2>
<p>This is the failure pattern that separates a Fort Worth garage from one up north. A closed, uninsulated garage bakes through a run of hundred-degree afternoons, and the logic board screwed to the ceiling cooks along with it. Electrolytic capacitors dry out faster at sustained high temperature, and solder joints cycle and crack. The symptoms get weird rather than obvious: a door that works at seven in the morning and ignores every button by four in the afternoon, or a wall switch that responds while remotes do not. Travel limits forget themselves, and the unit reboots mid-cycle. Boards and capacitors are individually replaceable on most residential heads, and a heat-baked receiver board is a part swap, not grounds for replacing the whole opener. Sensor housings suffer the same way, which is why photo eyes in this climate go out of tolerance sooner than the manual suggests.</p>
<h2>Motor runs, door stays put</h2>
<p>Watch the rail while somebody presses the button. If chain or belt is traveling and the door is not, the trolley has parted from the carriage. Usually the emergency release cord was pulled, or snagged by a roof rack, and never re-engaged. Tug the cord toward the door and run the opener until it clicks back in. Two minutes, no tools, no charge.</p>
<p>If the motor hums and the chain sits dead still, the trouble is inside the housing. Chain-drive heads around fifteen years old typically give up their nylon drive gear, the teeth rounding off until they stop catching the worm shaft. Grinding with no travel is the signature. That is a repair, and worth insisting on before anyone quotes a replacement unit. A motor that buzzes briefly then quits without grinding points instead at the starting capacitor.</p>
<h2>When replacing it is genuinely correct</h2>
<p>Any opener old enough to lack photo eyes entirely should be replaced, not repaired, and so should a unit with a cracked housing or a discontinued board nobody stocks. A belt drive with battery backup is a real improvement over a chain unit hammering away under a bedroom, and backup power matters in a region where storm outages are routine. <h2>The answer you should get</h2>
<p>A named fault, a part price, and a candid read on whether spending it makes sense given how old the head is. <h2>Stop touching it</h2>
<p>Whatever cables and rollers remain engaged are the only things holding a derailed door up, and none of that geometry is stable once a section has left the rail. Hitting the button to find out how bad it is turns a partial derailment into a door lying across a hood. Kill power at the outlet, keep everyone clear of the opening, and resist the urge to lever it back on with a pry bar. This failure is legitimately urgent and gets pulled to the front of the day.</p>
<h2>Three things put a door off its rail</h2>
<p><strong>A cable unspooled from its drum.</strong> Each lift cable is anchored at the bottom bracket and winds onto a grooved drum sitting at the end of the torsion shaft. Let one jump those grooves, which commonly happens when a spring failure drops the door hard, and that half of the door loses its lift entirely and racks in the opening. The giveaway is one corner noticeably low with loose cable coiled near the jamb.</p>
<p><strong>A roller came out.</strong> Rollers are the wheels on the edge of each section. Nylon ones turn brittle and crack, and in this climate they do it faster, because a closed garage through a Fort Worth August is an oven. Steel ones seize once the bearings run dry and dust packs in. A seized roller skids instead of rolling and eventually climbs out, almost always at the radius where the vertical rail curves back to horizontal.</p>
<p><strong>The rail is bent.</strong> Reversing into it is the classic cause. Around here, so is weather. The National Weather Service office in Fort Worth logged supercells firing along the dryline over the middle of the Metroplex on 27 April 2026, with hail reported up to teacup size, and the following afternoon brought softball-size stones and an EF3 tornado near Mineral Wells just west of Tarrant County. Storms like that bend rails and bow struts across the whole region, and that damage matters far more than the dent people actually call about.</p>
<h2>Why the hail dent is not the problem</h2>
<p>This is the great misunderstanding of North Texas storm season. Homeowners ring about the pockmarks in the skin, which are cosmetic and can wait indefinitely, while the real damage is a rail pushed out of plumb and a strut bowed across the back of a section, quietly destroying rollers on every cycle. After hail or straight-line wind the useful questions are whether the rails are still true, whether the struts are still flat, and whether the top section meets the header evenly. An insurance claim for cosmetic panels can run on its own schedule; the mechanical repair should not wait for it.</p>
<h2>Cables and rollers wear out on a schedule</h2>
<p>Lift cables fray where they wrap the bottom bracket and take the most flex, and grit driven under the door accelerates it. A frayed cable is a planned replacement; a parted cable is an emergency. Rollers on a door used several times daily typically last ten to fifteen years, less if the nylon has spent every summer above a hundred degrees. Neither part is expensive, so replacing the full set in one sitting beats paying for three visits as they fail one by one.</p>
<h2>The repair itself</h2>
<p>The door gets clamped below the lowest roller so it cannot travel. Sections are brought back into line one at a time, rollers are re-seated or replaced, cables are respooled with matched tension on both drums, and bent rail is straightened or swapped for new section. Hinges get checked at the same time, since a section that came out of the rail usually took a hinge with it. Then the same hand-balance test as any other job: off the opener, the door should hold at waist height by itself. <h2>What it costs</h2>
<p>Rollers and cables are cheap. Rail sections are moderate. What actually drives the number is how far the door came out and whether any sections bent in the process. ### Reading a quote line by line

A quote worth signing names the part, the measurement and the labor separately. On springs, that means the wire diameter, the inside diameter and the length of each spring, plus the cycle rating, because a 10,000-cycle spring and a 25,000-cycle spring are different products at different prices. On an opener it means the drive type, the horsepower class, and whether the rail, safety eyes and wall control are new or reused. On a full door it means the model, gauge, insulation value, window layout, the color, the permit line, and who hauls the old sections away.

Two lines deserve extra attention. First, whether the trip or diagnostic charge is credited against the repair if the work proceeds. Second, whether the price covers one spring or the pair. Replacing a single broken spring on a two-spring shaft leaves a part of unknown remaining life carrying half the door, and the second failure usually arrives within a season.

### The oversell in this trade

The move that costs Fort Worth homeowners the most is a spring failure answered with a proposal for a whole new door. A snapped spring is loud, dramatic and completely normal at somewhere between 10,000 and 20,000 cycles, which most families reach in seven to twelve years of daily use. It is a hardware repair on a sound door. The other version is a storm visit that turns into a replacement pitch on the strength of a dented skin. After the April 2026 hail episode the National Weather Service documented across the middle of the Metroplex, plenty of doors picked up cosmetic bruising that changes nothing mechanically, while the damage that actually stops the door is a track pushed out of plumb or a bowed strut. One of those is an insurance conversation on the homeowner's timeline. The other is a repair that should happen before the rollers are destroyed.

### What the online average leaves out

A national cost page quotes the door and basic installation. It does not carry the Fort Worth permit line or a possible reinspection, rebuilt wood jambs on a garage where the framing has been wet since the last hailstorm, or a low-clearance kit for an alley garage. It also omits a new opener bracket where the old header will not take one, or a jackshaft opener when the ceiling is too shallow for a standard rail. Lead time is missing too. Insulated doors in a specific color and window pattern are ordered, not stocked, and in the weeks after a metro-wide hail event that queue is long enough to matter more than the price.

## svc_commercial_overhead_door_repair_lede

A dock door or a rolling steel curtain at a shop is a production asset, and it is not built or regulated like a house door. The parts are heavier, the cycle counts are higher, and downtime is what the repair is really about.

## svc_commercial_overhead_door_repair_body

<h2>The door types on a Fort Worth commercial building</h2>
<p>Sectional steel doors, similar in principle to a residential door but in heavier gauge with commercial-grade hardware and often glazed sections, on light industrial and service buildings. Rolling steel curtains, built from interlocking slats that coil onto a barrel above the opening, which is what most alley-side and dock openings on older buildings use because they need no ceiling space. Rolling sheet doors, a lighter single-sheet version of the same idea, common on self-storage and small warehouse bays. Grilles at retail frontages. Each has its own failure list, and a technician who only works on houses will not have the barrel, the slats, or the operator parts for them.</p>
<h2>Where the regulation differs</h2>
<p>This matters more than most building owners realize. The federal opener rule, 16 CFR part 1211, defines its scope around residential use: it covers operators for garage doors intended for residential buildings with one to four family living units, running on 600 volts or less. Commercial operators are engineered to a different set of expectations, which is why a commercial door can legitimately have a constant-contact control or an interlocked control station rather than a photo-eye pair, and why swapping a commercial operator for a cheaper residential head is not a shortcut anyone should take. Guarding on a commercial door is chosen for the traffic pattern and the door type, and it should be documented for the site rather than assumed. The <a href="../services/">service list</a> covers the residential side.</p>
<h2>The wear pattern is a cycle-count problem</h2>
<p>A house door runs a handful of times a day. A shop door can run dozens of times in a shift, and the parts consumed are the same parts, just faster: bearings, cables, the counterbalance, the bottom seal, the operator's contactor and limit switches. That changes the right strategy. On a residential door you replace parts when they fail. On a door running high volume, the cheaper approach is scheduled replacement before failure, because a bearing that seizes at ten in the morning stops trucks. High-cycle spring assemblies exist for exactly this and are specified by expected cycles rather than by price, which is worth asking about when a counterbalance is being replaced on a busy opening.</p>
<h2>What breaks on rolling steel specifically</h2>
<p>Slats bend when they are struck, and a bent slat can be cut out and replaced individually on most curtains, which is the single most useful thing to know about these doors. Guides get spread by forklift contact and the curtain starts riding out of them. The barrel's counterbalance loses tension and the door gets heavy at the bottom of travel or drifts. Bottom bars go out of level, which makes the curtain track crooked. And the operator, usually a jackshaft or a hoist-style unit with a chain fallback, loses its limit setting after a slip. Diagnosis on a curtain is different from a sectional door, and the honest first step is to establish whether the fault is the curtain, the guides, the barrel or the drive.</p>
<h2>Fort Worth conditions on a commercial opening</h2>
<p>Two local factors show up repeatedly. Heat: a metal building with a large south or west opening, in a city with a July average high of 95.6 degrees at DFW and around twenty days a year at or above 100, is hard on operator electronics and on grease in the barrel bearings. Storms: the city averages 46.5 thunderstorm days a year, and the National Weather Service documented softball hail and six tornadoes across the region during the April 24 to 29, 2026 outbreak, which is the kind of event that bends slats and pushes guides on exposed dock openings across an industrial park in one afternoon. Both argue for inspection intervals rather than reactive calls.</p>
<h2>Registration, permits and who is allowed to do the work</h2>
<p>Fort Worth requires a valid contractor registration to obtain building, mechanical, plumbing or electrical permits, and electrical work under the city's adopted codes requires a State of Texas electrical contractor license behind that registration. The homestead exception that lets an owner work on their own occupied home does not apply to a commercial building. So on a commercial opening, ask about registration and about who is doing any power or control wiring. The city enforces the 2021 International Building Code along with the 2023 National Electrical Code, effective March 1, 2023.</p>
<h2>How the call should be handled</h2>
<p>Response time is the product on a commercial door. A useful first conversation establishes what the door is, what stopped, whether the opening can be secured in the meantime, and whether a temporary measure keeps the business running while a part arrives. A written price for the repair comes before work, with parts identified, and where a curtain or a section needs to be ordered the interim plan should be part of the quote rather than an afterthought. Overnight and same-day failures are handled the way the <a href="../emergency-garage-door-repair/">emergency repair</a> page describes.</p>

## svc_emergency_garage_door_repair_lede

Some door failures cannot wait until morning: a car shut in behind a door that will not lift, a door stuck open with the house exposed, or a door hanging in the opening with nothing holding it. Those get worked the same day or the same night.

## svc_emergency_garage_door_repair_body

<h2>What counts as an emergency</h2>
<p>Four situations, and they have nothing in common except urgency. A broken counterbalance with a vehicle trapped inside, which is the most common night call. A door stuck fully or partly open, which leaves the house and everything in the garage accessible from the street. A door that has come off its rail and is being held by whatever cable and roller is still engaged, which is a load waiting to drop. And a door that has been struck, usually by a vehicle or by storm debris, and can no longer be trusted to stay where it is. Everything else on the <a href="../services/">service list</a> is a scheduled repair, and being told your problem is in that second category is better news than being charged a night rate for it.</p>
<h2>The first four things to do</h2>
<p>Cut power to the opener at the outlet, not just at the wall button, so nobody can send the door anywhere while it is unstable. Get people and vehicles out from under the opening and keep them out. Do not prop the door with a jack, a broom or a stack of lumber and then work under it. If the door is up and you need it down for security, understand what is holding it first: a door with an intact counterbalance can be lowered by hand after the release cord is pulled, and a door with a broken spring or a parted cable will come down fast and should be left alone. Then describe what you are seeing when you call, including whether anything is currently bearing weight, because that determines what gets loaded on the truck.</p>
<h2>Fort Worth calls the night brings</h2>
<p>Storm nights produce the volume. The city averages 46.5 thunderstorm days a year, and the National Weather Service recorded six tornadoes and softball-size hail across the region during the April 24 to 29, 2026 outbreak, so a single evening can leave doors bowed, rails out of plumb, and openers dead behind a power cut across whole subdivisions. That is also when people discover their opener has no battery backup, pull the release cord in the dark, and find the door too heavy because the counterbalance failed at the same time. Summer produces a different pattern: a door that worked all day quits at six in the evening in a garage that has been sitting above 100 degrees, and the fault is usually electronic rather than mechanical.</p>
<h2>Securing an opening tonight when a full repair cannot happen</h2>
<p>Occasionally the correct answer at eleven at night is to make the opening safe and finish properly in daylight, because a section has to be ordered or the door needs two people and full light to hang. In that case the door gets brought down and physically secured, clamped in the rail so it cannot be lifted, with the opener disconnected. That is a legitimate outcome and it should be stated plainly rather than turned into a rushed repair with the wrong parts. What should never happen is a door left balanced on a bent rail with the opener still plugged in, or a section forced back into place so it looks closed while nothing is actually holding it.</p>
<h2>The manual release, and what it does not do</h2>
<p>The red cord hanging from the trolley disconnects the door from the opener carriage. It does not make a heavy door light and it does not repair anything. Pull it with the door down whenever you can; pulling it with the door up on a failed counterbalance is how doors land on cars. Once you are through, the trolley re-engages by pulling the cord back toward the door and running the opener until it clicks in, which takes about two minutes and no tools. If the trolley will not catch, the carriage or the chain is the problem and that is a repair. Note also that a door disconnected from the opener has no powered lock, so it should be clamped or the garage should not be left empty.</p>
<h2>Pricing at night, said out loud</h2>
<p>Emergency work is priced before dispatch, including whatever after-hours element applies, and the repair total comes before the tools come out. Two things are worth insisting on. First, that the diagnosis is not deferred: a technician who cannot say what failed before quoting has not looked. Second, that an urgent repair is not turned into a replacement pitch while you are standing in the dark wanting the car out. A broken counterbalance at midnight is still a <a href="../garage-door-spring-repair/">spring repair</a>, and a storm-damaged door is still a <a href="../storm-and-wind-damage-repair/">storm and wind damage</a> job in the morning. Getting the door safe tonight and the cosmetic work scheduled later is usually the cheaper path and it is the one you should be offered.</p>

## svc_garage_door_cable_repair_lede

Lift cables are the two galvanized wire ropes that actually carry your door up and down. They wear at a predictable spot, they fail without any bang at all, and a parted cable is the reason a door ends up sitting crooked in the opening.

## svc_garage_door_cable_repair_body

<h2>What a cable does, and what it is not</h2>
<p>Each cable is anchored to the bottom bracket at the base of the door, runs up the inside of the jamb, and winds onto a grooved drum at the end of the shaft over the opening. The counterbalance stores the force; the cable is what delivers it to the door. That distinction matters when you are trying to tell what failed. A counterbalance letting go announces itself and leaves the door dead on the slab. A cable letting go is nearly silent, and the door keeps moving on the side that is still connected, which is what racks it in the opening. If you are hearing a bang and seeing a gap in a coil, you are on the wrong page and should read <a href="../garage-door-spring-repair/">spring repair</a> instead. The full <a href="../services/">job list</a> splits these on purpose, because the parts and the sequence are different.</p>
<h2>Where cables fail</h2>
<p>Almost always at the bottom loop. That is where the cable takes its tightest bend, where it flexes on every cycle, and where grit driven under the door collects and works into the strands. What you see first is a few broken filaments standing off the lay of the rope, usually within a foot of the bottom bracket. Sometimes you see rust staining on a cable that spent years next to a slab that wicks moisture. The other failure spot is the drum: if a cable has ever unspooled and gone slack, it can score its own groove or ride up over the flange, and after that it frays fast because it is being crushed rather than wound.</p>
<h2>Alley garages and safety cables</h2>
<p>Fort Worth housing stock makes this two different jobs. Post-1990 garages out toward Woodland Springs are sectional doors with a shaft, two drums and two lift cables, which is the arrangement most people picture. The detached alley structures behind Arlington Heights and Fairmount often run extension springs along the horizontal rails instead, and those have a second kind of cable entirely: an interior safety cable threaded through the spring so a snapped spring cannot travel. Older doors reach a technician with that safety cable missing more often than not, because it was never reinstalled after some earlier repair. Adding it is cheap and it is the difference between a broken spring staying put and a broken spring crossing the garage.</p>
<h2>Frayed is a schedule, parted is a stop</h2>
<p>A cable with visible broken strands is planned work. Put it on the calendar, keep using the door gently in the meantime, and expect both cables replaced together, since they have taken the same number of cycles in the same heat. A cable that has actually parted is different. The door is now hanging on one side, the opposite drum is unwinding out of time, and every button press makes the alignment worse. Cut power at the outlet and leave it where it stands. That call gets treated as urgent for the same reason a derailed door does, and the fault often travels together with <a href="../off-track-garage-door-repair/">off-track and cable damage</a> because a slack cable is how a section leaves the rail in the first place.</p>
<h2>How the replacement goes</h2>
<p>The door is clamped so it cannot move, tension comes off the shaft in a controlled way with winding bars, and the old cables come off both drums. New cables are sized to door height and looped over the bottom bracket studs, then wound on with the drums set so both sides carry the same length. Getting the drums timed to each other is the whole job; a quarter turn of difference shows up as one corner sitting low and the door pulling against a rail. The bottom brackets get inspected while everything is loose, because a bracket with an elongated stud hole will chew the new cable, and the bottom fixtures are the one place on the door where the fastener is under load whenever the door is up.</p>
<h2>What to expect on the visit</h2>
<p>You get a price before anything is taken apart. Cable stock is inexpensive, so what moves the number is what else has to come off: a scored drum, a bent bottom bracket, a bearing that has been running dry, or a section that got tweaked while the door was hanging on one cable. The technician should finish by pulling the release cord and lifting the door by hand. It should travel evenly and hold near waist height. If it drags on one side after new cables, the drums are not matched, and that is worth fixing on the visit rather than living with.</p>

## svc_garage_door_keypad_and_remote_repair_lede

A remote that quit and a keypad that no longer responds are radio problems, not door problems. The door hardware is fine. What has changed is the battery, the receiver, the stored code table, or the airwaves around your house.

## svc_garage_door_keypad_and_remote_repair_body

<h2>Establish which side of the link failed</h2>
<p>Press the hardwired wall control. If the door runs normally from the wall and ignores every remote and the keypad, the mechanical side of the system is healthy and the problem is radio. If the wall control does nothing either, this is not a remote fault and you should start from the <a href="../services/">service list</a>. That single test saves an hour. Next, work out whether one transmitter failed or all of them: a single dead remote is a battery or a contact, while every transmitter failing at once points at the receiver in the motor head or at the antenna wire hanging from it.</p>
<h2>Batteries, heat and the keypad membrane</h2>
<p>Fort Worth is hard on the outdoor keypad specifically. It is mounted on a jamb in direct sun, and the National Weather Service normal for July at DFW is a 95.6-degree average high, with about twenty days a year at or above 100 and 71 of them in the summer of 2011. That cycling breaks down the rubber keypad membrane, so keys stop making contact, and it shortens the life of the coin or nine-volt cell inside. The tell for a weak battery in either a remote or a keypad is range that shrinks before it disappears: the button works with the transmitter held near the head, and not from the driveway. The tell for a failed membrane is one or two specific digits that need to be pressed hard while the rest still work.</p>
<h2>Rolling codes and relearning</h2>
<p>Anything made in roughly the last thirty years pairs by rolling code, which changes the transmitted value every press and stores a table in the head. That is why pairing means putting the receiver into a learn state and pressing the remote, rather than matching switches. Much older units used dip switches inside the transmitter and the receiver, and those have to be set identically. Both approaches are still in Fort Worth garages, because a chain-drive head from the early nineties can still be running in a Wedgwood carport built in the sixties. The important operational habit is unrelated to age: if a remote is lost or a car with a built-in transmitter is sold, clear the whole stored table and re-teach the household's own devices. Erasing everything is the point.</p>
<h2>The interference problem near the base</h2>
<p>There is a genuinely local cause worth knowing about. Some opener manufacturers chose to work between 380 and 399.9 MHz, a band reserved for government and military use, and the Army has published guidance explaining that as military land mobile radio systems moved into that range from 2004 onward, homeowners near installations began seeing reduced range or transmitters that stopped working, without any risk of doors moving on their own. Naval Air Station Joint Reserve Base Fort Worth sits inside the city, was established in 1994 as the country's first joint reserve base, and supports around ten thousand personnel. Unlicensed devices have no protected status: under 47 CFR 15.5 they must accept interference from authorized users. The practical fix is a receiver on a different frequency or a newer opener that does not use that band, and the diagnostic clue is a remote that works at the door and dies at the curb.</p>
<h2>Other things that jam a receiver</h2>
<p>LED and compact fluorescent lamps installed in or near the motor head are the most common self-inflicted cause, because the drivers radiate broadband noise directly at the antenna. Wireless equipment sharing the band, a nearby transmitter, or a metal shelving unit standing between the antenna and the driveway all narrow range. Before ordering parts, it is worth hanging the antenna wire straight down, swapping any lamp in the head for a rough-service incandescent or a fixture rated for garage door openers, and retesting from the same spot each time.</p>
<h2>What the repair looks like</h2>
<p>The order is battery, contacts, learn cycle, antenna, receiver. A dead remote gets a cell and a cleaned contact. A tired keypad gets replaced as a unit, because the membrane is not serviceable. A head whose receiver has failed can often take an external radio receiver wired to the terminals, which is cheaper than an opener and useful when the head itself is sound. Where the goal is phone control rather than a working handset, read the <a href="../smart-garage-door-opener-installation/">smart opener installation</a> page instead. You get the price before anything is replaced, and the wall control gets checked at the end so the household is not left with only one way to open the door.</p>

## svc_garage_door_opener_replacement_lede

Replacing an opener is a different decision from repairing one. It is worth doing when the head is old enough to predate the federal safety rule, when the board is discontinued, or when the noise under a bedroom has finally become the point.

## svc_garage_door_opener_replacement_body

<h2>When replacement is the right call</h2>
<p>Three cases are clear. An opener old enough to have no external sensors at all, which means it was built before part 1211 applied to residential operators made on or after January 1, 1993, should be replaced rather than patched. A head whose logic board is discontinued, where nobody stocks the part, is a replacement no matter how sound the motor is. And a unit that has had two failures inside two years is telling you something about the rest of its components. Outside those cases, most heads on Fort Worth houses are worth fixing, and the honest version of this conversation starts at <a href="../garage-door-opener-repair/">opener repair</a> and only arrives here if the repair does not make sense. The <a href="../services/">service list</a> covers the rest of the door.</p>
<h2>Choosing the drive</h2>
<p>Chain drive is the cheapest and the loudest, and it is entirely adequate on a detached alley garage where nothing above it is a bedroom. Belt drive costs more and is dramatically quieter, which matters on the very common Fort Worth layout where the garage shares a wall or a ceiling with living space. Wall-mounted jackshaft units drive the torsion shaft directly from beside the door and use no ceiling rail at all, and that is the answer in the older detached garages behind the near south side where headroom above the header is measured in inches. Rail length has to match door height, because a seven-foot door and an eight-foot door do not take the same rail, and an extension kit is a specified part rather than an improvisation.</p>
<h2>Power, and what the city requires of it</h2>
<p>Most residential heads plug into a standard outlet, so a straight replacement is usually not electrical work. It becomes electrical work when the garage has no outlet in the ceiling and one has to be added, or when an existing circuit is being extended. Fort Worth enforces the 2023 National Electrical Code, effective March 1, 2023, and the city's contractor registration rules require anyone pulling a permit under the electrical provisions of its codes to hold a valid registration backed by a State of Texas electrical contractor license. Homeowners working on their own occupied homestead are excepted from registration. Worth knowing before somebody offers to run a new circuit as a favor.</p>
<h2>Battery backup and the outage question</h2>
<p>North Texas loses power in storms, and an opener without a backup battery leaves a heavy door to be lifted by hand in the dark. Backup units carry a sealed battery in the head that runs a limited number of cycles after an outage. That is a genuine benefit here rather than a sales feature, and it is worth asking how many cycles the specific model claims and what a replacement battery costs in three or four years. The counter-argument is honest too: if your garage has a side door and you rarely need the car urgently, a chain drive without backup is a perfectly sensible purchase and the money is better spent on the door itself.</p>
<h2>Horsepower is not the interesting number</h2>
<p>Head units are sold by horsepower class, and the number is less important than what the door weighs and how well it is balanced. An opener is meant to overcome friction and start a balanced door moving, not to lift the weight; the counterbalance does the lifting. A half-horsepower unit on a properly balanced single door will outlast a three-quarter unit fighting a door with dry bearings and seized rollers. So a competent replacement includes a balance check, and if the door does not hold at waist height on its own the counterbalance is corrected before the new head is programmed. Anything else installs a stronger motor to hide a mechanical fault.</p>
<h2>What the installation should include</h2>
<p>The old head, rail and hardware come down. The new rail is hung off framing with proper angle iron, not off drywall or a strap somebody bent by hand. Travel limits and force settings are set with the door, then tested, and the force is never dialed up to mask drag. The warning label that comes with the unit goes near the wall control, which is where the rule tells the manufacturer to direct it. Old hardware is hauled away. You get an itemized price first, including whether the wall control, the sensors and the rail are new. If phone control is part of what you want, read the <a href="../smart-garage-door-opener-installation/">smart opener installation</a> page before choosing a model.</p>

## svc_garage_door_roller_replacement_lede

Rollers are the ten or eleven small wheels that let a door climb its rail. They are the cheapest moving part on the door and the one that Fort Worth summers destroy fastest, and worn ones quietly ruin more expensive hardware.

## svc_garage_door_roller_replacement_body

<h2>Which rollers you have</h2>
<p>Look at the wheel and the stem. A steel wheel with visible ball bearings around the hub and no cover is the builder-grade part, and it is what came on most tract housing garages. A white or off-white plastic wheel is nylon, either running on a plain bushing or on sealed bearings, and it is quieter because the tire itself is softer than the rail. Stems come in a couple of lengths, and the length has to match your hinge and track spacing rather than whatever the parts bin holds. On a standard double door with four sections you are replacing ten wheels, two per hinge line plus the top fixtures, so this is always a set job and never a single wheel.</p>
<h2>Heat is the local variable</h2>
<p>This is where Fort Worth differs from the way roller wear is usually described. National Weather Service normals for DFW show 20.2 days a year at or above 100 degrees, with 71 of them in 2011 and 55 in 2023, and a closed garage sits well above the outside air on those afternoons. Cheap nylon takes a set, then goes brittle and cracks at the tire edge. Unsealed steel bearings behave differently: the grease thins, runs, and picks up the fine dust that blows in under the bottom seal, and after a couple of summers the wheel is skidding along the rail instead of rolling. A skidding roller is what puts the flat wear scars you can feel on the inside face of the vertical rail.</p>
<h2>The symptoms that point at rollers and not something else</h2>
<p>A rumble that rises and falls with door speed, evenly, across the whole travel. Chatter that gets loudest right at the radius where the vertical rail curves to horizontal, because that is where side load is highest. A door that shudders on the way up rather than moving smoothly. Rollers do not cause a door to reverse at the floor and they do not cause a motor to run with nothing moving. If the complaint is noise, work it by sound before ordering parts; if the complaint is a door that stops, look at <a href="../garage-door-cable-repair/">cable damage</a> or the <a href="../services/">full service list</a> for something that fits better.</p>
<h2>What worn rollers cost you elsewhere</h2>
<p>The reason to do this before it becomes urgent is that everything downstream is more expensive. A seized wheel loads the hinge it is bolted to, and hinges crack at the knuckle. Side load bends the rail. The extra drag makes the opener work harder, which pushes it into its close-force ceiling and produces a reversal that looks like a sensor problem. And a roller that finally climbs out of the rail takes the whole door with it, which is a different and much longer visit. Replacing wheels is the smallest job on this list; the failures it prevents are not.</p>
<h2>Doing it properly</h2>
<p>Rollers come out one hinge at a time with the door clamped and the tension undisturbed, working from the top section down, so the door is never free to slide. On older doors the hinge itself often gets replaced with the wheel, because a hinge that has been running a seized roller has an oval hole and will not hold the new stem square. The bottom fixtures are the exception and are handled with the door down and the cable slack, since those brackets are under load. Rail gets wiped clean and the wheels get a light film of a lubricant meant for door hardware, on the stem and the bearing race, not on the tire and not on the inside face of the rail, where any oil just collects grit.</p>
<h2>Choosing what goes back on</h2>
<p>For a door that runs several times a day in this climate, nylon on sealed bearings is the part worth paying for. It is quieter, it does not need re-oiling, and the sealed race keeps dust out, which is the actual failure mode here rather than wear. Uninsulated detached garages off the alleys are the hardest duty on the list, since nothing moderates the temperature swing at all. You get the price before the work starts, and the check at the end is a hand lift with the opener disconnected: the door should coast, not stagger. If it staggers with new wheels, the rail is out of alignment and the <a href="../garage-door-track-replacement/">track work</a> needs doing too.</p>

## svc_garage_door_safety_inspection_lede

A garage door is the heaviest moving object in most houses, and the federal rules governing it exist because children were killed by closing doors. An inspection is a short list of specific tests, and you should be shown each one.

## svc_garage_door_safety_inspection_body

<h2>Why the rules exist</h2>
<p>In 1991 the Consumer Product Safety Commission announced a safety standard aimed at garage door deaths, citing 46 confirmed deaths of children between the ages of two and fourteen from March 1982 through December 1990 in incidents where an automatic door closed on them. The Commission published final rules in December 1992: openers manufactured on or after January 1, 1991 had to meet the requirements of the industry standard under the 1990 legislation, and additional requirements applied to units manufactured on or after January 1, 1993, including an electric eye, an edge sensor, or a constant-contact wall button as the secondary means of protection. That 1993 date is the line an inspection is looking for, and 16 CFR 1211.1 sets it out.</p>
<h2>The reversal test, done to the rule's numbers</h2>
<p>The federal rule is specific about behavior, so a real inspection tests against it rather than eyeballing it. Under 16 CFR 1211.7, an opener has to reverse a closing door within two seconds of contacting a solid obstruction and return it to the fully open position, and the rule exempts the first foot of travel from the fully open position from that requirement. The same section limits how long the motor may run: a unit must not be able to continue driving a door for more than thirty seconds under the conditions the rule describes. The test itself is a piece of solid material laid flat under the door, closing the door onto it, and watching whether it stops and returns fully open, not partly.</p>
<h2>The secondary device and its self-check</h2>
<p>16 CFR 1211.8 requires a secondary entrapment protection device on a vertically moving residential door: a photoelectric sensor, an edge sensor, an inherent sensor independent of the primary system, or an equivalent device. When it triggers, the closing door has to reverse, return fully open, stop there, and an open door must not be allowed to close. The requirement people are least aware of is the monitoring one: the operator has to verify that the device is present and working at least once during every close cycle. An inspection therefore proves the device twice, once by interrupting it mid-travel and once by disconnecting it, so the head is seen to refuse the close rather than ignoring the fault.</p>
<h2>The label, and where the rule puts it</h2>
<p>16 CFR 1211.17 requires an entrapment warning label to be installed near the wall control. The rule even specifies the size: at least five inches by six and a quarter inches, with the word warning in orange letters at least seven sixteenths of an inch tall. It is a small thing and it is missing in a large share of garages, either because it was never installed or because the drywall was painted around it. It goes back on, because it is the only instruction in the garage that tells the next occupant of the house not to let children play with the control.</p>
<h2>The mechanical half of the inspection</h2>
<p>Pull the release cord and lift the door by hand. A balanced door moves with moderate effort and holds where you leave it, roughly waist height; a door that slams down or flies up is out of balance and the counterbalance needs correcting before anything else. Then the parts: cables at the bottom loop where they fray, drums for scoring, bearings for play, hinge knuckles for cracks, rollers for flat spots, rail for plumb and for bracket lags that have loosened, struts for straightness, and the header bracket and rear hanger for fasteners walking out of framing. On any door in this city, that inspection also looks at heat: with a 95.6-degree July normal high and about twenty days a year over 100 at DFW, nylon rollers and dry bearings show their age early here.</p>
<h2>What you should do between inspections</h2>
<p>The Commission's guidance with those final rules told consumers to test the reversing mechanism monthly, and that remains the single most useful habit. Do the obstruction test and the beam test once a month, take thirty seconds, and note it somewhere. Keep the wall control high enough to be out of reach of children and never let anyone stand under a moving door. And if your opener has no external sensors at all, it predates the 1993 requirement and its replacement should be on your list rather than in the future; the <a href="../garage-door-opener-replacement/">opener replacement</a> page covers that decision, and the <a href="../services/">service list</a> covers what an inspection commonly turns up.</p>

## svc_garage_door_sensor_alignment_lede

The two small units bolted near the slab either side of the opening are what federal rule calls secondary entrapment protection. When the door bounces back up before it seals, they are the first thing to check, and alignment is usually all that is wrong.

## svc_garage_door_sensor_alignment_body

<h2>What the rule actually requires</h2>
<p>Federal rule 16 CFR 1211.8 says a secondary entrapment protection device on a vertically moving door must be one of a short list: an external photoelectric sensor, an external edge sensor mounted on the door edge, an inherent door sensor independent of the primary system, or an equivalent device. Whichever it is, when it triggers it has to reverse the closing door, take it back to the fully open position, stop it there, and refuse to let an open door close. The same section requires the operator to check that the device is present and working at least once during every close cycle. That last requirement is why a broken sensor wire does not simply disable the feature quietly: the head knows, and most units respond by flashing a code and refusing to close from the remote. Part 1211 applies to residential operators built on or after January 1, 1993.</p>
<h2>Why they drift out of line in this city</h2>
<p>The brackets are thin steel, mounted low, in the busiest four feet of the garage. They get caught by a car door, a trash bin, a bicycle handlebar, a bag of mulch. Over years the door itself shakes the jamb every cycle and the bracket walks. Then there is the local variable: fine grit blows under the bottom seal on windy days and settles on the lens as a film that looks like nothing and cuts the beam strength badly, and late-day sun straight into a west-facing opening can saturate a receiver until the door will not close between roughly six and sunset in high summer. If your door only misbehaves in the afternoon, that is a strong clue and not a coincidence.</p>
<h2>Reading the indicator lamps</h2>
<p>Nearly every residential pair has an indicator on each unit. The convention differs between brands, so use the label on the housing rather than assumption, but the pattern is consistent: one lamp shows the unit has power, the other shows the pair has each other. A dark lamp on one side is a wiring or power problem on that side. A lamp that flickers or dims as you move the bracket by hand is an alignment problem. Both lamps steady with the door still refusing to close moves the fault into the head, and that belongs on the <a href="../garage-door-opener-repair/">opener repair</a> page rather than here. The <a href="../services/">full job list</a> is worth a look if the door is not moving at all.</p>
<h2>The wiring is where the real faults hide</h2>
<p>Sensor wire is low-voltage bell wire, usually run up the jamb and stapled. Three failures repeat. A staple driven through the insulation makes a short that comes and goes with temperature, which is a classic Fort Worth summer fault as the jamb expands. A splice made with a twist and a wrap of tape corrodes at the copper and goes high-resistance. And a wire run across the floor or along the bottom of the jamb gets rolled over by a tire until the conductors part inside intact insulation. Finding these takes a meter and a look at the whole run, not a lens wipe, which is why a door that keeps coming back after being realigned needs the wiring traced end to end.</p>
<h2>What the alignment visit involves</h2>
<p>Brackets get set to a consistent measured height off the slab on both sides, squared to the door path, and tightened into sound material rather than into a hole that has already been stripped. Lenses get dry-wiped rather than cleaned with solvent, which hazes plastic. Where sun is the problem, a shield or a bracket relocation solves it properly, and it is worth doing rather than accepting a door that will not close on summer evenings. Damaged wire is replaced along its full length instead of spliced. Then the pair is proved twice: once by breaking the beam mid-travel with a broom handle to confirm the door reverses and returns fully open, and once with the beam clear to confirm the door seals and stays down.</p>
<h2>What not to do about it</h2>
<p>Two shortcuts cause the injuries the rule exists to prevent. Moving the sensors up the jamb to get above whatever is interrupting them defeats the geometry, because the whole point is a beam low enough that a small child crawling under the door breaks it. Taping or facing the units at each other on the same side of the opening is worse, and it is something technicians find in garages regularly. If the sensors are genuinely obstructed by stored items, move the items. If the pair keeps failing, replace the pair. Testing belongs on a schedule too, which is covered on the <a href="../garage-door-safety-inspection/">safety inspection</a> page along with the reversal and balance checks. You get a price before the work starts, and on a straightforward alignment there is no reason for it to include parts you did not need.</p>

## svc_garage_door_threshold_and_seal_replacement_lede

Seals are the cheapest work on a garage door and the most visible when they fail: daylight along the jamb, grit across the slab, water running in after a storm, and a garage that never cools down.

## svc_garage_door_threshold_and_seal_replacement_body

<h2>The four separate things people call a seal</h2>
<p>The bottom seal, sometimes called the astragal, is the flexible strip in the retainer along the bottom section, and on most steel doors it slides into an aluminum channel. The stop molding is the flexible flap nailed to the face of the jamb and the header that the closed door presses against on the sides and top. The threshold is a separate rubber or vinyl ramp bonded to the slab under the door, which is a different product from the bottom seal and solves a different problem. And on sectional doors there are joint seals between sections, which are part of the section rather than a replaceable trim. Diagnosis starts by asking which one is failing, because replacing the wrong one changes nothing.</p>
<h2>How each one fails here</h2>
<p>Bottom seals go first because the sun reaches them under the door and the slab reflects heat back up. In a climate with a 95.6-degree July normal high at DFW and roughly twenty days a year over 100, vinyl hardens, then splits at the folds and tears out of the retainer. Stop molding on a west or south-facing opening goes chalky and stiff, and eventually the nails pull as the wood behind it swells and dries. Thresholds fail at the adhesive line, usually because they were installed on a slab that was not clean or in weather too cool for the adhesive. The retainer itself corrodes at the fasteners and stops holding the bead of the new seal, which is why the retainer sometimes has to be replaced along with the seal.</p>
<h2>The threshold decision</h2>
<p>A bonded threshold is the right answer for one specific problem: a driveway that slopes toward the garage, or an apron that has settled so water sheets under the door. Fort Worth thunderstorm days average 46.5 a year and the rain comes hard and fast rather than steadily, so surface water at the door line is a real drainage problem in a lot of older neighborhoods. A threshold also blocks the fine grit and leaf litter that a gust drives under the door. What it does not do is fix a door that will not seal because the slab has heaved or the bottom section is bowed, and it slightly changes how the door meets the floor, so a very worn bottom seal should be replaced at the same time. Where the concern is wind-driven damage rather than water, read the <a href="../storm-and-wind-damage-repair/">storm and wind damage</a> page.</p>
<h2>Sealing is not the same as insulating</h2>
<p>A well-sealed uninsulated door still transmits heat through the steel. An insulated door with a torn bottom seal and gaps along the jamb loses a good part of what you paid for. The two jobs work together and the sealing half is a fraction of the cost, so it is the right thing to do first, and often the right thing to do instead when the door itself is sound. If the garage is attached and used as living or working space, the <a href="../insulated-garage-door-installation/">insulated door</a> page covers when the bigger investment is worth it. The <a href="../services/">service list</a> has the rest.</p>
<h2>What the work involves</h2>
<p>The door comes down and the old bottom seal is drawn out of the retainer, which is a two-person pull on a wide door. The retainer gets inspected and cleaned or replaced. New seal is cut to the door width with the right bead profile for the channel, because a bead that does not match the channel will pull out again. Stop molding is removed with the nails, the jamb is checked for rot at the bottom foot where water sits, and the new molding is set with a consistent reveal so it just touches the closed door rather than being crushed against it. Overtight molding drags on the door face and makes an opener strain. Thresholds go down on a slab that has been swept, degreased and allowed to dry, with the adhesive the manufacturer specifies.</p>
<h2>Pests, light and the test at the end</h2>
<p>Two side benefits worth naming. A tight seal ends the gap where rodents and insects get in, which is the reason a lot of these calls are made in the fall. And a proper stop molding line ends the daylight strip along the jamb that makes a garage feel like it is outdoors. The finished check is simple and should be done with you: close the door and look for light along the bottom, the sides and the top, then run the opener once to make sure the new seal has not added enough drag to trip the close force. You get the price up front, and on this job it should be short: seal, molding, threshold if you are having one, and labor.</p>

Track is the steel rail the door rides in, and it only has to be a quarter inch out of line to start destroying wheels and hinges. Bent rail is also the storm damage nobody calls about, because the dents are easier to see.

## svc_garage_door_track_replacement_body

<h2>The parts people lump together as track</h2>
<p>There is a vertical rail bolted to each jamb, a curved radius piece, a horizontal rail running back into the garage, and a rear hanger holding that horizontal run off the ceiling framing. The vertical rail is carried by a flag bracket at the top and jamb brackets down its length, and the whole assembly is only as straight as those brackets. A door that has been shoved out of true rarely needs every piece. What it usually needs is one rail section, a couple of brackets, and the alignment reset across the rest, which is why a measurement matters more than a parts list here. Start from the <a href="../services/">service index</a> if you are not sure the rail is what failed.</p>
<h2>How rail gets bent in Fort Worth</h2>
<p>Three ways, in order of frequency. A vehicle catches the vertical rail while backing out, which bends it inward at bumper height and leaves the door binding at the bottom of travel. A door comes down on an obstruction and racks itself, which spreads the rail near the radius. And weather does it: the National Weather Service Fort Worth office documented supercells over the middle of the Metroplex during the April 24 to 29, 2026 outbreak, with hail to teacup size on the 27th and softball reports the following day, and pressure and debris in storms like that push doors and rails out of plumb across whole neighborhoods at once. The city designs to a 105 mph three-second gust under the local amendments to the 2021 residential code, and the rail brackets and jamb fasteners are the hardware that has to hold when a gust loads the largest opening in the wall.</p>
<h2>Why small misalignment is expensive</h2>
<p>The rail sets the path, and the wheels have nowhere else to go. Pinch the rail and the roller stems bind, so the wheels stop turning and start dragging. Spread the rail and the wheels wander to the edge of the flange, then jump out at the radius. Twist the vertical rail and the door pulls on one hinge line, cracks the hinge knuckles and stretches the bracket holes. Every one of those shows up on the invoice as rollers and hinges, over and over, until the rail is corrected. If you have replaced <a href="../garage-door-roller-replacement/">rollers</a> once already and the noise came straight back, the rail is the reason.</p>
<h2>What alignment actually involves</h2>
<p>The door gets clamped and the tension stays where it is while the rail is worked. Vertical rails are shimmed and reset to plumb against the jamb, spacing from the door face is set consistently top to bottom, and the horizontal run gets checked for level and for the drop from front to back. Loose lag screws in the jamb brackets are the single most common finding, and in Fort Worth's older detached garages those lags are often in wood that has been wet since the last hailstorm, so the fastener has nothing to bite. That gets addressed as part of the job, not ignored, because a bracket that will not hold torque puts the rail right back where it was.</p>
<h2>When the section gets replaced instead</h2>
<p>Rail is repairable up to a point. A gentle bow comes back with hand tools and a straightedge. A crease, a kink at a bolt hole, a section that has been straightened once before, or rust that has eaten into the flange are all replacements, and rail is sold in sections so it is a modest part. Radius pieces are less forgiving than straight rail because the curve is the part that has to be exactly right. Rear hangers are replaced whenever they are bent or whenever somebody has hung the horizontal rail off a strapped joist with wire instead of angle iron, which is a common find in garages that have been through a few owners.</p>
<h2>Where the storm question comes in</h2>
<p>If the door was hit by hail or straight-line wind, the rail check comes before any conversation about the skin. Insurance work on dented panels runs on the adjuster's schedule; a rail out of plumb keeps damaging hardware every time the door cycles, and it is cheap to fix now and expensive to fix after it has taken a section with it. Ask for the rail and the struts to be inspected together after any storm, and read the <a href="../hail-damage-garage-door-repair/">hail damage</a> page for what an adjuster will want documented. You get a price for the alignment and for any section being replaced before the work starts.</p>

North Texas hail hits the garage door harder than almost anything else on the house, because the door is a thin steel skin with nothing behind it. What stops the door working, though, is rarely the dent.

## svc_hail_damage_garage_door_repair_body

<h2>What this region actually throws at a door</h2>
<p>The record for the Fort Worth forecast area is a 5.25-inch stone, measured during the April 11, 2016 storm that produced more than fifty severe hail and wind reports in one afternoon. <h2>Cosmetic damage versus mechanical damage</h2>
<p>A dent in the skin is a finish problem. A steel door section is a formed shell with insulation or air behind it, and a bruise in it does not change how the door travels. What changes how the door travels is the hardware the impact loaded: a strut bowed across the back of a section, a vertical rail pushed off plumb, a hinge with a stretched hole, and rollers that took a side load. Those keep working against the door on every cycle. So the useful order after a storm is mechanical first, cosmetic second, and the two do not have to happen in the same week. <h2>What an adjuster will want</h2>
<p>Document before anything is straightened. Photograph the whole door in flat light and again in raking light late in the day, which is what makes shallow bruising visible. Where a section is discontinued, that matters to the claim, because it decides whether the correct scope is one section or the door. <h2>Dent repair, honestly</h2>

## svc_insulated_garage_door_installation_lede

In Fort Worth an insulated door is bought for July, not January. The attached garage is where the opener, the paint, the tools and the second refrigerator all sit, and an uninsulated door is the largest heat path into that space.

## svc_insulated_garage_door_installation_body

<h2>What the heat actually does</h2>
<p>National Weather Service normals for DFW give July an average high of 95.6 degrees and a normal year 20.2 days at or above 100. The extremes are worse: 71 such days in 2011, 55 in 2023, and a stretch of 42 consecutive days in 1980. A steel door in afternoon sun radiates into a closed garage all evening, and that heat is what shortens the life of the opener's logic board and capacitors, embrittles nylon rollers, thins grease off bearings, and cooks anything stored along the wall. On an attached garage it also loads the wall and ceiling shared with conditioned space, which the air conditioning then has to fight. The energy argument for insulation here runs the opposite direction from the one used in cold climates, and it is a stronger argument, not a weaker one.</p>
<h2>How R-value on a door is actually derived</h2>
<p>Worth understanding before comparing numbers on brochures. The door and access systems manufacturers association publishes the calculation in its technical data sheet 163, and two points in it matter to a buyer. First, the figure describes the door section, not the whole door assembly, and the sheet says explicitly that it should not be used for an assembly, because joints between sections and stile attachments are not accounted for. Second, the calculation uses air films totaling 0.85, made up of 0.68 for still air inside a closed door and 0.17 for outside air moving at 15 mph, with the insulation contribution being its thickness divided by a k-factor from ASTM C177 or C518 testing. The sheet's worked example, a 22-inch section with polyurethane at two thicknesses, comes out at R-16.47. So a headline R-value is a section value calculated to a documented method, and a door with a high number and leaking joints will still let heat in.</p>
<h2>Polystyrene, polyurethane and the joints</h2>
<p>Two constructions dominate. Expanded polystyrene panels are cut and slid into a steel shell, usually with a thin back skin or a vinyl liner, and they are the affordable option. Polyurethane is foamed in place between the two steel skins, which bonds them into a stiffer sandwich section and fills the cavity completely, so it gets a higher value per inch of thickness and a considerably more rigid section. That rigidity is worth as much as the insulation in this market, because a stiff section resists both hail bruising and wind flex. The other half of real-world performance is sealing: the bottom astragal, the side and top stop molding, and the joint between sections. A high-R door with a worn bottom seal and daylight along the jamb performs like a cheaper one, which is why the <a href="../garage-door-threshold-and-seal-replacement/">threshold and seal</a> work belongs in the same conversation.</p>
<h2>Weight, springs and what installers get wrong</h2>
<p>An insulated replacement is heavier than the uninsulated door it replaces, sometimes substantially. The counterbalance is sized to the door's actual weight, so reusing the existing springs is the single most common shortcut on an insulated door installation, and it produces a door that will not hold position, an opener straining against the imbalance, and springs consumed early. Ask directly whether new springs are in the quote and what wire size and length they are. On a wide door, ask about struts as well, since a heavier section flexes and a strut is what keeps it flat. The <a href="../services/">service list</a> sets out what else gets checked while the door is off.</p>
<h2>Where it is worth it and where it is not</h2>
<p>An attached garage sharing a wall or a ceiling with living space earns the premium clearly, and so does a garage used as a workshop or a gym, which many Fort Worth houses now are. A detached alley garage behind a near-south-side house usually does not: nothing conditioned adjoins it, and the money is better spent on hardware and a proper seal. In between are attached garages with an interior wall that was never insulated, where insulating the door alone helps less than expected. Whoever quotes the job should be able to say which of those three your garage is. You get the full itemized figure, including the door, hardware, springs sized for the new weight, labor, the city permit for a door replacement, and disposal of the old sections, before anything is ordered.</p>

Sensor faults are covered on the <a href="../garage-door-sensor-alignment/">sensor alignment</a> page.</p>
<h2>The network problem in a Fort Worth garage</h2>
<p>Garages are the worst room in the house for wireless coverage and attached garages in newer subdivisions are often at the far corner from the router. The door itself is a large steel plane that reflects and blocks, and it moves, so a signal that tests fine with the door open can fail with it closed. Add heat: National Weather Service normals put July at DFW at a 95.6-degree average high, with around twenty days a year at or above 100, and a closed garage runs hotter than that. Consumer network gear and lithium cells in position sensors both age faster in those conditions. A sound installation means checking signal strength at the mounting location with the door in both positions, mounting the controller where air can move around it rather than tight against the ceiling deck, and choosing a position sensor with a replaceable battery.</p>
<h2>What you actually get from it</h2>
<p>Three things are worth the money. Notification and state, so you can tell from the office whether the door is open, which is the feature people use daily. Remote closing, which is the one the safety discussion above is about. And scheduled auto-close, useful in a city where the door gets left up after an evening in the yard. Camera integration and delivery access are separate purchases with separate subscriptions, and it is fair to ask what still works if the subscription lapses.</p>
<h2>The parts of the job people skip</h2>
<p>Wiring to the wall button terminals is low voltage and is not difficult, but it should be run and secured like the rest of the low-voltage wiring on the door rather than draped across the rail. The door position sensor needs to sit on the top section in a spot that does not foul the header seal. Firmware gets updated at commissioning rather than left at whatever shipped. And the household gets shown two things: how to work the door when the network is down, and where the manual release cord is. The wall control stays, always. A smart controller is an addition to the ways the door can be operated, not a replacement for the switch on the wall.</p>
<h2>Testing before it is signed off</h2>
<p>Same tests as any opener work, done in front of you. Break the beam with the door closing and confirm it reverses and returns fully open. Close from the app while standing in the garage and confirm the audible and visual warning behaves the way the manufacturer documents. Pull the release, lift by hand, confirm the door holds near waist height, then re-engage the trolley. Confirm the app reports the correct state after each of those. A price comes before the work, and it should separate the hardware, the labor, any sensor or wiring repair the door needed first, and any subscription that is not optional.</p>

## svc_storm_and_wind_damage_repair_lede

Wind does not dent a garage door. It loads it like a sail, and the failure shows up at the fasteners, the struts and the jamb rather than in the skin. That is a different repair from hail work.

## svc_storm_and_wind_damage_repair_body

<h2>What the door is designed to hold</h2>
<p>Fort Worth adopted the 2021 International Residential Code with local amendments in Ordinance 25383-03-2022, effective April 1, 2022, and its local climatic table sets the design wind speed at 105 mph as a three-second gust. A double garage door is typically the largest single opening in the wall it sits in, so it is also the largest surface for a gust to push on. The load does not stop at the door. It travels through the roller stems into the rail, through the jamb brackets into the framing, and through the rear hanger into the ceiling structure. When a door fails in wind, that whole chain is what gets inspected, not just the door.</p>
<h2>Fort Worth has real wind events, not just tornadoes</h2>
<p>The National Weather Service Fort Worth office documented the derecho of May 2, 2007, with an estimated 75 mph two miles south of Benbrook and 60 mph measured at Arlington Airport, and estimates of 90 to 100 mph further east in Kaufman County. The April 24 to 29, 2026 outbreak produced six tornadoes in the region, including an EF3 rated at 145 mph near Mineral Wells, immediately west of Tarrant County. A door does not need a tornado to fail. Straight-line gusts in the range those events produced across the Metroplex are enough to bow a door inward, pull lag screws out of a jamb, or push the top section past the header.</p>
<h2>What wind damage looks like from inside</h2>
<p>Walk the inside of the door with a flashlight. Look for a strut that is no longer straight across the section, for hinge leaves that are dished around the fastener heads, and for roller stems that sit at a slight angle in their hinges. Look at the jamb brackets and put a wrench on the lags: wind failures very often show up as a bracket that has walked loose rather than as a broken part. Check the top section where the opener arm bracket is bolted, since a door that flexed under load pulls hardest right there. Finally look at the header and jamb wood itself. In older detached garages behind the near-south-side neighborhoods, the framing has usually taken decades of weather, and a lag in soft wood cannot hold a design gust.</p>
<h2>After a power outage, before anything else</h2>
<p>North Texas storms take the power with them, and that is when people discover their opener has no battery backup. Pull the release cord, lift the door by hand, and get the car out if you need it, but do not leave a heavy door held up by a bent rail. If the door is bowed or the rail is deformed, leave it down and unplugged; a door that has lost its geometry can come out of the rail without warning. That call is genuinely urgent and is handled the way the <a href="../emergency-garage-door-repair/">emergency repair</a> page describes.</p>
<h2>The repair sequence</h2>
<p>Fasteners first: jamb brackets reset into sound wood, with the framing repaired or blocked where the lag has nothing to bite. Then geometry: rail plumbed and spaced, radius checked, rear hanger squared and re-hung off framing rather than off drywall. Then the door: struts replaced across any section that flexed, hinges renewed where holes are elongated, sections judged individually. Then the counterbalance is rechecked, because a door with new struts weighs more than it did. Nothing about this is a straightening job with a pry bar. A door that was bowed and forced back flat will keep binding at the same point until the underlying part is replaced.</p>
<h2>Reducing the odds next time</h2>
<p>Struts across the wide sections are the single most effective upgrade, and they are cheap compared to a section. Correct jamb bracket spacing with lags into framing, rather than into the jamb trim, is the second. On a replacement door, ask what wind load the specific model is built for and get the answer in writing, and ask whether the springs are being sized for the new door's actual weight rather than the old one's. Where a claim is involved, hail and wind damage are usually documented together, so read the <a href="../hail-damage-garage-door-repair/">hail damage</a> page too, and use the <a href="../services/">service list</a> to see what else the storm may have touched.</p>

## svc_wood_garage_door_repair_lede

Wood doors on Fort Worth's older streets are usually worth repairing rather than replacing, and in the historic districts the city's review process makes that even more true. The work is carpentry, hardware and paint, in that order.

## svc_wood_garage_door_repair_body

<h2>Two very different wood doors</h2>
<p>The first is an original or early carriage-style door on a detached structure behind a bungalow, hung as a swing or slide door or converted to sectional at some point. City preservation guidance for the Fairmount district notes that garages there were typically built detached, often of brick, concrete block, hollow clay tile or pressed tin, and were kept away from the house because of the fire risk of storing gasoline. Doors on those structures are frame-and-panel joinery, and they are repaired the way a window sash is repaired. The second is a modern wood or wood-composite sectional door, sometimes a steel door with an overlay, where the repair is closer to standard sectional work. Which one you have decides everything about the approach. The <a href="../services/">service list</a> covers the steel-door repairs.</p>
<h2>The historic district question, before anything else</h2>
<p>Fort Worth requires a Certificate of Appropriateness before a building permit is issued for exterior alterations in a historic district, and the city names doors and accessory structures among the work that needs one. Its district list includes Fairmount, Mistletoe Heights, Elizabeth Boulevard, the Stockyards and Morningside, and the Fairmount design guidance states plainly that all permitted exterior work needs the certificate. In practice this changes the economics: repairing and repainting an original door is usually a faster path than getting a replacement design approved, and where replacement is genuinely necessary the material, panel layout and light configuration will be reviewed. Nobody should be ordering a stock steel door for a contributing structure without checking, and the property owner is the one left holding that problem.</p>
<h2>What actually rots, and where</h2>
<p>Water travels down and sits at the bottom, so the bottom rail and the lower few inches of each stile are where a wood door dies. Look for paint that has lifted in a blister rather than flaked, which means moisture is behind it, and probe with an awl: sound wood resists, decayed wood takes the point with no effort. The other failure points are the joints. Frame-and-panel doors are held by mortise and tenon or by dowels, and once the glue line opens the door racks into a parallelogram, which shows as a door that catches on one corner. Panels themselves shrink and swell and are meant to float, so a hairline gap around a panel is normal and a cracked panel is not.</p>
<h2>Repair methods that hold up in this climate</h2>
<p>Dutchman repairs, where the decayed section is cut out square and a matching piece of the same species is let in and fastened, outlast filler on any part of the door that carries load. Epoxy consolidants and fillers have a place on non-structural areas and on carved detail, but a large epoxy patch on a bottom rail that is still getting wet will fail again. Loose joints get taken apart where possible, cleaned and reglued with clamps, rather than pinned with screws through the face. Then the paint: full priming on all six sides of any new wood, including end grain, and a coating rated for exterior use, because a July average high of 95.6 degrees with about twenty days over 100 is hard on a south-facing finish. Bare end grain at the bottom rail is how the next round of rot starts.</p>
<h2>Weight, balance and hardware</h2>
<p>Wood doors are heavy, and a repaired door weighs more than it did when half its bottom rail was rotten, because sound wood is denser than decayed wood and because new stock has been added. The counterbalance has to be checked and usually adjusted after significant carpentry, and springs on very old conversions are frequently the wrong size for the door as it now stands. Hinges and roller carriers on original doors are often screwed into wood that has stopped holding, so the screw holes get plugged and redrilled rather than filled with a longer screw. A hand lift at the end is the proof: the door should travel evenly and hold near waist height.</p>
<h2>What to expect from the visit</h2>
<p>An assessment first, in writing, saying which parts are being repaired, which are being replaced, whether the joints are coming apart, and whether the door needs to come off the building for shop work. A price follows that scope. Where the door is in a district, the assessment should say what the city will need to see. And where a door is genuinely past saving, that should be stated rather than absorbed into an open-ended repair; the <a href="../garage-door-replacement/">full door replacement</a> page covers that route. Storm damage to a wood door is a separate conversation, on the <a href="../storm-and-wind-damage-repair/">storm and wind damage</a> page.</p>

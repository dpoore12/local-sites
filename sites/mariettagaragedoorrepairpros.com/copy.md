## qa_3_question
Why do steel hardware and wood doors act up after humid weather?

## qa_3_answer
North Georgia has long warm, humid periods and about 50 to 55 inches of rain in a typical year. That moisture does not break a good door by itself, but it helps surface corrosion form on shafts, cables, bearings, and fasteners when water remains on the hardware. ## svc_garage_door_spring_repair_lede

Spring failures arrive as noise. The door was fine on the way out and immovable on the way back, and the part that changed sits on a shaft over the opening holding more stored energy than anything else in the garage.

## svc_garage_door_spring_repair_body

### The bang, and what the shaft shows

A counterbalance spring letting go is loud, and the sound is most of the diagnosis. Afterward the door either refuses to rise or comes up a few inches and stalls, because the springs were carrying the weight of all that sheet steel and the operator was only steering it. Walk in and look along the shaft above the header from a few feet back. A failed torsion spring shows a clean break with both halves relaxed and slightly unwound, leaving a visible gap in what used to be a continuous coil. In an older detached garage you may find extension springs instead, stretched back beside the horizontal rails with one of them hanging slack. The trade's own inspection checklist asks whether extension springs are contained by a cable running through the center of each one, because that cable is what keeps broken parts from flying across the garage.

### What the counterbalance on your street probably is

The age of the garage decides this more reliably than the age of the house. Marietta's Church Cherokee homeowners' handbook records that attached garages debuted in the 1940s and gained popularity through the 1950s, and that before the middle of the century most families kept a single car in a small secondary structure with one bay and one door. Those detached garages and carriage houses are still standing across the blocks covered by the Church-Cherokee and Whitlock Avenue districts, and they frequently pair shallow headroom above the opening with original extension hardware. Later subdivisions read differently. Charlton Forge, the Westside streets and the newer pockets out along the Loop are sectional-and-torsion, usually two bays wide, with room over the header for a full-length shaft. Arriving with the wrong assumption costs a second appointment.

### Wet air is a spring problem, and the industry says so

Manufacturers are blunt about moisture. The association data sheet on spring cycle life tells the trade to store springs dry so that surface rust never starts, since rust eats into the cross section of the wire and takes strength with it. It goes further: corrosion pits become the place fatigue cracks start and accelerate, which cuts cycle life outright. The same sheet notes that a door parked open for long stretches leaves its springs exposed to swings in temperature, moisture and air quality. Now set that against the Weather Service climatology for this part of the state. Rain runs 50 to 55 inches in a typical year, falls on roughly 120 days, and thunder is heard on 50 to 60 of them, with summer arriving as long spells of warm humid air. Spring wire here is aging on a schedule that wire in a dry state never sees.

### Winding bars, and why nothing in the toolbox substitutes

A broken torsion spring is still dangerous. The surviving half remains partly loaded, and the shaft it sits on can rotate. Bars cut to fit the winding cone are the only correct tool for touching one. A screwdriver, a length of rebar or a socket extension will slip free under load and fly. The published checklist says it without hedging: with a broken spring, do not operate the door, and do not attempt to remove, repair or adjust springs, cables, brackets or the wood and steel they mount to. Until help arrives, cut power to the operator, keep the door on the slab, and keep vehicles and people out from under the opening.

### Two springs, one age, one decision

When a two-spring door loses one, the survivor has logged the same openings in the same damp garage and is not a fresh part. Fitting both at once costs a little more steel and removes an entire return trip. The cycle life sheet also warns that a spring has to match the door's counterbalance requirement, which is where a repainted door gets people. Adding coats to a real wood door can push it past the weight the manufacturer specified, and a technician can put a scale under it once the counterbalance is disabled rather than guess. That is a live question on the older streets, where doors are wood and get refinished on a cycle.

### Where a hurried swap goes wrong

Two things separate a spring job that lasts from one that comes back. The first is turn count, which the door maker specifies for that height: too much torque loads every other counterbalance part, and too little leaves the door drifting down out of its open position. The second is everything the failure touched on the way down. A break drops the door hard enough to unseat a cable and bruise the bottom brackets, so cables, drums and bearings get inspected while the tension is off.

### What the visit looks like, and where the number lands

Wire diameter, inside diameter, length and wind direction come off the failed spring or its stamped cone, and replacements are matched to the door's measured weight instead of a chart. Both springs come off, the drums are reset, and the new pair is wound to the specified turns. Corroded fasteners often have to be cut out rather than turned, which is where the extra minutes in a humid garage go. Then the operator is disconnected and the door is lifted by hand: it should hold near waist height rather than sinking or climbing. Ask to watch that. Pricing is quoted before a tool comes out, and the spread inside it comes from spring count, wire size, cycle rating, and whether cables, bearings or drums go with them.

## svc_garage_door_opener_repair_lede

Two unrelated complaints reach the phone with the same label. A door that retreats partway down is the safety circuit doing exactly what it was built to do, and a motor running while the door sits still is often a one-minute fix.

## svc_garage_door_opener_repair_body

### Down a couple of feet, then straight back up

Federal rules put that behavior in writing. Part 1211 of title 16 requires a downward moving residential door to begin reversing within two seconds of meeting an obstruction, then return the door to the fully open position and stop there. So an operator that backs off and parks itself open is not malfunctioning in any obvious sense. It is reporting that something interrupted the close. The whole job is figuring out what it believes it touched, and the answer is almost never the motor overhead.

### What the rule actually requires of the box on your ceiling

The same federal standard applies to every residential operator manufactured on or after January 1, 1993. Each one has to carry inherent primary entrapment protection built into the drive. On top of that, a vertically moving door needs a second layer: either constant pressure on a control installed within sight of the door, or a connection for an external secondary device, meaning a photoelectric sensor or an edge sensor mounted on the door. The rule also governs the red handle. It must be visually distinct from the rest of the operator, adjustable to six feet above the garage floor, and able to release an obstructed door under a pull of no more than fifty pounds. The industry checklist adds the measurement nobody takes: that photo eye beam should sit no higher than six inches above the floor.

### Pollen, condensation and two lenses near the slab

Humid air here does not break an operator, but it reliably confuses the two eyes bolted beside the jambs. Warm moist air meeting a cool lens leaves droplets on it, which is the same mechanism the door industry describes for condensation on panels and glazing, and a wet car parked inside raises the humidity that feeds it. Spring pollen sticks to the film. Grit blown under the door builds up in the small brackets, and years of the door shaking the jamb walks those brackets out of square. Wipe both lenses dry with a soft cloth, sight the brackets at each other, and look for a steady indicator on both housings before suspecting anything electronic.

### Turning the force up is not a repair

When a door binds, the operator reads the resistance as an obstruction and reverses, which is the protection working correctly on a mechanical fault. A swollen wood bottom section, a bearing that has rusted dry, a cracked roller or a bottom seal grabbing the slab will all do it. Raising the close force setting hides the symptom for a while and spends the safety margin that caught the problem. The honest test is the one the checklist describes: lay a solid object an inch and a half thick flat under the center of the door and close it. The door should reverse on contact and return fully open. If it does not, that operator needs service before anyone talks about force settings.

### Motor noise, and a chain that never moves

Watch the rail while somebody presses the button. Chain or belt traveling with the door standing still means the trolley has come off the carriage, usually because the emergency release was pulled or snagged. With the door flat on the floor and moving freely by hand, it re-engages in about a minute. A grinding rattle with nothing traveling is different: that is normally a nylon drive gear with its teeth rounded off, which is a part inside the head rather than grounds for a new unit. A short hum followed by silence points instead at a starting capacitor or the board.

### Wiring in a detached garage is a separate trade

Behind the older houses, the garage often sits at the end of a narrow linear drive, sometimes past a covered carriage entrance, and it may have no dedicated circuit at the ceiling at all. That is not a door problem. Marietta requires an electrical permit for work done on electrical systems, and the state minimum electrical code adopted by the Georgia Department of Community Affairs is the 2023 National Electrical Code with Georgia amendments. The city also runs stand-alone trade inspections in occupied homes over a video call, scheduled by phone with an installer standing at the equipment, which keeps a small electrical scope from turning into a week of waiting.

### When a new head is honest, and what you should be told

An operator old enough to have no secondary entrapment protection at all should be replaced rather than nursed, and so should one with a cracked housing or a board nobody stocks. Two repairs inside two years is a fair signal as well. Short of that, most heads on houses here are worth fixing, and the answer you should get is a named fault, the price of the part, and a straight read on whether spending it makes sense given the age of the unit. Battery backup and a belt drive are real improvements in a place where storm outages happen, and they should be offered as upgrades in exactly those words rather than folded into a repair.

## svc_off_track_garage_door_repair_lede

When a door hangs at an angle in its frame, the fault is in the cables, the rollers or the rail. This is also the one failure that reliably worsens while somebody stands in the driveway cycling the button to gauge it.

## svc_off_track_garage_door_repair_body

### Read it from the doorway and leave it there

Whatever cables and rollers are still engaged are the only things holding a derailed door up, and that geometry stops being stable the moment a section leaves its rail. Cycling the operator to see how bad it is turns a partial derailment into a door across a hood. Kill power at the outlet, keep everyone out of the opening, and put the pry bar down. This is the failure that gets pulled to the front of the day rather than scheduled for later in the week.

### The three ways a door leaves its rail

A lift cable can jump its drum. Each one anchors low on the door and climbs into machined grooves on a drum carried at the end of the spring shaft, and a hard drop after a spring break can throw it clear of them. That half of the door loses its lift and racks in the opening, with loose cable coiled near the jamb as the tell. A roller can also come out. Nylon wheels crack and steel ones seize once their bearings run dry and grit packs in, and a seized roller skids until it climbs out, almost always at the curve where the vertical rail turns horizontal. Or the rail itself is bent, from a bumper, a fallen limb, or a section that has already been forced back once.

### Red clay, a sloping lot, and a jamb that moved

Most of this county sits on Piedmont uplands, and the soil series mapped across that landscape is Cecil. The federal soil survey places it on divides, ridges and side slopes at grades running from level to twenty-five percent. Under a sandy loam surface it turns to red clay the official description calls firm, sticky and plastic, carrying on well past sixty inches before bedrock.

Two consequences land on a garage. Surface runoff is rated medium and these lots are graded, so a garage at the low end of a drive receives the water coming off it, roughly fifty inches a year. Every roller and rail in that opening is also referenced to a jamb bolted to framing at the slab edge. Manufacturers treat that connection as being as important as the door itself. They publish anchor spacing schedules for wind loads from ten to sixty pounds per square foot and recommend engineering advice for the attachment. A jamb that has settled or rotated a fraction of an inch out of plumb shows nothing from the street. It shows up as one roller climbing at the radius.

### The bottom two feet take the weather

Water sitting at the slab edge does its damage low. On a wood door, the bottom section wicks moisture and receives more of it than any other part, which is why the trade's wood door guidance asks for inspection every twelve to eighteen months and a close look at the wood behind every hardware attachment point. Loose hardware on a swollen section is not a tightening job, it is a structural question. The bottom brackets carry full cable tension, so corroded fasteners there usually get cut out instead of turned. Grit ground into rollers, a seal no longer meeting the slab, and a rusted bearing plate all show up first as drag, then as a door that comes off.

### What not to attempt in the meantime

Nothing on a derailed door is a homeowner repair, and the reason is that the counterbalance and the cables are still loaded even when the door looks slack. Do not clamp, wedge or rope it into position, do not lift one corner to reseat a roller, and do not run the opener to test whether it caught. Park vehicles outside the bay. If the door is standing partly open, keep the whole opening clear until somebody has secured it below the lowest roller.

### How the repair proceeds

The door gets clamped so it cannot travel, then sections come back into line one at a time. Rollers are reseated or replaced, cables are respooled with matched tension at both drums, and rail is straightened only where its shape and mounting holes are still sound. A rail with a hard crease gets replaced rather than persuaded, because a forced rail binds again within weeks. Hinges are checked at every joint, since a section that came out usually took one with it. The jamb, the header attachment and the plumb of both vertical rails get verified, which is the step that matters on an opening that has moved. Then the door comes off the operator and is balanced by hand.

### What decides the number

Rollers, cables and hinges are inexpensive parts, and rail sections are moderate. What actually moves the total is how far the door traveled out of line, whether any section bent in the process, and whether the framing behind the rail has to be repaired before anything is anchored back to it. A section damaged past sensible repair should be named as that on site, before the work, rather than billed as a repair and then a replacement.

### Historic review comes before the order, not after

In August 2013 a stretch of Kennesaw Avenue became the city's first locally designated historic district, and properties inside it need a Certificate of Appropriateness before starting the categories of work listed in the preservation article of the development code. Downtown, exterior work goes to the Historic Board of Review on a Certificate of Approval application, and the city states that a building permit will not be issued without an approved certificate where one is required. That guidance also notes those garages historically had a single bay and one door. A wide flush steel panel on a 1920s carriage house is a review problem long before it is a taste problem.

### The code the inspector is working from

Georgia adopts construction codes at the state level. The Department of Community Affairs lists the mandatory residential code as the 2024 International Residential Code with Georgia amendments, and city inspectors work from that adopted edition rather than a local rewrite. Licensing sits at the state as well. The Secretary of State's policy statement on traditional specialty contractors names overhead doors as a specialty category. Specialty contractors working inside their own specialty are not required to hold a residential or general contractor license. They still have to comply with every applicable national, state and local code and ordinance. On the city side, a residential permit application asks for a copy of the general contractor's state license and business license. A homeowner pulling the permit personally signs a notarized property owner's affidavit under the conditions in the state licensing statute.

### One section, or the whole door

A single crushed section can often be replaced on its own, which is the cheaper answer when the door is a steel profile still in production, the other sections are sound, and a color match is acceptable. Matching gets harder with age, and an embossed pattern discontinued fifteen years ago will not blend. Full replacement earns its keep when the stiles of a wood door have gone soft, when several sections are creased, or when the door has already been rebuilt more than once.

### Measuring an older opening

Nothing about a replacement can be quoted from a photograph. The measurements that decide the order are the finished opening width and height, headroom between the header and the ceiling, backroom for the horizontal rails, the condition and plumb of both jambs, and the level of the slab across the threshold. Older detached garages are where this bites. Headroom is often short enough to force low-clearance hardware, the jamb has usually been repaired at least once, and a slab that has settled at one corner leaves a gap the new bottom seal has to close. Any jamb or header rebuilding belongs in the written scope, not in a conversation on installation morning.

### Specifying a door for wet summers

Material choice is where a local specification differs from a catalog. A wood or wood-clad door should be finished on all six sides before it is hung, because a door finished after installation leaves faces exposed and can void the manufacturer's warranty, and wood doors want inspection and maintenance every twelve to eighteen months here. Insulated doors and glazing bring condensation into the conversation, and the industry answer is ventilation, tight seals at the glazing perimeter and around each section, and cleaning products the glazing maker approves. Sealed rollers, galvanized or coated hardware and a bottom seal sized to the actual slab profile are worth naming line by line in the quote.

### Lead time, and what moves the total

Stock steel doors in common sizes move quickly. A made-to-order wood or carriage-house door takes weeks, and where the address needs design review, that clock starts only once the certificate is approved. Costs track construction and size rather than brand: one bay against two, insulation, glass, real wood against clad or steel, custom hardware, jamb repair, removal of the old door, and the permit where one applies. Nothing should be ordered before the opening is measured, the jurisdiction confirmed, and any review approved in writing.

## pricing_body

### Three things move the number

Start with the failed component. Springs, cables, rollers, bearings, an opener gear set, a control board and a damaged section are separate parts at separate prices, and they are not interchangeable diagnoses.

Second, the material. Wood and wood-clad doors are common on older houses and around the square, and wood behaves nothing like steel. It absorbs moisture, gains weight through a wet season, swells against the jamb and rots at the bottom rail where water sits. A heavier door needs stronger springs, and a swollen door needs the balance reset rather than the opener asked to force it shut.

Third, access. A detached garage behind a Frasier Park or Westside house, down a narrow drive with one circuit and no ceiling blocking, is a longer job than a two-car attached garage on a newer street. Access never shows on a price list and always shows on an invoice.

### Humidity is a cost driver here

This is not a decorative detail. The Weather Service describes north Georgia summers as long stretches of warm, humid weather, with the region averaging 50 to 55 inches of rain across roughly 120 days of measurable precipitation a year. Standing damp, pollen and grit work into the cable strands, the shaft bearings, the hinge pins, the roller stems and the fasteners holding the bottom fixture on. Corroded steel wears faster and binds sooner.

What that means in dollars is twofold. Spring and cable life is shorter here than in a dry climate, so those repairs come around more often. And seized hardware has to be cut out rather than loosened, which turns a thirty-minute job into a ninety-minute one. Labor is the majority of most repair bills in this market: the May 2025 median hourly wage for general maintenance and repair workers across metro Atlanta is $23.86, and the billed hour is a multiple of that once the vehicle, the stocked parts and the drive time are covered.

### Permit math, and the downtown exception

Servicing hardware on an existing door is not a permit matter. Replacing the door assembly can be. The city states that a permit is required whenever an owner or contractor intends to construct, enlarge, alter, repair, move or demolish a building or structure, and publishes no garage-door-only exemption, so scope and address should be confirmed with Building Inspections first. Addresses outside the city limits fall to Cobb County instead. When a permit does apply, Marietta calculates it from construction value at $5 per $1,000 of labor and materials with a $50 minimum, and failed inspections escalate at $50, then $75, then $100 for the third and each subsequent visit.

The bigger local variable is the Downtown Historic District. Any exterior work there requires a Certificate of Approval from the Historic Board of Review, and the city says building permits will not be issued without an approved certificate when one is required. For a garage door that means material, color, window layout and overall appearance are reviewed before anything is ordered. A homeowner who selects a door first and applies second can lose weeks, and a special-order door the board will not approve is the costliest mistake this trade offers here.

### What a straight quote looks like

It separates the diagnostic charge from the repair, and says whether the first applies against the second. It gives the spring specification in full, meaning wire diameter, inside diameter, length and cycle rating, and says whether one spring or the pair is being replaced. Leaving an original spring beside a new one on the same shaft is how a household pays twice for one visit. It identifies roller type, since sealed nylon and bare steel wear differently here. On an opener it lists the drive type, the horsepower class, and whether the sensors, rail and wall control are new. On a replacement it names the manufacturer, model, construction, insulation value, finish, glass, the permit if one applies, historic review if the address needs it, jamb and header repair, and removal of the old door.

### The upsell, and the part nobody prices online

The pitch to be skeptical of is a moisture problem answered with a full replacement. A door dragging at the jamb, a bottom seal that no longer meets the slab, surface rust on the track: those are adjustments, seals, bearings and rollers. Rot that has climbed into the stiles of a wood door is a different conversation, and an honest technician will show you the wood rather than describe it.

What national averages never carry is the local overhead: the valuation-based permit, an escalating reinspection, historic board review downtown, jambs rebuilt where water has sat for years, wiring and blocking for a detached garage, and the wait on a made-to-order wood or clad door. Those items are the space between the middle and the top of every band above.

Commercial openings fail differently from residential ones. The doors are heavier, the cycle counts are ten times higher, some of them are rated fire assemblies with annual testing obligations, and downtime stops work rather than inconveniencing a household.

<h2>Four door types, four failure patterns</h2>
<p>A rolling steel service door coils onto a barrel above the opening and fails at the barrel, the curtain slats and the guides. A sectional commercial door is a heavier version of the residential design, usually on high-cycle springs and a jackshaft operator. <h2>Cycles are the number that matters on a loading bay</h2>
<p>A household door might see four cycles a day. A shop bay off Franklin Gateway or a service door on South Marietta Parkway can see forty, and springs are rated in cycles rather than years. <p>If the door is a labeled fire assembly, NFPA 80 requires inspection and testing immediately after installation and at least annually thereafter, performed by a person whose qualifications are acceptable to the authority having jurisdiction, with thirteen items verified. <h2>What genuinely warrants an after-hours call</h2>
<p>Four situations move to the front of the queue. A door standing open that will not come down leaves the house open. <p>Every operator has a manual release, usually a red cord on the trolley. It is meant to be pulled with the door fully closed. <p>An after-hours call is priced differently from a scheduled one, and that difference belongs in the conversation before the truck rolls. ## svc_garage_door_hinge_repair_lede

Nothing on a sectional door costs less than a hinge, and nothing does more damage on its way out. A cracked leaf tears the section steel it is bolted through, and the bracket at each bottom corner is holding a loaded lift cable.

## svc_garage_door_hinge_repair_body

<h2>The hinges are numbered, and the numbers are not decorative</h2>
<p>On a sectional door the hinges are stamped with a number, and the number sets the offset that keeps each section tracking as the door moves through the curve. A number two hinge in a number three position pulls the section out of line, binds a roller and wears the rail. When someone has previously replaced a hinge with whatever the store had, the symptom is a door that rubs at one point in its travel and a roller that keeps failing on that side. Any hinge replacement starts by reading the stamp on the hinge that is coming off. Related repairs are grouped on <a href="../services/">the services index</a>.</p>
<h2>Where the steel cracks, and why the hole grows</h2>
<p>Failure begins at the hinge's fastener holes. Each cycle transmits load through those holes, and once the hole elongates the hinge moves before it lifts, which is the pop you hear at a joint. From there two things happen. The hinge leaf cracks across its narrow section, or, worse, the sheet steel of the section face tears around the fasteners and the hinge tears out of the door. On the older houses inside the East Park Community and similar pre-1980 blocks, the same story plays out in wood: the fastener holes in the stile enlarge, the wood softens with moisture, and the hinge screws no longer have anything to hold.</p>
<h2>Humidity, and hardware fixed to swollen wood</h2>
<p>Wood doors here take on moisture through long humid summers, and the industry's wood door guidance asks specifically for inspection of the wood at every hardware attachment point on a twelve to eighteen month cycle. That is because a hinge on a swollen or softened stile is not a tightening job. Driving the screws harder crushes the fibers and the hinge is loose again in a month. The correct repair is to assess the wood, plug and redrill or replace the stile where the material is sound enough to accept it, and to say honestly when the section itself has gone past repair. Steel doors have an equivalent failure: fasteners corroded into the section face, which have to be cut out rather than turned.</p>
<h2>The bottom bracket is the one part to leave alone</h2>
<p>The bracket at each bottom corner is not a hinge, even though it looks like one. It anchors the lift cable, so it carries the door's full counterbalance tension, and unbolting it with the springs wound sends it and the cable across the garage. This is the part where the industry checklist stops giving instructions and tells the reader to call a trained technician. A corroded bottom bracket, a bracket bent by a vehicle bump, or a bottom section that has begun to tear around it, all require the tension released and the door secured first.</p>
<h2>Substituting parts is not the same as matching them</h2>
<p>Manufacturers specify every component on a door for a reason, and the trade's guidance on substitution is explicit that visual similarity does not establish equal strength, that differences in material thickness, composition and strength cannot be identified by eye, and that substituting components on a tested or wind-load-rated door can weaken the assembly. Corrosion resistance is part of that specification too, which matters in a climate with fifty to fifty-five inches of rain a year. A galvanized hinge and a painted one look alike in a parts bin and do not age alike on a jamb ten feet from a wet driveway.</p>
<h2>What the repair covers, and what it prevents</h2>
<p>A hinge and bracket visit inspects every joint on the door, replaces cracked or elongated hinges with correctly numbered parts, checks the section steel or stile behind each one, checks the rollers those hinges carry, and verifies fastener tightness against the specification rather than by feel. The reason to do it before it becomes urgent is what comes next: a hinge that tears out at the top of travel is the most common way a door leaves its rail, which is a much larger repair covered under <a href="../off-track-garage-door-repair/">off-track work</a>. ## svc_garage_door_insulation_lede

Insulation ratings on garage doors are quoted two different ways, and only one of them describes the door you would actually be buying. The other is a calculation for a single panel with none of the door's leaks in it.

## svc_garage_door_insulation_body

<h2>R-value and U-factor are answers to different questions</h2>
<p>The industry's own technical guidance is unusually direct about this. An R-value on a garage door is calculated for the section only. A U-factor is measured on the whole installed assembly under a test standard, ANSI/DASMA 105, and is expressed in Btu per hour per square foot per degree Fahrenheit. The two are not reciprocals of one another and are not comparable, because the assembly test includes what the guidance calls thermal shortcuts: the joints between sections, the end stiles, the thermal breaks, air spaces and the perimeter seals. A door advertised with a high panel R-value and a poor assembly can perform worse than a lower-rated door built tighter. Ask for the U-factor. The rest of the work on a door is listed on <a href="../services/">the services index</a>.</p>
<h2>What a Marietta summer does to an attached garage</h2>
<p>North Georgia runs thirty to sixty days a year at or above ninety degrees, arriving as long humid spells rather than dry heat. A west-facing steel door absorbs that all afternoon and radiates it into the garage after sunset. In the attached two-bay garages that dominate Charlton Forge and the newer subdivisions, that heat has somewhere to go: the shared wall with the house, the ceiling under a bedroom, and the door between the garage and the kitchen. Insulating the door does not air-condition the garage. What it does is lower the peak surface temperature and slow the transfer, which is measurable at the shared wall and noticeable in a room over the garage.</p>
<h2>Winter is the smaller half of the case here</h2>
<p>The cold side of the argument is real but modest. The Weather Service counts fifty to seventy nights a year at or below freezing in this part of the state, with the average last spring freeze in early April. That is enough to matter for a garage holding a water heater, a laundry pair, stored paint or a workshop, and enough to stiffen an uninsulated door's bottom seal and its grease. It is not enough to justify the top of the product range on heating grounds alone. The stronger local reasons are summer heat, noise, and door rigidity.</p>
<h2>Adding insulation adds weight, and weight changes the springs</h2>
<p>This is the part retrofit kits leave out. Bonding polystyrene panels or a batt system into an uninsulated steel door adds real mass, and the counterbalance was wound for the door's original weight. Manufacturer guidance on spring life is clear that a spring has to match the door's counterbalance requirement, and a door that has gained weight now runs under-sprung: it feels heavy by hand, sinks from waist height when released, and the operator carries what the springs should. The correct sequence is to weigh the door after the work and adjust or replace the springs to suit, which is why an insulation retrofit is a counterbalance job as much as a thermal one. That work is covered under <a href="../garage-door-spring-repair/">spring repair</a>.</p>
<h2>Two-layer and three-layer construction, and what the layers mean</h2>
<p>A single-layer door is one skin of steel with nothing behind it. A two-layer door has insulation bonded to the back of that skin, usually polystyrene, and is a large improvement in rigidity as well as temperature. A three-layer or sandwich door has a second steel skin over the insulation, is stiffer again, is quieter under wind load, and is what polyurethane foamed-in-place construction is normally sold as. Rigidity matters here for a reason unrelated to comfort: a stiffer door resists the wind pressure of a summer thunderstorm and the flexing that loosens hinge fasteners over years.</p>
<h2>Retrofit or replace, and the seals that decide the outcome</h2>
<p>Retrofitting an existing steel door is sensible where the door is sound, the sections are straight and the springs can be adjusted for the new weight. It is not sensible on a door with soft wood, creased sections or a jamb that has moved. And on any insulated door the perimeter is the deciding factor, because the assembly test exists precisely to capture what the perimeter loses; the guidance itself lists the seals among the thermal shortcuts. Replacing the bottom strip and the stop moulding at the same time is not an add-on, it is half the result, and that work is covered under <a href="../garage-door-weather-seal-replacement/">weather seal replacement</a>.</p>

<p>Two measurements decide whether a standard kit will fit. Rail length has to match door height, and a seven-foot door and an eight-foot door take different rails. Backroom is the depth from the door line to the far wall, and it has to accept the horizontal rails plus the operator body behind them. In a shallow single-bay garage of the kind common on the older streets, there is not always room, and the answer is either low-headroom hardware or a wall-mount unit rather than forcing a ceiling-mounted rail into a space that will not take it.</p>
<h2>The permit side, and how the city schedules it</h2>
<p>Marietta requires an electrical permit for work performed on electrical systems, and there is a separate low-voltage application covering telephone, cable and security wiring, which is the category a network cable or an accessory keypad run can fall into. Applications go through the city's online submission system, and the published expectation is twenty-four to forty-eight hours to process, with an emergency repair potentially qualifying for a faster release. New meters or any work requiring a meter connection need a safe-to-connect permit. Inspection requests made before four in the afternoon are scheduled for the following day, the first inspection carries no fee, and failed reinspections escalate from fifty to seventy-five to one hundred dollars, which is a real argument for having the rough-in right the first time.</p>
<h2>What every new head must have on it</h2>
<p>A newly installed residential operator carries the entrapment protection the federal rule requires, meaning the inherent protection built into the drive plus an external secondary device, which on a sectional door is the photoelectric pair. Two installation details are worth watching. The beam should be set no higher than six inches above the floor. And the release cord has to be adjustable to six feet above the floor so an adult can reach it without a ladder. If the old head is being kept and only misbehaving, the diagnosis path is <a href="../garage-door-opener-repair/">opener repair</a> instead, and if the reason for the new unit is phone control, the requirements change and are covered under <a href="../smart-garage-door-opener/">smart opener installation</a>.</p>
<h2>Power at a detached garage is its own job</h2>
<p>Behind the older houses in the Church-Cherokee and Whitlock Avenue districts, the detached garage may have a single lamp on a switch loop and nothing else. An extension cord run from the house is not an installation, it is a fire risk and it will not pass an inspection. A dedicated circuit to the garage is electrical work with its own permit and its own inspection, and it should appear in the quote as a separate line with its own price rather than being absorbed into the operator figure. Where that circuit is not in the budget yet, a wall-mount or ceiling unit still needs a proper outlet at the ceiling, and the sequence is electrical work first, operator second.</p>

## svc_garage_door_remote_programming_lede

Remotes and keypads fail in ways that look identical from the driveway and are not. A dead keypad, a remote the head no longer recognizes, and a receiver being talked over by a stronger transmitter all end with a button that does nothing.

## svc_garage_door_remote_programming_body

<h2>Start by proving which half is deaf</h2>
<p>Press the hardwired wall control inside the garage. If the door runs from the wall button and not from the remote or the outside keypad, the motor, drive and safety circuit are fine and the problem is radio or programming. If nothing runs from the wall control either, the fault is in the head or its power, and the rest of this page does not apply; the other repair paths are listed on <a href="../services/">the services index</a>. That single test saves an hour of pointless remote pairing.</p>
<h2>Learn buttons, rolling codes and lost remotes</h2>
<p>Openers made through the 1980s used a bank of tiny switches, and matching the pattern in the remote to the pattern in the head was the whole procedure. Modern heads use a rolling code: the remote and receiver agree on a new value every press, so a captured transmission is worthless the second time. Pairing is done at the learn button on the head, which is also the button that erases every stored remote when it is held down. That erase step is the correct response to a remote lost with a vehicle, and it is why every other remote and keypad in the household has to be re-taught in the same visit.</p>
<h2>A garage door band that the military is licensed to use</h2>
<p>This matters more here than in most cities. Openers are unlicensed Part 15 devices, which carry the label saying the device must accept any interference received, and many are programmed at 390 MHz. Federal auditors documented that Department of Defense land mobile radios operate from 380 to 399.9 MHz, that this overlaps the range used by unlicensed low-power openers, and that 387 to 393 MHz carries the maximum interference potential because opener receivers listen across a wide bandwidth. Dobbins Air Reserve Base sits inside Marietta, sixteen miles northwest of Atlanta, supporting more than 5,700 personnel across four services. Interference is not the first thing to suspect, but it is a real category, and the pattern to look for is a remote that works at three feet and not at thirty, with the effect coming and going by time of day rather than by weather.</p>
<h2>What that means for a receiver that has gone weak</h2>
<p>Range loss usually has a duller cause. A remote battery reading low under load will fire an indicator and not the door. Antenna wire on the head gets tucked up into the housing during a repair or clipped short, and the receiver loses most of its reach. On a genuinely persistent problem, newer opener families operating in the 310 to 320 MHz range are a real answer rather than a sales pitch, and replacing the head to escape a band is a decision worth making with the numbers in front of you rather than on a hunch.</p>
<h2>Keypads live outdoors in a Georgia year</h2>
<p>The wireless keypad on the jamb is the one control exposed to weather. North Georgia gets fifty to seventy nights a year at or below freezing, which is what finally kills a marginal nine-volt cell; the same keypad then works again at noon, which convinces people it is haunted. Summer is worse in a slower way. Warm humid air gets under a cracked rubber keypad membrane, and the contact pads behind it corrode until one digit stops registering. A keypad that takes several presses per digit is failing mechanically and does not need reprogramming.</p>
<h2>The two changes worth making while a technician is there</h2>
<p>Reprogramming is a short visit, so two things are worth doing at once. First, a PIN that is not a house number or a birth year, because a keypad code is a door key. Second, a temporary code for a contractor or a dog walker where the head supports one, deleted afterwards. If the reason for the visit is that a phone should open the door as well, that is <a href="../smart-garage-door-opener/">a smart opener question</a> with its own federal alarm requirements. And if the head is old enough that no compatible remote is still made, the honest path is <a href="../garage-door-opener-repair/">opener repair or replacement</a>, quoted as a part and a labor figure rather than folded into a programming charge.</p>

## svc_garage_door_safety_inspection_lede

A safety inspection is a pass or fail exercise against published criteria, not an opinion about a door's condition. The tests are specific, the measurements are numbers, and a failure on any one of them stops the inspection.

## svc_garage_door_safety_inspection_body

<h2>Which rule applies to the operator overhead</h2>
<p>The governing document for residential operators is 16 CFR 1211, and it applies to every unit manufactured on or after January 1, 1993. Two layers of entrapment protection are required. The first is inherent, built into the drive, and it is what makes a door moving down begin reversing within two seconds of meeting an obstruction and then travel back to fully open. The second is external: either a control requiring constant pressure and installed within sight of the door, or a device such as a photoelectric pair or an edge sensor. Establishing the operator's age is therefore the first thing an inspection does, because it sets what should be there at all. Everything else on the door is covered on <a href="../services/">the services index</a>.</p>
<h2>The reversal test, and the object it is done with</h2>
<p>The trade's published checklist calls for a solid object an inch and a half high, such as a piece of two-by-four at least six inches long, laid flat on the floor under the center of the door. The door is closed onto it. A passing door touches the object and reverses. A door that stops and sits, that pauses and pushes, or that reverses only after several seconds, fails. This is deliberately a physical test rather than an electronic one, because it measures what the door actually does rather than what a control board reports. The same checklist tells the reader to stop at any answer of no and call a trained technician instead of continuing, which is the right instruction and the reason a homeowner inspection and a professional one are not the same product.</p>
<h2>The photo eye, the labels and the release cord</h2>
<p>Three measurements complete the operator side. The photoelectric beam should sit no higher than six inches above the floor, which is low enough to detect a small child crawling and is routinely found mounted at knee height instead. The door's warning labels should be present and legible in the places the industry specifies: at the spring or bracket, on the back of the door, and near the bottom corner brackets. And the manual release should be visually distinct from the rest of the operator, adjustable to six feet above the floor so it can be reached without a ladder, and able to release an obstructed door under a pull of no more than fifty pounds. A release cord tied up out of reach or removed entirely is a failure.</p>
<h2>What fails an inspection in the older half of this city</h2>
<p>City census figures put thirty-seven percent of Marietta's housing ahead of 1980, and inspections there fail on a consistent short list. Operators predating any external entrapment protection. Photoelectric sensors that have been unplugged or jumpered at the head by somebody tired of the door reversing. Extension springs with no containment cable through the center, which the checklist asks about specifically. Warning labels painted over or long gone. Manual latches still live on a door that runs on an operator. And a counterbalance that has drifted so far out of adjustment that the door will not stay at waist height with the operator disconnected, which is a failure of the door rather than the electronics.</p>
<h2>Why the balance test belongs in a safety report</h2>
<p>An unbalanced door is a safety finding, not a comfort one. If the door is heavy, the operator's force setting has usually been raised to compensate, and a raised force setting is exactly what defeats the inherent reversal protection the federal rule requires. So a report that lists a passing reversal test on a door with a raised force setting is describing a coincidence. The correct sequence is to disconnect the operator, weigh or lift the door by hand, record whether it holds at waist height, restore the balance mechanically, and only then verify the reversal and the sensors.</p>
<h2>What the written report should contain</h2>
<p>For a sale, a rental turnover or a household with young children, the point of hiring this out is the document. It should record the operator's manufacturer, model and date, whether external entrapment protection is present and of what type, the beam height in inches, the result of the reversal test with the object described, the release pull and its height, the label condition, the balance result, the spring type and any containment cables, and the condition of cables, rollers, hinges and bottom brackets. Each finding should be marked pass, fail or repair recommended. If the door needs work rather than a report, that is <a href="../garage-door-tune-up/">a tune-up</a>, and if the failure is confined to the beam and its wiring, it is <a href="../garage-door-sensor-repair/">sensor repair</a>.</p>

## svc_garage_door_sensor_repair_lede

Two small housings bolted low beside the jambs stop more closing cycles in this city than any motor fault. When a door reverses for nothing, the beam between them is broken, dirty, misaimed, or reaching the head through wire that has been quietly failing for years.

## svc_garage_door_sensor_repair_body

<h2>Six inches off the slab, and why that number is fixed</h2>
<p>The photoelectric pair is not an accessory. Federal rule 16 CFR 1211 requires a residential operator built on or after January 1, 1993 to carry a second layer of entrapment protection beyond the drive itself, and a photo eye is the layer most houses got. The trade's own inspection sheet puts a number on the installation: the beam should sit no higher than six inches above the floor, low enough to catch a child or a pet rather than the roof of a bumper. Sensor work is diagnostic work, and it belongs beside the other repairs listed on <a href="../services/">the services index</a> rather than treated as a battery swap.</p>
<h2>What each light pattern is telling you</h2>
<p>Look at both housings before touching anything. Most sending units show one steady indicator whenever they have power, and the receiving unit shows a steady indicator only while it can see the beam. A dark sender is a power or wiring problem at the head. A sender lit and a receiver blinking or dark is an aim, obstruction or lens problem. Both dark, with the operator refusing to close but running fine on the up cycle, usually points at the low-voltage terminals on the motor head instead of the eyes.</p>
<h2>Yellow spring air, and a lens you cannot see through</h2>
<p>Marietta sits under a genuinely heavy pollen load, and the daily count published by the region's only National Allergy Bureau certified counting station in Atlanta is measured as grains of pollen in a cubic meter of air over the previous twenty-four hours. Those grains settle on any surface at ankle height. A lens does not have to look dirty to scatter enough infrared to drop the signal below the receiver's threshold, and a driveway that sheds grit toward the opening finishes the job. Wipe both lenses with a dry soft cloth, never a solvent, and do it before assuming a part has failed.</p>
<h2>Aim, brackets and a door that shakes the jamb</h2>
<p>An infrared beam is narrow. The two housings have to face each other squarely across the opening, level with one another and square to the door line. What moves them is ordinary use: every closing cycle shakes the jamb a little, and a decade of that walks a sheet-metal bracket out of position or bends a slotted mount. On the attached two-bay garages common in Charlton Forge and the newer streets, the tell is a door that closes at midday and refuses at night, because a bracket has drifted to the edge of the beam's tolerance and thermal movement finishes it.</p>
<h2>Bell wire is the fault more often than the sensor</h2>
<p>Sensor wiring is thin two-conductor low-voltage wire, usually stapled up the jamb and along the ceiling to the operator terminals. Staples driven too hard cut the insulation and short the pair. Splices twisted together and taped make a joint that corrodes through a humid summer, and one green conductor end can drop the circuit for weeks before failing outright. A technician tests continuity from the head to each housing rather than swapping sensors and hoping, then replaces the run with properly stapled wire and mechanical connectors where a splice is genuinely needed.</p>
<h2>Older garages without a photo eye at all</h2>
<p>Some heads on the pre-1980 half of this city's housing predate secondary entrapment protection entirely, and a few have had the sensor circuit jumpered out by somebody who tired of it. Neither is acceptable. Some manufacturers sell a compatible sensor kit for a given head, and where none exists the honest answer is a new operator rather than a bypass. If the reversal problem turns out to be resistance rather than the beam, that is <a href="../garage-door-opener-repair/">an opener fault</a>; if the door reverses only in the last inches of travel, start instead with the sequence on <a href="../garage-door-wont-close/">why a door refuses to close</a>. Either way the beam is checked, cleaned and measured before any force setting is touched.</p>

## svc_garage_door_tune_up_lede

A tune-up is a set of measurements and a short list of adjustments, not a can of spray. Done on a schedule it catches the parts that fail expensively: cables, bearings, the counterbalance and the reversal system.

## svc_garage_door_tune_up_body

<h2>The checklist is published, and it takes about fifteen minutes</h2>
<p>Homeowners are often surprised that the inspection has a written form. The garage door industry publishes a checklist for home inspectors and consumers that takes roughly fifteen minutes and needs one prop: a solid object an inch and a half high, such as a piece of two-by-four at least six inches long. It walks through the reversing test, the door's warning labels, the condition of the hardware and the release. It also tells the reader to stop on any answer of no and call a trained technician instead of continuing. A professional tune-up starts from that list and adds the work items, which are the ones on <a href="../services/">the services index</a>.</p>
<h2>Balance is the measurement that predicts the next failure</h2>
<p>Disconnect the operator, raise the door to about waist height, and let go. A correctly counterbalanced door stays roughly where it was put. A door that sinks is under-sprung, and the opener has been carrying weight it was not built to carry, which is what burns out a drive gear. A door that climbs is over-sprung and will not stay down without the operator forcing it. Either reading means the counterbalance is out, and no amount of lubrication changes it. This is the single test worth watching the technician perform.</p>
<h2>Cycle life, and why a damp garage shortens it</h2>
<p>Springs are rated in cycles, not years, so the household's habits set the schedule. A door opened eight times a day ages three times faster than one opened twice. Local conditions then apply their own multiplier. The manufacturers' data on spring cycle life instructs the trade to keep springs dry precisely because surface rust starts corrosion pits, and those pits are where fatigue cracks begin and spread, cutting rated life. In a climate with fifty to fifty-five inches of rain across roughly a hundred and twenty days a year, that is not a theoretical warning, and it is why cable and bearing inspection carries as much weight here as spring inspection.</p>
<h2>What actually gets adjusted</h2>
<p>The work list is short and specific: rollers checked and replaced where a wheel is cracked or a bearing is dry, hinges checked at the pin holes, cable inspected strand by strand at the bottom bracket and the drum, drum set screws checked, torsion and end bearings lubricated, the bearing plate fasteners checked, hinge and bracket fasteners set to the correct tightness rather than as tight as the driver will go, the rail plumbed and its hangers checked, and the opener's travel limits and force settings verified after the mechanical work rather than before. The last step is the reversal test with the object on the floor.</p>
<h2>Twice a year, and which two times in Marietta</h2>
<p>Timing matters more here than the calendar interval. One visit in late winter or early spring, before the pollen load arrives and before storm season, catches sensor lenses, dry bearings and worn seals while the weather is mild. The second in autumn, ahead of the fifty to seventy nights a year that fall to or below freezing, is when stiff grease, a hardening bottom seal and a marginal opener battery show themselves. Summers here run thirty to sixty days at or above ninety degrees, and an attached garage on those afternoons is hotter still, which is hard on an opener's electronics and on a door's finish.</p>
<h2>Which house on your street needs it most</h2>
<p>City census figures split Marietta's housing into three eras: thirty-seven percent ahead of 1980, twenty-one percent through 1999, and forty-two percent later. The newest third mostly has sealed nylon rollers and a modern operator, and a tune-up there is genuinely preventive. The middle band is where original springs are now past their rated cycles. The oldest third is where a tune-up regularly turns into a real repair, because extension hardware, worn bearing plates and a jamb that has moved are all common. If the door has also become loud, the sound itself narrows the diagnosis, which is covered under <a href="../noisy-garage-door-repair/">noisy door repair</a>. If the reason for the visit is a sale, a rental turnover or a household with small children, the right product is <a href="../garage-door-safety-inspection/">a documented safety inspection</a> rather than a service call.</p>

## svc_garage_door_weather_seal_replacement_lede

A garage opening is sealed at four separate places, and each one fails on its own schedule. Water, grit, pollen and insects all arrive through whichever of the four has given up first.

## svc_garage_door_weather_seal_replacement_body

<h2>Four seals, and knowing which one you are missing</h2>
<p>The bottom seal, sometimes called the astragal, is the flexible strip held in a retainer along the bottom edge of the door. The stop moulding runs down both jambs and across the header, carrying a flap that touches the outside face of the door. A threshold seal is a separate rubber ramp bonded to the slab, forming the lower half of a seal against the door's own bottom strip. Between sections, on an insulated door, there are section joint seals that are part of the panel, not a field-replaceable part. Diagnose from daylight: light along the floor is the bottom seal or an uneven slab, light down a side is stop moulding, and light between sections on a door that is otherwise sound often means the door itself is finished. The other repair paths are on <a href="../services/">the services index</a>.</p>
<h2>Fifty inches of rain, and where it goes</h2>
<p>Rain totals here run fifty to fifty-five inches across roughly a hundred and twenty days a year, and a garage takes that water at the one edge that is closest to grade. On the slab-on-grade houses that make up much of the Westside Subdivision and the newer streets, the finished floor sits close to the driveway, so surface water arrives at the door line rather than below it. A hardened bottom seal that no longer conforms to the slab lets a thin sheet under the door with every storm, and that water sits at the bottom bracket, the roller stem and the bottom section's lower edge. Every corroded bottom fixture a technician cuts out started as a seal that was three years past replacement.</p>
<h2>The insect argument for closing the gap</h2>
<p>There is a second reason to care about a half-inch gap at the slab. The eastern subterranean termite, Reticulitermes flavipes, is the most common house-infesting termite in the United States, and university extension guidance describes swarmers appearing most often in spring and colonies using shelter tubes to travel from soil into a structure. A garage with a persistent gap at the slab, damp sills and stored cardboard is a convenient route inward. Sealing the opening properly is not termite control, and extension entomologists are direct that termite control is not a do-it-yourself project and that annual inspection is the right practice. What a door technician can do is remove the standing moisture and the easy access, and say plainly when the wood at the jamb needs somebody else's expertise.</p>
<h2>The retainer decides the part, not the rubber</h2>
<p>Bottom seals are not interchangeable. The retainer along the door's bottom edge takes a specific profile: a single or double T-end, a bead, or a slotted U-channel, and the strip has to match both the retainer and the door width. Guessing produces a seal that pulls out of one end within a season. The retainer itself is often the real fault on an older door, corroded thin or crushed where a vehicle caught it, and replacing the retainer with the seal is the repair that lasts. On a wood door, the fasteners holding that retainer pass into the bottom rail, which is the wettest wood on the building, and the industry's wood door guidance specifically asks for inspection of the wood at every hardware attachment point.</p>
<h2>Thresholds, sloped slabs and what a seal cannot fix</h2>
<p>A threshold seal bonded to the slab is the right answer to a floor that has settled at one corner or a driveway pitching slightly toward the garage, and it also keeps blown leaves and pollen out. It is not a solution to a garage that takes flowing water in a heavy storm, because a ramp bonded across the opening will also hold water inside once it is past. Where runoff volume is the real problem the fix is outside the door: regrading, a drain, or extending downspouts away from the apron.</p>
<h2>What replacement day looks like</h2>
<p>Seal work is short and mostly not disruptive. The door is opened, the old strip and retainer come off, the bottom edge is cleaned and inspected, the new retainer is fastened, and the strip is fed in with a lubricant so it does not tear. Stop moulding is replaced in full lengths and nailed to the jamb, not to a previous layer of moulding. Then the door is closed onto a strip of paper at several points to check contact across the width. If the seal has been failing long enough that the bottom brackets or hinges have corroded, that becomes <a href="../garage-door-hinge-repair/">hinge and bracket work</a>, and if the reason for the visit is heat or cold in the garage rather than water, the seals are only the first half of the answer, with the rest under <a href="../garage-door-insulation/">door insulation</a>.</p>

## svc_garage_door_wont_close_lede

A door that opens normally and will not stay down is one of two things: a safety system doing its job, or a mechanical fault the operator is correctly refusing to force. The order you check them in decides how long the diagnosis takes.

## svc_garage_door_wont_close_body

<h2>Check the free things first, in this order</h2>
<p>Before anything is unbolted, work through four items. Confirm the slide lock or T-handle on the door is not engaged, since a lock left thrown is the single most common cause and the one that damages a section when the opener pulls against it. Check the vacation or lock switch on the wall control, which disables remotes and looks identical to a dead receiver. Look for a bag, a bin or a hose lying across the beam path. Then watch the door itself rather than the opener, because what happens in the last foot of travel names the fault. The other repair categories that these steps rule out are listed on <a href="../services/">the services index</a>.</p>
<h2>Reversal within two seconds is a legal requirement</h2>
<p>Federal rule 16 CFR 1211 requires a residential door moving downward to begin reversing within two seconds of contacting an obstruction and then return to fully open. That is why the behavior looks so decisive: the door does not hesitate or grind, it simply goes back up. An operator doing this is reporting a condition, not failing. So the useful question is what it is sensing, and there are only three broad answers: the beam is interrupted, the travel limit is set past the floor, or the door is meeting real resistance on the way down.</p>
<h2>Limits, and a floor that is not where the opener thinks it is</h2>
<p>Every operator stores a close limit, and if that limit sits below the slab the door lands, keeps driving, senses resistance and reverses. This is what happens after somebody adjusts a bottom seal, replaces a door, or repairs a slab edge. The tell is a door that reverses only after it touches the ground, with a slight bump at the bottom. It is a setting on the head, not a part, and it takes minutes. A door that instead stops one to two feet up and reverses cleanly is nearly always the beam.</p>
<h2>Sloped lots, standing water and a section that swelled</h2>
<p>Where this becomes local is at the threshold. Frasier Park and the older streets around it are graded lots on Piedmont clay, and a garage sitting at the low end of a drive takes the runoff from fifty to fifty-five inches of annual rain. The bottom section absorbs that. A wood or wood-clad bottom rail swells against the jamb, a rubber seal grows sticky with grit, and the door meets resistance in the final inches, which the operator reads exactly as an obstruction. In winter the same threshold gives trouble differently: with fifty to seventy nights a year at or below freezing here, water in the seal profile can freeze it to the slab overnight, and the operator's reversal is the only thing preventing a torn seal.</p>
<h2>Frayed cable, loose spring, unequal lift</h2>
<p>Two mechanical faults produce a reversal with no obstruction in sight. A lift cable that has begun to unwind at the drum lets one side drop faster than the other, so the door racks in the opening and binds at the rail. A weakening spring changes the balance, and the door becomes heavy enough on the down cycle to drag. Both are visible: loose cable coiled at a jamb, a gap in a torsion coil above the header, or a door that will not hold at waist height with the operator disconnected. If any of that appears, stop and treat it as <a href="../garage-door-spring-repair/">a counterbalance repair</a>.</p>
<h2>What not to do, and what a visit covers</h2>
<p>Do not raise the close force setting to push the door through. That is the safety margin that caught the fault, and spending it makes an obstruction case out of a maintenance case. Do not hold the wall button down to force the close on a door that is binding. A visit starts with the beam and the brackets, which are covered in detail under <a href="../garage-door-sensor-repair/">photo-eye repair</a>, then the limits, then the door off the operator and lifted by hand to find the resistance directly. Nothing about the force setting changes until the mechanical cause is named.</p>

## svc_noisy_garage_door_repair_lede

A door that has become loud is a door telling you which part is wearing, and the sound narrows it down before anyone climbs a ladder. Grinding, popping, squealing and banging each come from a different component.

## svc_noisy_garage_door_repair_body

<h2>Listen from inside, once up and once down</h2>
<p>Stand in the garage, run the door through a full cycle in each direction, and note where in the travel the noise happens. A sound that repeats at the same point every cycle is a worn part passing a fixed feature, usually the curve of the rail. A sound that grows through the whole run is a bearing or a roller. A single event at the top or bottom is limits, cable slack or the stop. Noise is one of the few complaints that can be sorted from the floor, which is why it belongs on its own page rather than buried in the general repairs on <a href="../services/">the services index</a>.</p>
<h2>Grinding and rumble: rollers with dry bearings</h2>
<p>Most doors here run steel rollers with an open bearing race, or nylon rollers on a steel stem. Grit works into the race, the grease it was packed with dries out, and the wheel skids along the rail instead of turning. That is the source of the deep rumble you hear two rooms away. A cracked nylon tire makes the same noise with a click added at one point per revolution. Rollers are inexpensive and are replaced as a set, and the stem length and wheel diameter have to match what the door was built for; the industry's guidance on substituting components is blunt that visual similarity does not establish equivalence.</p>
<h2>Sharp pops at each joint: hinges and worn holes</h2>
<p>A pop as each joint passes the curve is metal moving in an oversized hole. The hinge leaf and the pin wear an oval where they should be round, and the section then shifts a fraction under load with an audible snap. Look at the numbered hinges along the center stile: elongated holes, shiny witness marks, or a hairline crack across the leaf all mean replacement, not lubrication. Hinge noise that is accompanied by a cracked section end is <a href="../garage-door-hinge-repair/">structural rather than acoustic</a> and gets treated that way.</p>
<h2>A squeal above the header: torsion tube bearings</h2>
<p>Three bearings usually carry the torsion tube, one at each end plate and one at the center bracket. When one runs dry it squeals under load and the sound seems to come from everywhere. Humid air is what accelerates this: surface rust in a bearing race grinds a groove, and a bearing that has begun to seize also drags on the counterbalance, which the opener then reads as extra weight. A bearing is a cheap part with the door's whole tension standing behind it, so the tension comes off before it is touched.</p>
<h2>In an older Marietta house the noise arrives in the bedroom</h2>
<p>Census figures for the city put thirty-seven percent of the housing stock ahead of 1980, and those houses were built without much thought for what a garage sounds like from the room next door. In the North Marietta Neighborhood and the streets inside the Church-Cherokee district, the garage frequently shares a wall or a floor with living space, and the ceiling is often the underside of a bedroom. Three changes make a measurable difference there: sealed nylon rollers instead of steel, a belt drive instead of a chain, and isolating the rail hangers from the framing with rubber grommets so the structure stops acting as a sounding board.</p>
<h2>What to lubricate, and what to leave dry</h2>
<p>The rail is not a lubrication point. Grease on the rail collects grit and turns into a paste that wears rollers faster than a dry rail ever would. What wants a light application of a garage door lubricant is the roller stems and bearings, the hinge pins, the torsion bearings, the end bearing plates and the opener chain. Water-displacing penetrating spray is a solvent, not a lubricant; it washes out what is left and evaporates. If the noise turns out to be everything at once, which is common on a door that has never been serviced, the sensible purchase is <a href="../garage-door-tune-up/">a full tune-up</a> with the hardware inspected and the balance measured rather than four separate visits.</p>

## svc_smart_garage_door_opener_lede

Phone control is a regulated feature, not a convenience app. Closing a garage door when nobody is standing there triggers a specific set of federal requirements, and one common type of Marietta garage door is excluded from it entirely.

## svc_smart_garage_door_opener_body

<h2>Unattended operation has a rule of its own</h2>
<p>The relevant federal text is 16 CFR 1211.14, and it is worth knowing before shopping. A system may close a door with no one present only if it requires an intentional action to enable that mode. Before any unattended closing movement it must sound and show an alarm for at least five seconds. The audible signal has to fall between 700 and 3400 hertz and produce at least 45 decibels measured ten feet in front of the device. The visual signal has to be at least a 40 watt incandescent lamp or 360 lumens, flashing at least once per second. And activating any user control during that warning period cancels the movement. A compliant system is loud and slow on purpose. The rest of the work on a door is listed on <a href="../services/">the services index</a>.</p>
<h2>One-piece and swinging doors are excluded</h2>
<p>This is the detail that ends the conversation on some Marietta houses. The same rule does not permit unattended operation on one-piece or swinging doors at all. Plenty of pre-1980 garages in this city, and thirty-seven percent of the housing stock predates that year, still carry a single rigid slab that tilts out and up on pivot hardware, or a pair of hinged doors that swing outward on a carriage-house opening. On those, phone-initiated closing is not a product that can be legally supplied, and any installer offering it is either replacing the door or ignoring the standard. The honest options are a controller that only reports position and opens, or a conversion to a sectional door.</p>
<h2>Where the signal actually has to reach</h2>
<p>The second practical problem is radio, and it is not the same problem as a remote. A controller needs a usable wireless network at the ceiling of the garage, and on the older streets the garage sits at the end of a long drive behind the house, which is exactly the position where a household router does not reach. Measure before buying. The workable answers are a wired connection out to the garage, or an access point mounted in the house nearest the garage rather than a repeater chained to a weak signal. Where a data or coaxial cable is run for that purpose, Marietta has a separate low-voltage permit application covering telephone, cable and security wiring.</p>
<h2>What the feature set is genuinely worth</h2>
<p>Two functions justify the purchase. The first is verification: an app that answers the question of whether the door is shut, from anywhere, which is the failure mode that actually costs households money in this city during storm season. The second is notification, including an alert that the door has been open for a set period, and a log of when it moved. Camera and voice-assistant integration are conveniences. What no controller does is make an unbalanced door safe, and a phone that reports a door closed while a bottom seal is being crushed has reported a fault as a success.</p>
<h2>Retrofit controller against a new head</h2>
<p>An add-on controller wired to a compliant modern operator is the cheaper path, and it works well where the head is recent and healthy. Two conditions rule it out: an operator without functioning photoelectric protection, which should not be given remote closing under any circumstances, and a head old enough that no controller lists it as compatible. In those cases the correct product is <a href="../garage-door-opener-installation/">a new operator</a> specified with the smart feature included and set up on site. Where the goal is simply that a phone replaces a lost remote or a dead keypad, the shorter and cheaper visit is <a href="../garage-door-remote-programming/">remote and keypad work</a>.</p>
<h2>What should happen at the end of the installation</h2>
<p>Ask for three things to be demonstrated before the technician leaves. The five-second audible and visual warning, actually heard and seen from inside the garage. The cancel behavior, proved by pressing the wall control during that warning and watching the movement abort. And the reversal test with an object on the floor, because unattended closing is only as safe as the entrapment protection under it. Then set up who has access on the account, and remove any installer credential, since app access to a garage door is the same category of thing as a key.</p>

## svc_torsion_spring_conversion_lede

Extension springs are still working on a large share of this city's older garages. Converting a door to a torsion shaft above the header changes how the weight is carried, how the door behaves when a spring fails, and how long the hardware lasts.

## svc_torsion_spring_conversion_body

<h2>What is above your rails right now</h2>
<p>Look along the ceiling beside the horizontal rails. A pair of long coiled springs stretched back from the front of the garage, one on each side, is an extension system: each spring is anchored to the front framing and pulls on a cable and pulley to lift its half of the door. A single spring, or a pair, wound on a steel shaft above the opening is a torsion system. City census figures put thirty-seven percent of Marietta housing ahead of 1980, and those garages are where extension hardware still predominates. Which system you have determines nearly everything about what a repair costs and how it is done; the categories are laid out on <a href="../services/">the services index</a>.</p>
<h2>Why the conversion is worth considering</h2>
<p>Three reasons, in order of weight. A torsion system holds the door with the shaft and cable drums rather than with two long springs under tension across the ceiling, so a failure is contained instead of releasing a spring across the garage. The inspection checklist the trade publishes asks specifically whether extension springs have a containment cable running through the center of each spring, and on a great many original installations the answer is no. Second, a torsion door is easier to balance accurately, which is what protects the operator. Third, torsion springs are available in high-cycle ratings, so the replacement interval can be chosen rather than accepted.</p>
<h2>Headroom is the first measurement, not the last</h2>
<p>A conventional torsion conversion needs roughly twelve inches of clear space between the top of the opening and the ceiling for the shaft, the drums and the bearing plates. Older single-bay garages frequently do not have it, which is where people are told a conversion is impossible. It usually is not. Low-headroom torsion hardware moves the shaft configuration and the drums, and a rear-mount arrangement places the shaft at the back of the horizontal rails. Both are real products with their own hardware sets and their own labor. What decides between them is measured on site: headroom, backroom, the depth of the header, and whether anything is already occupying the ceiling.</p>
<h2>The framing has to be able to hold it</h2>
<p>A torsion system concentrates the whole counterbalance load into the header above the door and the two end bearing plates. The industry's mounting guidance is explicit that hardware must be fastened to surfaces capable of withstanding fastener and spring-tension forces, that installers should inspect jambs, headers and ceiling structures particularly on retrofit work, and that mounting into drywall is not acceptable, with the drywall cut away or a wood buck secured to the building instead. On an older garage that often means adding a proper header plate or blocking before any hardware goes up. It is a real line item and it is not optional.</p>
<h2>One-piece doors are a different project</h2>
<p>Some pre-1980 garages here still carry a single rigid door that tilts outward and up on pivot arms, and a conversion in that case is not a spring job at all: the door itself is replaced with a sectional door and then given a torsion system. Two facts are worth knowing before starting. The federal operator rule does not permit unattended, phone-initiated closing on one-piece or swinging doors, so a one-piece door cannot be made into a smart door. And because the whole assembly changes, this is the point at which the opening, the jambs and the header all get measured properly rather than worked around.</p>
<h2>What the work includes, and what to ask for in writing</h2>
<p>A conversion is a specified job, not a parts swap. The scope should name the shaft diameter and length, the spring wire size, inside diameter and length, the cycle rating you are paying for, the drum model matched to door height, the bearing plates and center bearing, new lift cables, the removal of the extension springs, pulleys and their anchors, and any header or jamb reinforcement. The door should be weighed rather than estimated, since the spring selection follows the actual weight. Ask to see the balance test at the end, described under <a href="../garage-door-spring-repair/">spring repair</a>, and if the reason for converting was a household with children or a pre-sale concern, the documented version of that check is <a href="../garage-door-safety-inspection/">a safety inspection</a>.</p>

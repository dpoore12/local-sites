## symptom_1_title

A fatal collision left more questions than answers

## symptom_1

A fatal crash case is not just an insurance claim with a larger loss. It begins with finding out what evidence still exists and who has it. The collision report is important, but it is not the whole investigation. There may be scene photographs, 911 recordings, dispatch logs, nearby camera footage, vehicle data, witness contacts, toxicology results, and records showing whether a commercial driver was working at the time. On Valley roads and Fresno streets, that can also mean looking beyond the two vehicles involved: a farm labor transport vehicle, a delivery route, a company-owned truck, a defective part, or a roadway condition may change who needs to be examined.

The family’s immediate job is smaller and more important than trying to solve the case. Keep every letter, claim number, photograph, and receipt in one place. Do not repair or dispose of a vehicle without first getting advice about preserving it. Write down what each witness told you while names and details are still available. If an insurer asks for a recorded statement or sends paperwork that seems routine, it is reasonable to pause. A release can end claims before the full loss is known.

California also separates the claims related to a death. A wrongful death claim addresses losses suffered by eligible relatives. A survival action concerns a claim that belonged to the person who died and is pursued by the personal representative or, in some circumstances, a successor in interest. That distinction can affect documents, claimants, and damages. The point of early legal work is to identify the right claims before evidence or options are lost, not to force the family into a quick decision.

## symptom_2_title

The death happened at a farm, plant, yard, or other jobsite

## symptom_2

A workplace death can involve more than one system at once. There may be an employer, a staffing company, a property owner, a contractor, an equipment manufacturer, a workers’ compensation carrier, and a state safety investigation. The family does not need to sort out every relationship alone in the first days. What matters first is preserving the record of where the person was working, what task was underway, what machine or vehicle was involved, and who saw it. Save pay stubs, schedules, work messages, identification badges, photographs, incident paperwork, and contact information for coworkers. Do not assume the employer’s report is the only version of events.

This matters in Fresno County. Agriculture and food production are a major part of the local work landscape, alongside packing, processing, construction, warehouses, and transportation. A fatal event in an orchard, field, processing facility, or road-side work area may raise questions about training, equipment guarding, vehicle use, heat protections, staffing, supervision, or a third party’s conduct. The workers’ compensation system may provide benefits, but it does not automatically answer whether another person or company caused or contributed to the death.

California requires an employer to report a work-connected employee death to Cal/OSHA immediately, generally no later than eight hours after it knew or should have known, absent limited circumstances. That report can matter. Ask whether it was made, which Cal/OSHA office received it, and whether an inspection or citation record exists. It is not a finding that someone is legally responsible. It is one piece of a much larger record. The family’s concern should be keeping that record available before equipment is repaired, a site changes, or witnesses move on.

## symptom_3_title

A death followed treatment, surgery, or a delayed diagnosis

## symptom_3

When a death follows medical care, it can be difficult to know whether a bad outcome was unavoidable, a known risk, or the result of care that fell below the required standard. Grief makes that uncertainty harder, and a hospital record can be long, technical, and incomplete without context. A careful review does not begin by accusing a doctor or a hospital. It begins by building an accurate timeline: what symptoms appeared, when help was sought, what was reported, what tests were ordered, what treatment was given, who made decisions, and when the condition changed.

Keep discharge papers, prescription information, appointment summaries, portal messages, bills, and any notes the family made at the time. Request the full records from every facility involved rather than relying only on a short after-visit summary. That can include emergency medical services, urgent care, a hospital, a specialist, a skilled-nursing facility, imaging providers, and follow-up care. The death certificate may be important, but it often does not answer every causation question. An appropriate review may require records from before the final hospitalization as well.

The two California claim paths matter here too. A wrongful death claim is about the losses of people the law recognizes as claimants. A survival action is the decedent’s own claim carried forward by the proper representative or successor. Under California’s general survival-damages rule, pain, suffering, and disfigurement are not included, though statutory exceptions can apply to particular filings. Those are technical questions with real consequences. They should be checked against the dates and facts of the case rather than assumed from an online article or an insurer’s summary.

Do not let the need for answers turn into a rushed narrative. Preserve the records, write down the questions, and allow a complete review to separate a tragic complication from conduct that may support a claim.

## symptom_4_title

The family is unsure who can make a claim or what losses count

## symptom_4

California does not use one simple label for everyone affected by a death. Under Code of Civil Procedure section 377.60, a wrongful death action may be brought by a surviving spouse, domestic partner, children, and certain other people identified by the statute. If there are no surviving children or other issue, the law can look to people who would inherit under intestate succession. Some dependent relatives and household minors may also qualify under particular conditions. That means a person can be devastated by the death and still need a careful legal review before being named as a claimant. Family structure, dependency, and estate status should be documented rather than guessed.

The losses are also personal. They can include the financial support and household services the person provided, as well as the loss of love, companionship, comfort, care, assistance, protection, affection, society, and moral support. The proper proof is usually ordinary life evidence: tax returns, pay records, household budgets, school schedules, calendars, texts, photographs, caregiving notes, and testimony from people who knew the family. It is not a contest to demonstrate grief. It is the work of showing what changed in the family’s daily life.

Then there may be a separate survival action. That claim is brought on behalf of the person who died, through a personal representative or, if there is none, a successor in interest under California law. It is different from the heirs’ own loss. Keeping the two paths separate helps prevent basic mistakes about authority, documents, and available damages.

If there is an estate proceeding, a will, a domestic partnership, a blended family, or disagreement among relatives, say so early. It does not mean the matter cannot proceed. It means the legal work needs to identify each person’s role correctly before a filing, settlement discussion, or distribution is put in motion.

## qa_1_question

Who can bring a wrongful death claim in California?

## qa_1_answer

California Code of Civil Procedure section 377.60 names the usual starting group: a surviving spouse, registered domestic partner, children, and the issue of deceased children. When there is no surviving issue, people who would inherit under intestate succession can be included. The statute also has rules for certain dependents, including a putative spouse, stepchildren, parents, and some household minors. The facts matter. A relationship that feels obvious in the family may require legal proof, especially with a blended family, a separated spouse, an estate, or a claimed dependency. The safest approach is to list every close relative and the estate status at the outset rather than deciding informally who is “the” claimant.

## qa_2_question

What is the difference between wrongful death and a survival action?

## qa_2_answer

They are related but distinct. A wrongful death claim seeks the losses eligible survivors suffer because their family member died. A survival action carries forward a claim the person who died could have brought if they had lived; it is pursued by the personal representative or, if there is none, a successor in interest. California’s general rule limits the damages in a survival action differently from the heirs’ losses and excludes the decedent’s pain, suffering, and disfigurement, subject to statutory exceptions that can turn on dates and procedural facts. That is why it is important not to use the two terms interchangeably or let an insurer treat them as the same claim.

## pricing_lede

California sets no percentage on what a lawyer may take out of a wrongful death recovery, and it protects families instead through what the contract has to disclose and what a judge has to approve, so this page lays out both, who the Code of Civil Procedure lets sue, and what the Fresno County courthouse charges to carry a case to trial.

## pricing_body

### Nothing in California law fixes the number

The starting point is short. Rule 1.5(a) of the California Rules of Professional Conduct forbids an agreement for, a charge of, or collection of an unconscionable or illegal fee, and no statute anywhere in California supplies a percentage ceiling for a wrongful death claim. In practice the percentage on a Fresno family's contract came from the lawyer who drafted it, and the law's answer is not to cap it but to force it into the open and, where a child or a dependent adult is involved, hand it to a judge.

### How unconscionability actually gets tested

Rule 1.5(b) measures the fee against all the facts and circumstances existing when the agreement was entered into, unless the parties contemplated that later events would affect it. The enumerated considerations include whether there was fraud or overreaching in negotiating or setting the fee, whether material facts went undisclosed, the amount of the fee in proportion to the value of the services performed, the relative sophistication of the lawyer and the client, whether the engagement precludes other work, and the amount involved and the results obtained. A grieving family is rarely a sophisticated buyer of legal services, and that asymmetry is itself on the list.

### What the contract has to say out loud

Business and Professions Code section 6147 governs the paperwork. Subdivision (a) requires a written contract signed by both the attorney and the client, or the client's guardian or representative, with a duplicate copy provided at the time it is entered into. It must state the agreed rate, explain how disbursements and costs incurred in prosecuting or settling the claim will affect both the fee and the client's recovery, and disclose any compensation the client might owe for related matters outside the contract. Then, at (a)(4), unless the claim falls under section 6146, the contract must state that no law sets this fee and that its amount is open to negotiation between the two of them. If any of that is missing, subdivision (b) makes the agreement voidable at the client's option and limits the attorney to a reasonable fee.

### The 25 and 33 percent figures belong somewhere else

Those two numbers circulate as though they were general injury law in California. They are not. Section 6146(a) applies only where a person seeks damages against a health care provider based on that provider's alleged professional negligence, and it caps the fee at 25 percent of the amount recovered where the case resolves by settlement and release signed before a civil complaint or arbitration demand is filed, and 33 percent where recovery follows the filing. A death caused by a truck driver, a defective machine, or an unsafe premises in Fresno County is outside that section entirely, and a contract applying those percentages to it has borrowed the wrong statute.

### Who California lets bring the claim

Section 377.60 of the Code of Civil Procedure decides standing, and it is broader than most families expect. Subdivision (a) covers the surviving spouse, domestic partner, children and the issue of deceased children, or, where no issue survives, the people who would take the decedent's property by intestate succession, and it lets legal guardians step in where the parents who could have sued are themselves deceased. Subdivision (b) adds, whether or not they qualify under (a), a dependent putative spouse and that spouse's children, stepchildren, parents, or legal guardians. Subdivision (c) reaches a minor who lived in the household for the 180 days before the death and depended on the decedent for at least half of their support. Any of them may sue, or the personal representative may sue on their behalf, and a contingent percentage attaches to each resulting share rather than to one lump.

### A child's money goes past the judge

Where part of a recovery belongs to a minor or a person with a disability, the fee stops being purely contractual. Rule 7.955(a)(1) requires the court to use a reasonable fee standard in approving attorney fees payable from that money, unless it approved the agreement in advance, and (a)(2) has the court evaluate the agreement on the facts as they stood when it was made. Subdivision (b) gives fourteen nonexclusive factors, including at (b)(9) the informed consent of the minor's representative to the fee and at (b)(13) the risk of loss borne by the attorney, the costs advanced, and the delay in payment. Subdivision (c) requires a declaration addressing the applicable factors. Under rule 7.952(a) the petitioner and the minor both attend the hearing unless excused for good cause, and the court may require the examining physician to testify.

### What the courthouse on O Street costs to use

The fee schedule the Fresno court publishes puts a first paper in an unlimited civil case at $435, and the same amount is due from each other party filing an answer. Summary judgment is $500 under Government Code 70617(d), and a complex designation adds $1,000 for all plaintiffs plus $1,000 per defendant up to $18,000 for the case. The advance jury fee is $150 and nonrefundable. Court reporting runs $30 for a proceeding of an hour or less, then $440 per half day or $880 per day. On the probate side, a petition to compromise a minor's claim where no civil action is pending costs $435 under 70655(c)(1), a petition for appointment of a guardian of the estate is $435, and guardianship of the person alone is $225. Every one of those is a case cost rather than a fee, and the contract should state who fronts them and what happens if the case ends with no recovery.

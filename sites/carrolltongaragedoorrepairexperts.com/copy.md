## symptom_1_title
A sharp snap, then a door too heavy to raise

## symptom_1
A loud snap from the garage followed by a door that barely moves is usually a failed counterbalance spring. The opener has not suddenly become weak; the spring was carrying most of the door’s weight and the motor is now trying to lift the full load. From several feet away, look above the door for a coil with a clear break in it. On an extension-spring arrangement, a loose spring may hang along the horizontal track instead. Do not put hands on either system.

The safest move is simple: keep the door closed if it is already down, unplug the opener, and stop testing the wall button. A door that is partly up can fall as the remaining hardware shifts. Do not pull the red emergency-release handle while the door is open or held crooked. Keep vehicles clear of the threshold until a technician can secure it.

Carrollton’s median housing age points to a lot of doors and springs installed in the 1980s and 1990s. A spring can last for many cycles, but it does not last forever. On a two-spring door, the unbroken spring is the same age and has performed the same work as the broken one. The on-site check should include both springs, lift cables, center bearing, end bearings and drums—not just the obvious split coil. The replacement must be sized to the actual door weight, height, track arrangement and cable drums. Then the door is released from the opener and balanced by hand. It should stay near the position where it is placed rather than sinking or jumping upward. That balance test protects the new spring and prevents an opener from doing work it was never designed to do.

## symptom_2_title
It starts to close, then immediately reverses

## symptom_2
A door that travels toward the floor and turns around is responding to its safety system. Begin at the photo eyes mounted near the bottom of the tracks. Each eye needs a clear view of the other. A trash bin, a garden tool, a cobweb, mud on the lens, or a bumped bracket can interrupt that beam. Wipe the lenses with a soft cloth, remove anything in the opening, and look for an indicator light that is dark or flickering. Do not bypass or tape over the sensors just to make the door close.

If both eyes are clean and aligned, the resistance may be coming from the door itself. Dry rollers, a binding hinge, a bent vertical track, or a spring that is losing its lift makes the opener think it has hit an obstacle. Increasing the closing-force setting can hide that condition for a while, but it removes the warning instead of fixing the drag. A technician checks the door by hand with the opener disconnected, watches every roller through the curve, verifies the track spacing and then tests the reverse function again once the mechanical issue is corrected.

Late-day glare can also confuse exposed sensors, particularly on a west-facing Carrollton garage. A proper diagnosis distinguishes glare or alignment from a stiff door, a damaged sensor wire, or a faulty control board. That matters because each requires a different fix. The visit should end with the door closing smoothly, reversing when it should, and staying within the opener’s force limits—not with the safety feature defeated. Keep children and pets away from the threshold while the fault is present, because repeated testing makes a door with a hidden mechanical bind less predictable.

## symptom_3_title
One side is low, a roller has escaped, or wind bent the track

## symptom_3
A door that leans to one side, has a roller outside the rail, or hangs at an angle needs to be left alone. The lift cables, rollers and spring system normally keep the sections moving squarely. When one cable unwraps from a drum or one roller jumps the track, that geometry changes fast. Pressing the opener again can pull the other side upward, fold a section, bend more track, or bring the full door down. Switch off the opener at the outlet and keep people, cars and stored items outside the path of the door.

Hail can mark a door’s skin; wind and impacts can alter the parts behind it that control movement. The on-site inspection should include both vertical and horizontal tracks, jamb brackets, hinges, cables, bottom fixtures and the struts across wide sections.

Repair begins by securing the door against travel. The technician brings the sections back into line, replaces damaged rollers or hinges, respools cables evenly and corrects or changes bent track where needed. The spring balance is checked afterward because a door can look straight but still be improperly supported. Cosmetic panel damage and mechanical damage are separate questions. If the door runs safely, a panel decision can be made on its own merits. If the track is distorted or a cable is loose, the mechanical repair should happen first. Do not try to pull a cable back onto its drum with household tools; the spring shaft can carry serious stored force.

## symptom_4_title
The opener makes noise but the door does not move

## symptom_4
Motor noise with no movement at the door does not automatically mean a new opener. First look at the rail while another adult presses the button from a safe position. If the chain or belt travels while the door stays put, the trolley may be disconnected from the carriage. That often happens after the emergency-release cord is pulled or caught. With the door fully closed and no damage visible, re-engaging the trolley may be possible by following the opener’s manual. If the door is open, crooked, or unusually heavy, leave the release alone and schedule the repair instead.

A hum, grind, or clicking sound with a motionless chain points somewhere else. Older chain-drive units can wear through an internal nylon gear. A unit that only clicks may have an electrical or control problem, while a motor that runs and then stops can be responding to excess drag in the door. The technician should not diagnose the opener in isolation. The door is disconnected and hand-tested first. A balanced door that rolls smoothly narrows the problem to the operator. A door that is heavy or binding needs its springs, tracks, rollers or hinges corrected before an opener setting or part is changed.

For a Carrollton house with original late-1980s or 1990s equipment, it is common to find an opener nearing the end of its useful support life while the door itself still has years left. Repair is sensible when a stocked component solves the fault and the unit has modern safety equipment. Replacement deserves discussion when parts are unavailable, the housing is compromised, or the older unit lacks current safety features. Either way, the final setup includes travel limits, force settings, photo-eye alignment and a reversal test. The aim is a door that operates safely, not simply a motor that makes noise again.

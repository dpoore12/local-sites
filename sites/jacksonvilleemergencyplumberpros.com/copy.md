## svc_emergency_plumbing_repair_lede

Three unlike failures arrive wearing the same phrase. Water that will not stop, a drain that will not take anything, and taps that have gone dry are all reported as emergency plumbing repair, and the correct first move differs in each case.

## svc_emergency_plumbing_repair_body

### Sorting the call before anybody opens a wall

Clean water under pressure behaves one way: it keeps running with the house empty and quits only when a valve closes. A blockage behaves in reverse, producing water solely while somebody upstairs uses a fixture, then going quiet. Lost supply produces nothing at all and feels like the calmest of the three, which is exactly why a failed service line can soak sandy fill beneath a slab for a week before the pressure drop registers with anyone. Match your house to one of those three descriptions first. The answer settles whether the right move is closing a valve, stopping all water use, or walking out to the meter box.

### Your side of the meter box, and JEA's side

The utility is unusually plain about ownership. Asked who owns the water service line, JEA answers that the property owner does, from the JEA meter box, which is usually located at the property line, to the plumbing inside the structure. Everything downstream of that box is yours to isolate and yours to repair. Wastewater follows the same logic: on a vacuum sewer conversion, JEA has the property owner or a plumber run the gravity pipe from the home toward the JEA connection at the property line, with all pipe and cleanouts installed to the current Florida building code. So the boundary is a physical object sitting in the yard rather than a matter of opinion, and a competent visit establishes which side of it failed before quoting anything.

### The permit line the city draws around a repair

Consolidation makes this simpler here than in most metros, because one Building Inspection Division covers the whole of Duval County and it publishes exactly which plumbing work skips a permit. Stopping a leak is exempt. So is replacing a faucet after the stop, water heaters excluded, and clearing a drain stoppage, but only where the repair does not replace or rearrange valves, pipes or fixtures. Past that line you are in permit territory. City ordinance separately lets a repipe not exceeding $5,000 in total cost proceed without plan review or inspection, and it charges twice the regular fee on work started before the permit was pulled. Anything filed since the start of 2024 is reviewed against the Florida Building Code 8th Edition, in effect since December 31, 2023.

### What a Jacksonville slab hides

Nobody here has a basement floor for a leak to announce itself on. Water leaving a failed line under concrete runs along the underside of the slab and surfaces at a baseboard, a closet corner, or one grout joint, often a room away from the break. Florida's plumbing code then adds a wrinkle unique to termite country: a protective sleeve through a slab-on-grade floor cannot contain cellulose. Where the soil has been treated for subterranean termites, that sleeve is capped at ten thousandths of an inch in wall thickness, sealed within the slab by a noncorrosive clamping device, with no termiticide applied inside it. Details of that kind are what an inspector looks for and what a rushed patch skips.

### What not to do while you wait

Do not wade into standing water to reach a shutoff. Do not put a wrench on a corroded main and pull; a contained leak with a working valve beats an open one with no valve at all. Do not tape a pressurized pipe and turn the water back on to see what happens. Do not chase a stain by cutting drywall or chipping concrete, since the stain marks the end of the water's path and the hole will land in the wrong place. During a backup, keep the household off every fixture, including the ones that seem unrelated.

### The shortcut that earns somebody a second visit

Two habits produce repeat trips. The first is a machine run through the nearest fixture opening rather than a proper cleanout, which shoves the obstruction along the pipe and leaves the same house blocked a month later. Florida's code puts cleanouts at intervals of not more than 100 feet on building drains and on building sewers under eight inches, and requires one at the junction of building drain and building sewer or within ten feet upstream of it. That access usually exists and is worth locating. The second habit is repairing a leak without asking why the pipe failed. HUD's rehabilitation guidance for drainage systems is worth borrowing here: inspect for corrosion, scale and lost wall thickness, because those tell you whether one fitting failed or a whole run is thinning.

### How the appointment runs, and what sets the figure

Arrival begins at the meter box and the main valve, not at the stain. Fixtures get closed and the meter watched for movement, accessible valves and the water heater are checked, the pattern of affected fixtures is tested, and the failure is narrowed to a fitting, a section, a blocked line, or something buried that needs locating before a saw comes out. Scope and figure come before repair work starts. Cost has a broad shape because the work does: a reachable connection is a tight parts-and-labor job, while a located and rerouted supply, a cleared main with a camera survey behind it, or a slab opened and put back all sit at different points. Ask what is included, what is being deferred, and who handles drying and restoration.

## svc_burst_pipe_repair_body

### The meter tells you more than the stain does

A pressurized failure ignores you. Water keeps arriving with every tap closed, overnight, and while the house is empty. Shut every fixture, then go read the meter: movement in the dial means water is leaving your private system somewhere between the box and the last faucet. Other signals worth reporting are a warm patch on tile from a failed hot line, pressure that sagged at every fixture on the same day, and a bill that jumped with nothing about the household changing.

### The build era decides what material you own

Jacksonville hands a plumber something different depending on the street. City preservation guidelines put Riverside's architecture across roughly 1871 to 1935 and Avondale's from 1909 to 1936. Springfield's concentrated development began around 1882 and its strongest growth ran from the early 1880s into the 1920s. San Marco came later, opening in 1925 as an 80-acre development carved into 250 lots. Arlington belongs to another generation entirely, having grown faster than any other part of the city for two decades after the Mathews Bridge opened in 1953, and Mandarin stayed its own political entity until the 1968 consolidation absorbed it. A single address can easily carry three supply materials at once, each one patched in by a different decade's plumber, which is why nobody should quote a repair method sight unseen.

### Fine sand, and a water table close enough to touch

Soil is the local complication. A USGS study of Duval County water resources reports the water table within 5 feet of land surface throughout most of the county, moving as much as 5 feet between wet and dry seasons. Go finer and it climbs. Soil survey descriptions used in Army Corps permitting for Jacksonville sites put the high water table of Leon fine sand at 6 to 18 inches from January through October, and Lynn Haven fine sand at or near the surface across those same months. A trench cut in saturated fine sand slumps almost as fast as it is opened, has to be pumped while somebody works in it, and readily washes soil out from under whatever pipe is already down there.

### Twelve inches of cover, not a frost line

The code number that matters here is not the freezing one. Florida requires exterior water supply piping to sit not less than 6 inches below the frost line and not less than 12 inches below grade, and in this county the second figure does all the work. Twelve inches of sand is the entire margin protecting an irrigation branch, a hose bib feed, or a service run from a shovel, a fence post, a new mailbox, or a truck parked across the side yard. Mention it if digging or heavy equipment preceded the leak, because that changes where a technician looks first.

### The first five minutes

Stay out of standing water while reaching for a valve. Do not force a stuck main; snapping the stem converts a leak you can still close into one you cannot. Leave the drywall and the concrete alone, since the wet patch marks where the water finished, not where it started. Once the supply is off, crack a low faucet to bleed the pressure down, lift rugs and cardboard clear of the floor, and get furniture legs out of the wet before anything wicks up them.

### Spot repair or reroute, decided honestly

One failed fitting in reachable pipe deserves a repair in place. A run that has produced two leaks inside a year, or a line buried in wet sand under finished flooring, is usually cheaper to abandon and reroute through a wall or an attic, which also takes the pipe out of the ground that ruined it. The permit question lands right at that fork. Stopping a leak is exempt work in Duval County, while replacing or rearranging pipe is not, and repiping above $5,000 in total cost picks up plan review as well. Anyone proposing to open a floor should be able to say which of those two categories the job falls into.

### The order of work, and the four things that set the price

The sequence is isolate, verify, locate, then open. Pressure gets read at a hose bib, the meter is watched with the house shut down, hot and cold sides are separated, and the suspect run is traced before anything is cut. Only then should the conversation turn to method, restoration and who patches the floor covering. Four things move the number: how much has to come up to reach the pipe, whether that means sand or tile, whether the answer is a single fitting or a rerouted run, and whether dewatering and shoring belong in the day. Get the locate in writing, with a depth and a distance, before authorizing a saw.

## svc_drain_cleaning_lede

Most blockages announce their own location, provided somebody runs one fixture at a time and watches what else stirs. Skip that and a truck arrives with the wrong machine at the wrong opening, and the house blocks again inside a month.

## svc_drain_cleaning_body

### One fixture, or the whole branch

A lavatory draining slowly while everything else behaves is a local problem: hair at the stopper, or grease resting in the trap arm within a couple of feet of the wall. A tub that fills when a toilet is flushed, a shower that burbles during a wash cycle, or two bathrooms slowing on the same weekend means the restriction sits further out, on a branch or a building drain the two share. Run each fixture on its own and write down what reacts. That short exercise beats any description of how the water looks, because it tells a technician which opening to use and whether a drum machine or a full-size cable is the tool.

### Why an old drain stops passing water

Age is not the diagnosis, and material is not something to assume. What a camera answers is whether the pipe is scaled, cracked or sitting in water. Where the line turns out to be iron, the failure mode is documented. EPA's work on hydrogen sulfide corrosion describes sulfate being reduced to sulfide in the slime layer on the submerged pipe wall, then the gas leaving solution into the sewer atmosphere. Bacteria on the damp upper surfaces oxidize it into sulfuric acid, and corrosion reduces the structural integrity of the pipe crown until backfill washes in through the failure. The same report notes a second mechanism, direct chemical reaction between the gas and metals including iron, and records sewers designed for 50 to 100 years failing in as little as 10 to 20 where those conditions held. HUD's drainage guidance supplies the counterweight worth keeping in mind: age alone does not indicate the condition of a system, and plenty have been found in excellent physical shape after decades of service.

### Where the cleanouts are supposed to be

Access is a code question, not a matter of luck. The Florida plumbing code wants cleanouts on horizontal drainage piping and building drains at intervals of not more than 100 feet, and the same interval on building sewers smaller than eight inches. It also wants a cleanout serving the junction of building drain and building sewer, either at that junction or within ten feet of piping upstream of it. Pulling a toilet does not count as providing that access. On a Duval County lot those openings usually sit along the side of the house or out near the property line, and finding the right one is the difference between clearing a line and merely relocating the trouble along it.

### Wipes, grease, and what the collection system was built for

JEA is direct about the design intent: its wastewater treatment system is meant for human waste and toilet paper only. The utility's list of things to keep out runs to baby wipes, fats, oils and grease, paper towels and tissues, cotton balls, swabs and dental floss, diapers, feminine products and hair, and it warns that wipes sold as flushable do not dissolve the way toilet paper does. Wipes and grease combine into what JEA calls fatbergs, hard masses of non-biodegradable material that back sewage up into homes, yards and streets. Cooking grease is the version that ruins a private line first, congealing on the pipe wall and narrowing the bore a little further with every meal.

### Leave the caustic bottle where it is

Once a fixture is holding water, drain opener never reaches the obstruction. It settles into the pool, warms up, and converts a sink of gray water into a chemical hazard somebody has to neutralize and bail out before a cable can safely go anywhere near it. Plunging a bowl already at the rim achieves the same result on a smaller scale. Boiling water can soften and deform a plastic trap arm. Flushing again to check progress simply relocates the overflow to the lowest opening in the building, which in slab housing is often a hallway shower or a laundry drain.

### The jetter sold without an inspection

The local pattern worth knowing is high-pressure jetting proposed on an old drain with no camera pass first. Water at that pressure inside a pipe whose bottom has already thinned can open a hole rather than clean a wall, and the homeowner then pays for the blockage and the excavation. The mirror image costs just as much: a low advertised clearing price, a camera down the line, and a five-figure replacement proposal at the kitchen table an hour later. Sometimes the pipe really is broken and the camera is telling the truth. Ask for the recording, a written locate with depth and footage, and a stated pipe material before anybody excavates, unless wastewater is actively entering the house.

### Clearing it once, and what that costs

Order of operations decides whether the line stays open. Which fixtures share a path gets confirmed, the correct cleanout is opened, and the cable or jetting head is sized to the pipe instead of to convenience. Once the line is open, the technician loads it with water from several fixtures to prove flow rather than calling it done when the standing water drops. A camera afterward earns its keep on any line that has blocked twice. Cost sits on a wide range for real reasons: a trap-level clog at one lavatory is the floor, a main line reached through an under-slab access is the ceiling, and access, distance and whether a locate is needed move the figure more than the labor hour does.

## svc_sewer_line_repair_lede

Wastewater surfacing in a first-floor tub or a yard cleanout is rarely a fixture fault. It is the buried pipe between the building and the utility connection, and in this county that pipe has sand, groundwater and heavy rain working against it.

## svc_sewer_line_repair_body

### The symptoms that point outdoors

One stopped toilet with everything else fine is a fixture. A sewer problem shows itself through the whole house: the shower filling while a toilet flushes, a floor drain burping during laundry, several fixtures slowing across weeks rather than in one evening, and waste appearing at the lowest opening in the building. In slab housing that lowest opening is generally a hall bathroom or a laundry drain, so the failure lands on finished flooring. Stop all water use the moment you see it, because every gallon the household sends down has nowhere to go but back.

### The pipe in the yard is yours, and here is where it ends

JEA states the water side plainly: the property owner owns the service line from the JEA meter box, usually at the property line, to the plumbing inside the structure. Wastewater is drawn the same way. On a vacuum sewer conversion the utility has the owner or a plumber run the gravity pipe from the home toward the JEA connection at the property line, with pipe and cleanouts installed to the current Florida building code. A JEA inspector witnesses the flushing of the new line before connection is authorized. Chapter 489 of the Florida Statutes then explains why one trade can carry the whole job: a plumbing contractor's scope covers sanitary drainage, storm and sanitary sewer lines, and any excavation work incidental to them, on private and public property alike.

### Sand and groundwater put a low spot in the line

Duval County ground does two things to a buried lateral. It holds water, with USGS placing the county water table within 5 feet of the surface across most of its area and the Jacksonville soil survey putting Leon fine sand's high water table at 6 to 18 inches from January through October. And it moves. Fine sand washes into an open joint and out from under the pipe, leaving a belly where solids settle, and the line then blocks at that same spot every few months. A camera pass that finds standing water partway along the run is usually looking at a sag, not at a clog, and cabling it again treats the wrong thing.

### Rain that overwhelms more than your pipe

The National Weather Service office here puts Jacksonville's annual precipitation normal at 53.40 inches on the 1991 to 2020 record. It does not arrive evenly. Between September 3 and 14 of 2024, flow into JEA's wastewater treatment system rose 58 percent above its 80-million-gallon daily average, and 10 to 15 inches of rain across the city, with upwards of 20 inches in places, produced a dozen separate sanitary sewer overflows in that stretch. JEA lists flooding that submerges manholes for long periods and extreme storm events among the reasons overflows occur at all, and reporting on that September noted that open manhole or cleanout caps add to daily flow and stress the collection system. A backup that follows a storm by a day or two is often this, which is worth saying out loud when you describe the problem.

### Septic is still a live possibility on this address

Do not assume a Duval County property is on sewer. JEA describes roughly 43,000 additional septic systems remaining in the county beyond current phase-out work, and a Florida Water Resources Journal account of the program counts about 65,000 septic systems across JEA's Duval, St. Johns and Nassau service areas, with a 2020 prioritization exercise sorting some 22,000 residential parcels into 35 project areas. A neighborhood only advances once 70 percent of property owners sign on, so conversion is uneven street by street. Meanwhile a full tank, a failed distribution box or a saturated drainfield can look exactly like a blocked lateral from inside the house. Permitting and inspection still run through the county, while Florida moved the governing septic program to the Department of Environmental Protection effective July 1, 2021.

### The backwater valve rule most houses have never heard of

Here is a code provision that decides whether a backup ruins a floor. Where fixtures sit on a floor whose finished elevation is below the elevation of the manhole cover of the next upstream manhole in the public sewer, the Florida plumbing code requires protection. Those fixtures need a backwater valve in the building drain or in the branch serving them, installed so that access is provided to the working parts. Fixtures above that elevation are not supposed to discharge through one, although existing buildings are excepted. On flat ground near the river that comparison is not academic. Ask whether a valve exists, whether anybody can reach it, and when it was last opened.

### Method, permit, and the shape of the cost

A real assessment opens a cleanout, sends a camera out to the connection point, records the pass, and marks the defect on the ground with a depth and a footage. Method follows that footage: an open cut for a short spot repair near the house, lining or bursting from two pits where the run is long, deep, or under a driveway. In wet sand either choice carries dewatering and shoring, and trench work beside a foundation has to stay out of the bearing plane the code draws at 45 degrees down from the outside bottom edge of the footing. Permits belong in the conversation from the first hour, because clearing a stoppage is exempt in Duval County while replacing or rearranging pipe is not. Cost spans an enormous range for honest reasons: length, depth, groundwater, a driveway or street cut, and surface restoration move the total far more than the pipe ever does.

## pricing_lede

This page explains what plumbing emergencies cost in Jacksonville, what the city and JEA publish in permit, tap and inspection fees, and why sand, a shallow water table and mid-century cast iron under a slab shape almost every number on it.

## pricing_body

### The variables that actually set the price

Ask two questions about any plumbing emergency in Duval County and you can usually predict which half of a range it lands in. Is the failed pipe reachable, or under concrete? Is it supply or drainage? A supply leak announces itself, holds pressure and can be isolated at a valve. A drainage failure under a slab announces itself slowly, through a damp spot or a smell, and by then the repair involves flooring, concrete and a schedule.

Hours matter too, though less than people expect. The bigger multiplier is whether the crew works in one trip or has to split the job into demolition, repair, inspection and restoration. Every added trip is mobilization billed again.

### Cast iron, sand and a water table five feet down

Two local facts explain most of the expensive work in this city. The first is drain material in mid-century housing. Where a drain under an older Riverside, Avondale, San Marco or Springfield house turns out to be cast iron, it very often runs beneath a slab, and it cannot be assessed without a camera or replaced without concrete work.

The second is water. A U.S. Geological Survey report puts the water table within five feet of the surface across most of Duval County, with as much as five feet of seasonal swing. That raises the cost of every excavation here: trenches in saturated sand slump and need shoring, water has to be pumped while somebody works, and city inspection guidance requires underground plumbing to be observed before it is buried. Add the septic tank phase-out, where a 2024 city presentation counted roughly 23,000 failing septic tanks across 35 failure areas, and a whole category of work in this market ends in a new sewer connection rather than a repair.

### What the published fees add

The city's schedule is itemized rather than flat. A plumbing permit carries a $60 minimum, and the arithmetic underneath it is $11 per roughed-in fixture or plugged outlet, $11 per building sewer connection, $11 per water service connection, and $11 for each $100 of estimated cost on repairs to water, waste, soil, vent or building drain pipe. Water softeners and solar water heaters are listed at $21. That is small money next to a trench, but it comes with inspection appointments, and on under-slab work an inspection has to happen before concrete goes back.

Utility fees are the ones that surprise people, because they arrive with a septic-to-sewer conversion or a new service rather than with a repair. JEA's tariff lists a six-inch sewer connection at $8,330, a sewer tap extension at $2,894 where an existing tap needs to reach the property line, and a water tap at $1,360 with a $300 meter set for a three-quarter inch service. None of those amounts belongs to the plumbing contractor, and none appears in a national cost estimate.

### How to read the estimate you are handed

A useful estimate answers three questions in writing. What failed, and what is the evidence: camera footage with footage marks and depths, or a leak location and the method used to find it. What is the plan: repair a section, reroute overhead, reline, or replace end to end, and why that choice over the others. What is restoration: concrete replaced to what thickness, flooring put back or left to the homeowner, sod or seed, and who pumps and dewaters the trench. Permits should appear as their own line with the fee stated. Under-slab work proposed without a camera inspection first is a plan built on a guess.

### The trap in this market

Under-slab cast iron is where the pressure selling lives. A single failed section becomes a whole-house repipe proposal, quoted at the kitchen table while a wet spot dries on the floor. Sometimes full replacement really is the right call, and a camera survey of the whole system is what proves it. Ask for footage of every branch, not one clip. Be equally careful with the reverse trap: patching one joint on a line that is visibly failing along its length buys a few months and then charges for the same demolition twice. A third pattern: jetting sold on old cast iron with no inspection, which can open a hole in pipe already thin.

### Why a national average lands low here

An average cost page assumes a repair reached from above, in dry soil, with no permit and no inspection wait. Jacksonville rarely provides that. Missing from those figures: dewatering, shoring in sand, the inspection before backfill, floor covering nobody can match, septic abandonment when a house converts to sewer, and the utility tap fees that arrive with a new connection. The pipe is cheap. Getting to it, and putting the house back, is the invoice.

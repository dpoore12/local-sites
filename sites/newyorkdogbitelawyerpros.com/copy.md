## svc_dog_bite_injury_claim_body

<h2>Two ways to reach a dog owner, and one of them is new</h2>
<p>For nineteen years the only route ran through strict liability. The injured person had to show the owner knew or should have known the animal had vicious propensities, the rule restated in Collier v Zambito. Bard v Jahnke then shut off ordinary negligence for harm caused by a domestic animal. On April 17, 2025 the Court of Appeals decided Flanders v Goodfellow and overruled Bard to the extent it barred negligence liability. Someone bitten in Manhattan can now plead both: knowledge of the animal's propensity, and a failure to use due care in the way it was handled. Older summaries of the law still say otherwise.</p>
<h2>What a vicious propensity looks like in the record</h2>
<p>Flanders is useful for the evidence the court accepted. Postal carriers swore in affidavits that the dog growled, snarled, bared its teeth, slammed into windows and tried to bite them through the glass. None of that is a bite, and the court still found a triable question about constructive knowledge. Collier describes the propensity as a tendency to do any act that might endanger others. So the file wants the small facts: the neighbor who started crossing the street, the muzzle that appeared, the note taped by the mailboxes, the porter who stopped sharing the service elevator.</p>
<h2>Section 123 pays the hospital and stops there</h2>
<p>Agriculture and Markets Law 123(10) makes the owner or lawful custodian of a dangerous dog strictly liable for medical costs resulting from injury that dog caused. That is the entire reach of the provision. Section 123(2) is a menu of what a judge may order once a dog has been adjudicated dangerous. The list includes spaying or neutering, microchipping, muzzling on public premises, and secure confinement. It also covers restraint on a leash by an adult of at least twenty-one, and a liability policy the statute caps at one hundred thousand dollars for injury or death. Lost pay, scarring and pain live outside 123(10) entirely.</p>
<h2>The city call, and the ten days that follow it</h2>
<p>Health Code 11.27(b) requires immediate telephone notice to the Department when a person is bitten by an animal capable of transmitting rabies. The duty falls on the person bitten, a parent or guardian of a minor, whoever owns or controls the biting animal, and anyone else who knows about it. City guidance puts that report inside 24 hours. Section 11.27(e) then requires the owner to observe the animal closely for ten days, which can happen at home, and to report its condition. A healthy dog at day ten is why most bitten people never need the immune globulin and four-dose vaccine series.</p>
<h2>Leash rules, park hours, and why the exact spot matters</h2>
<p>Health Code 161.05(a) allows no restraint longer than six feet in any public place, or in an open or unfenced area abutting one. The Health Code penalty schedule sets $200 for permitting a dog in public unrestrained, and $400 on default. Off-leash time is narrow: 161.05(b)(1) forbids Parks from opening any window that starts before 9:00 p.m. or runs past 9:00 a.m., and off-leash handlers must produce license and rabies proof on demand. In Riverside Park the courtesy hours exclude Sakura Park, the Forever Wild Bird Sanctuary from 116th to 129th, the blocks between 83rd and 91st, and everything below 79th.</p>
<h2>The clocks, including the short one nobody expects</h2>
<p>CPLR 214(5) gives three years from the bite for a personal injury action. That is the ordinary calendar against a private owner or a private building. If a city agency is in the picture, General Municipal Law 50-e(1)(a) requires a notice of claim served within ninety days after the claim arises, and 50-i(1) requires the action itself within one year and ninety days. Those two rules are unforgiving, and nothing about a pending insurance discussion suspends them. Comparative fault under CPLR 1411 reduces a recovery in proportion to the claimant's own conduct rather than wiping it out.</p>
<h2>The habit that quietly thins a strong claim</h2>
<p>The common loss is not a legal argument. It is a name. Health Department data for 2024 shows 39 percent of reported bites came in without a complete owner address or contact phone, which strands the animal's history and the policy behind it. Get the handler's name, apartment and number at the scene if it is safe, photograph the dog, and ask a witness for a way to reach them. Then stop narrating the injury on social feeds, because an adjuster reads that account next to the emergency-room note and works the gaps.</p>

<p>Do not rehearse the account with the child or record repeated retellings; a coached narrative is worth less than an unpolished one. Do not accept a verbal promise from the owner to pay a bill. <h2>Two defendants, two different tests</h2>
<p>The tenant who keeps the dog answers on propensity knowledge, and now, after the 2025 Flanders decision, potentially on negligence in handling as well. A building owner is a separate inquiry. In Agro v Olivieri, decided April 10, 2024, the Second Department restated the standard for a property owner. Three things must be shown: notice that a dog was being harbored on the premises, actual or constructive knowledge that it had vicious propensities, and sufficient control of the premises to remove or confine the animal. All three, or the claim against the owner fails no matter where the attack happened.</p>
<h2>The lease signed with the dog already known</h2>
<p>Strunk v Zoltanski, decided by the Court of Appeals in 1984, is the case behind the control element. A landlord who knows before leasing that the tenant will keep a vicious dog, and rents anyway without taking precautions available through the lease or otherwise, may answer in negligence to a person later bitten on the premises. The duty described there is the ordinary one: avoid exposing third parties to foreseeable, unreasonable harm. Later decisions extend it to a landlord who learns during the term and keeps the power to require removal or confinement.</p>
<h2>Knowledge is still the hinge in a negligence theory</h2>
<p>Some readers assume the veterinary-clinic decision in Hewitt erased the notice requirement for premises defendants. The Second Department rejected that reading in Cantore v Costantine on November 15, 2023, holding the propensity notice requirement was not eliminated for pet-friendly establishments. Summary judgment went to a restaurant that had posted a sign and shown it knew nothing about the dog. The practical lesson for a building case is documentary. What management was told, by whom, in what channel, and on what date is the case.</p>
<h2>Public housing runs on its own rulebook</h2>
<p>Public housing authority rules allow one dog or one cat per household, with registration filed at the management office within 30 days of acquiring the animal. A registered dog's adult weight may not exceed 25 pounds, and Doberman Pinschers, Pit Bulls and Rottweilers are prohibited by breed, with assistance animals exempt from weight and breed limits. Dogs must wear the authority-issued pet tag and stay on a leash of six feet or less in public areas inside and outside the buildings. Pets are barred from laundry rooms, basements, roofs, roof landings, playgrounds and management offices.</p>
<h2>Where these bites actually occur</h2>
<p>Health Department figures for 2024 put 1,176 reported bites, 31 percent of the citywide total, on dogs owned in the same building or household as the person bitten. Another 1,858 reported bites happened outside the victim's own building, and 1,107 involved someone passing by or entering the dog's space. That last category describes hallway and vestibule geometry more than it describes temperament. A narrow entry, a blind turn at a stair landing, and a service corridor shared by a dog walker are physical facts worth photographing.</p>
<h2>The evidence that erases itself</h2>
<p>Send a written preservation request the same week, naming the camera positions, the date and a time window. Ask for the incident report, the porter's log, elevator service records, and any complaint file about that apartment. Pull your own 311 history and any building message thread about barking or lunging. Meanwhile the city's own dangerous-dog track under Health Code 161.07 moves fast: a petition and notice of hearing go out no later than fifteen days after a dog enters a shelter, with the hearing at the administrative trials office within twenty days of mailing.</p>
<h2>Which clock applies to which landlord</h2>
<p>Against a private owner or managing agent, CPLR 214(5) gives three years from the bite. Against a public landlord or another city agency, everything compresses. General Municipal Law 50-e(1)(a) requires a notice of claim within ninety days after the claim arises, and 50-i(1) requires the suit within one year and ninety days of the event. Identify the ownership early, because a deed search takes an afternoon and a missed municipal notice ends the claim. Comparative fault under CPLR 1411 reduces damages proportionally rather than barring them.</p>

## pricing_body

### The rule that controls a Manhattan bite claim

New York County lies within the First Judicial Department, and its Appellate Division has adopted 22 NYCRR 603.25(e). The provision reaches any claim or action for personal injury or wrongful death, resolved by judgment or by settlement, in which the attorney's compensation depends on the outcome, and it excludes claims alleging medical, dental or podiatric malpractice. A fee equal to or below the scheduled amount is deemed fair and reasonable. A fee above it, the rule says, constitutes the exaction of unreasonable and unconscionable compensation in violation of the Rules of Professional Conduct, unless a court has authorized it by written order.

### Schedule A, the graduated ladder

The first of the two options in the rule steps down as the recovery grows. Fifty percent of the first $1,000 recovered. Forty percent of the next $2,000. Thirty-five percent of the next $22,000. Twenty-five percent of any amount above $25,000. Serious bite injuries settle well past that last breakpoint, so the twenty-five percent tier carries most of a substantial recovery and the blended rate falls as the number grows. Run that arithmetic on the figure realistically in play, not on the headline percentage at the top of the ladder.

### Schedule B, the flat alternative

The second option is a percentage not exceeding thirty-three and one third percent of the sum recovered, and it is available only if the initial contractual arrangement provides for it. There is a tradeoff written into the rule. Choosing the flat option removes access to the procedure by which counsel may later apply for additional compensation on grounds of extraordinary circumstances. Under Schedule A that route stays open. Where counsel believes in good faith the scheduled fee is inadequate because of extraordinary circumstances, an application may be made on affidavit to the designated justice. The client and anyone holding a lien get written notice and an opportunity to be heard.

### Net or gross, and the choice the client makes

Subdivision (e)(3) is the part of the rule most likely to change what actually lands in a client's account. The percentage may be computed one of two ways, and the client selects which in the retainer agreement or letter of engagement. One method uses the net sum recovered, after deducting expenses and disbursements for expert testimony and investigative or other services properly chargeable to the claim. The other uses the gross sum before any such deduction, and is available where the attorney has agreed to pay those costs under Judiciary Law 488(2)(d). The agreement must describe both methods, explain the financial consequences of each, and clearly show which one the client picked. Costs as taxed, including interest on a judgment, count as part of the amount recovered. No deduction is made in the computation for a lien or claim in favor of a hospital, for medical care and treatment by doctors and nurses, or of a self-insurer or an insurance carrier.

### The malpractice scale that does not apply here

Judiciary Law 474-a is frequently quoted as though it governed all injury work in New York. It does not. Its sliding scale reaches medical, dental and podiatric malpractice actions only. The cap runs thirty percent of the first $250,000 recovered, twenty-five percent of the next $250,000, twenty percent of the next $500,000, fifteen percent of the next $250,000, and ten percent of anything above $1,250,000. A claim against the owner of a dog that bit someone on a sidewalk in Chelsea is an ordinary personal injury claim, so the First Department schedule applies and this one does not. If a hospital's handling of the wound became its own claim, that separate claim would fall under 474-a.

### A child's claim goes before the court

Judiciary Law 474 governs fee agreements made with the guardian of an infant. Compensation dependent on the success of the child's claim, or set as a percentage of the child's recovery, is invalid and unenforceable unless the agreement was made subject to the court's power to fix the amount. On a recovery, award, compromise or settlement, the attorney applies on notice to the guardian, the court proceeds summarily to determine the value of the services by affidavit, reference or examination of witnesses, and then orders a suitable amount out of the money. Given how many bite victims in the city are children, this is the operative fee mechanism in a large share of cases.

### What the paperwork and the County Clerk cost

Rule 1215.1 of the Rules of the Chief Administrator requires a written letter of engagement before the representation begins, or within a reasonable time after where that is impracticable. It must set out the scope of the services, the fees, expenses and billing practices, and, where it applies, the client's right to fee arbitration under Part 137. A signed retainer covering those subjects satisfies the rule, and a real change in scope calls for an updated letter. On the litigation side, CPLR 8018(a) fixes the index number fee at $190 payable in advance, plus $5 for records management and $15 for the cultural education account, so $210 opens the file. Filing a note of issue costs $125 where no request for judicial intervention was needed. City licensing runs $8.50 a year for a spayed or neutered dog and $34 for an unaltered dog older than four months, with a $2 fine for each year a license went unrenewed.

## svc_animal_care_centers_bite_reports_lede

A bite in New York City generates paperwork in at least three systems: a public health record, a shelter file if the animal is impounded, and a medical chart. Each is created on a different deadline, and each says something a later dispute will ask about.

## svc_animal_care_centers_bite_reports_body

<h2>Reporting is a duty, and it falls on several people at once</h2>
<p>New York City Health Code 11.03(a) lists an animal bite, or an exposure to rabies, among the conditions reportable to the Department. Section 11.27(b) is more specific about who must act: when a person or a susceptible animal is bitten or otherwise exposed, the person bitten or a parent or guardian of a minor, the person who owns or controls the biting animal, the person controlling the animal that was bitten, and any other person having knowledge of the bite must immediately notify the Department by telephone. Department guidance states that any type of animal bite must be reported within twenty-four hours of the event.</p>
<h2>Who reports from the clinical side</h2>
<p>Health Code 11.05 identifies the reporters within the health care system, including physicians, dentists, licensed chiropractors, doctors of osteopathy, physician's assistants, nurse practitioners, the person in charge of a hospital, clinic or institution, and clinical laboratories, along with their designees. Where no such person is in attendance, the head of a private household or the person in charge of an institution must report. City data for 2024 shows why the redundancy matters: 2,947 of 3,814 reported bites, seventy-seven percent, were documented as treated by a medical provider, and at least eleven percent of provider-treated bites, 333 of them, were not reported by a medical provider.</p>
<h2>The ten-day observation and the alternatives</h2>
<p>Health Code 11.27(e) requires the owner of a biting dog, cat, ferret or livestock animal to observe it closely for ten days and to report: an immediate telephone call if the animal dies, with delivery of the remains to the Public Health Laboratory; a call if it becomes ill; an immediate call if it escapes; and a call plus return of the Department-supplied postcard if it is healthy on the final day. Section 11.27(f) provides that an unowned biting animal is held ten days, or humanely destroyed after two days. Section 11.27(d) covers an exposed animal rather than a biting one: unvaccinated animals are isolated six months at the owner's expense under daily veterinary supervision, while vaccinated animals are revaccinated immediately and observed closely for forty-five days.</p>
<h2>What a shelter file contains</h2>
<p>Health Code 161.07(b) requires a shelter to report to the Department no later than the first business day after a dog is admitted, or after a sheltered dog injures a person, with the circumstances of the incident, contact information for injured persons, the owner and witnesses, and the types of injuries. Section 161.07(c) requires ten days of rabies observation at the shelter, extended by another ten days if a new incident occurs, with euthanasia and testing if signs of rabies appear and early release possible where the owner reports daily. Section 161.07(e) makes a failure to produce a dog for behaviorist examination evidence that the dog is dangerous.</p>
<h2>Two different hold periods, from two different sources</h2>
<p>Animal Care Centers of NYC states that shelters are required to hold a stray animal with no identified owner for three days, after which the animal may be adopted, transferred, fostered, sent to rescue or in rare cases euthanized for serious medical or aggression problems, and directs anyone who finds a pet outside business hours, or one that is sick, injured or a threat to public safety, to call 9-1-1. Agriculture and Markets Law 117 sets different statutory periods for seized dogs: five days for an unidentified dog, seven days after personal notice for an identified one, and nine days from mailing, with impoundment fees beginning at not less than ten dollars and rising for each additional twenty-four hours. The two figures come from different authorities and should not be merged.</p>
<h2>Why the report becomes evidence</h2>
<p>Health Code 161.02 gives the paperwork legal weight. A Department record of a bite report made under section 11.03, or a record of a dog bite or other injury made by a police officer, is prima facie evidence that a dog is dangerous within the meaning of that article. The same section defines a dangerous dog as one that menaces, threatens, attacks or bites a person who is peacefully conducting himself where he may lawfully be. Section 161.07(l) adds that a medical provider must forward records of an injury inflicted by a dog when the Department makes a written request.</p>
<h2>The gap the data shows</h2>
<p>Reporting only helps if the animal and its keeper can be identified. City figures for 2024 record that thirty-nine percent of reports were missing a complete owner address or telephone number, and that of 2,314 reports where the Department had a way to contact an owner, only 1,146, about half, produced a response about the dog's health, with 1,500 reports carrying no contact information at all. The age of the person bitten was missing from twenty percent of 2024 reports, against four percent in 2015. A name, an apartment number and a phone number taken at the scene, where it is safe to do so, fills the gap the system leaves.</p>

## svc_apartment_and_hallway_attacks_lede

Vestibules, stair landings, service corridors and elevator cabs are where a leashed dog and a stranger are forced within a few feet of each other. The geometry of the space is often the most important physical fact in the file, and it is the one nobody photographs.

## svc_apartment_and_hallway_attacks_body

<h2>The city's own figures point at shared space</h2>
<p>Health Department data for 2024 describes where these incidents happen. Of 3,814 reported bites to residents, forty percent occurred away from the victim's own residential building, and among those 1,858 incidents 187 involved a dog that was not leashed while 137 involved a dog that was leashed but not under control. The single largest reported activity category was a person passing by or entering the dog's space, 1,107 incidents, and at least 628 of those took place away from the person's own building. That last figure describes lobbies, corridors and doorways more than it describes temperament.</p>
<h2>Rules that apply specifically to common areas</h2>
<p>Two Health Code provisions operate inside buildings. Section 161.03 makes it a violation to permit an animal to commit a nuisance on a sidewalk, in a common area or in any other public place, and the city penalty schedule sets two hundred dollars for that violation with four hundred on default. Section 161.05(a) requires a leash or other restraint no longer than six feet in any public place and in an open or unfenced area abutting one. Section 161.04(b) separately requires a valid metal license tag on the dog's collar in public, which is often the fastest route to identifying an animal whose handler leaves before anyone takes a name.</p>
<h2>Public housing adds a second rulebook</h2>
<p>In housing authority buildings the pet policy revised in January 2021 governs alongside the Health Code. It permits one dog or one cat per household, caps a dog's adult weight at twenty-five pounds, prohibits Doberman Pinschers, Pit Bulls and Rottweilers by breed while exempting assistance animals from weight and breed limits, and requires registration at the management office within thirty days of acquiring the animal. Dogs must be leashed at six feet or less at all times in public areas inside and outside the buildings, and are barred from playgrounds, community facilities, laundry rooms, basements, barbecue areas, roofs and roof landings. Vicious, threatening or biting animals are not permitted on the property at all.</p>
<h2>Two defendants can occupy one hallway</h2>
<p>An apartment corridor usually involves a keeper and a controller. The keeper is the person who harbors or keeps the dog under Agriculture and Markets Law 108(15). The controller is whoever had sufficient control of the premises to remove or confine the animal, the third element of the property-owner test restated in Agro v Olivieri on April 10, 2024, alongside notice of harboring and knowledge of a vicious propensity. In a co-op the corporation holds title while a shareholder holds a proprietary lease; in a condominium the board governs common elements and the unit owner holds the deed. Which entity had the power to act determines which claim is worth pleading.</p>
<h2>The measurements that matter later</h2>
<p>A corridor case is won on dimensions and sight lines. The width of the vestibule, the position of the mailboxes, whether the inner door swings inward, the blind turn at a stair landing, the depth of an elevator cab and where the dog was standing in it are all facts that stop being available once furniture moves or a lobby is renovated. Photographs taken the same week, with a tape measure visible, do work that testimony cannot. Note the lighting too, because a dim service corridor and a bright lobby produce different accounts of how much warning anyone had.</p>
<h2>Building records disappear on their own schedule</h2>
<p>Camera coverage in New York residential buildings is frequently overwritten within days. A written preservation demand naming camera positions, the date and a time window, sent immediately, is the single most useful piece of paper in a hallway case. Ask separately for the incident report, the porter's log for the shift, elevator service records, and any complaint file concerning the apartment. Pull the 311 service-request history for the address. In a housing authority building, complaints about unleashed dogs run through the authority's customer contact center rather than the city's ordinary channel.</p>
<h2>The deadline depends on who owns the hallway</h2>
<p>Against a private landlord, managing agent or co-op corporation, CPLR 214(5) allows three years from the date of the bite. Against a public housing authority, General Municipal Law 50-e(1)(a) requires a notice of claim within ninety days after the claim accrues, and 50-i(1) requires suit within one year and ninety days. The complaint must also allege that thirty days have elapsed since the notice was served without adjustment. Comparative fault under CPLR 1411 reduces damages in proportion to a claimant's own culpable conduct rather than barring recovery outright.</p>

## svc_child_bite_injuries_lede

Most children are bitten in familiar rooms by animals they already know. That changes the evidence problem more than the legal one: the adults involved are related, embarrassed or both, and the facts that decide the case get discussed rather than written down.

## svc_child_bite_injuries_body

<h2>The 2024 numbers describe household dogs</h2>
<p>Of the 3,814 bites reported to city residents in 2024, 1,176 involved a dog owned in the same building or household, and 783 involved a dog living in the same household as the person bitten. Ninety-nine reported bites followed food or the taking of a toy or other object, and fifty-four percent of that group were household dogs. Handling the animal preceded nine percent of reported bites and playing with it preceded eight percent. Those categories describe ordinary domestic scenes, which is exactly why a claim built on them needs contemporaneous records rather than reconstruction.</p>
<h2>A severity threshold with a legal definition</h2>
<p>New York City Health Code 161.02 defines severe injury as physical injury inflicted by a dog that causes at least a broken bone or a disfiguring laceration requiring either multiple stitches or cosmetic surgery. Under 161.07(f) a dog that caused severe injury remains in the shelter until an administrative law judge reports that it is not dangerous. On the state side, Agriculture and Markets Law 108(29) defines serious physical injury to include injury creating a substantial risk of death and injury causing serious or protracted disfigurement, protracted impairment of health, or protracted loss or impairment of the function of a bodily organ. Both definitions turn on findings a treating physician can record in the chart, and neither is satisfied by an adjective.</p>
<h2>Rabies decisions run on a ten-day clock</h2>
<p>Health Code 11.27(e) requires the owner of a biting dog to observe the animal closely for ten days and report its condition, including an immediate telephone call if it dies, becomes ill or escapes. A healthy animal at the end of that period is usually why a child never begins post-exposure prophylaxis. In 2024, 433 bitten residents received that treatment, eleven percent of reported cases, up from seven percent and 245 people in 2015. City data also records that no rabid dog has been found in New York City in more than sixty years. Getting the owner's name and phone number at the scene is therefore a medical step as much as a legal one.</p>
<h2>Documenting an injury on a growing child</h2>
<p>Children heal onto a moving target, and a claim closed early cannot be reopened because a scar widened as the child grew. Ask the treating provider to record location, depth, the method of repair, and any referral for hand therapy, plastic surgery or behavioral follow-up. Keep the discharge instructions, the prescriptions and the original photographs rather than edited copies. CPLR 3043(a) lists the items a defendant is entitled to demand in a bill of particulars, including a statement of injuries and whether they are permanent, time confined to bed and to the house, and time lost from employment, so those categories are worth tracking from the first week for the child and for the parent who missed shifts.</p>
<h2>The deadline is longer, and that is a trap</h2>
<p>CPLR 214(5) sets three years for a personal injury action, and CPLR 208(a) extends the period where the plaintiff was under the disability of infancy at accrual, allowing the action to be brought within three years after that disability ends. The section's usual ten-year outside limit does not apply to infancy outside medical, dental and podiatric malpractice. That extra time does not preserve evidence. Witnesses move, buildings change hands and animals are rehomed, and if a city agency is involved the ninety-day notice of claim under General Municipal Law 50-e still governs regardless of the child's age.</p>
<h2>A judge approves the settlement and the fee</h2>
<p>Under CPLR 1207 a guardian or a parent with legal custody applies for court approval of the compromise of a minor's claim, brought as a special proceeding where no action is pending. The rule provides that the order has the effect of a judgment and approves the fee of the infant's attorney. Judiciary Law 474 reinforces the point by making a percentage agreement with an infant's guardian unenforceable unless it was subject to the court's power to fix the amount. Families should expect a court appearance and a written order rather than a private release.</p>
<h2>The offset nobody mentions in the first call</h2>
<p>CPLR 4545 allows a court, after a verdict, to reduce an award for past or future economic loss where the loss was or will be replaced from a collateral source such as insurance, less the premiums paid for the two years before the claim accrued and the projected future cost of those premiums. Voluntary charitable contributions are excluded from the calculation. For a child's claim that means the health-coverage record and the premium history become part of the damages arithmetic, and both are easier to assemble while the treatment is current than three years afterward.</p>

## svc_dangerous_dog_proceedings_lede

A dangerous-dog case is not the injury claim. It is a separate proceeding about the animal, with its own filing route, its own five-day calendar, its own standard of proof and its own penalty schedule, and in New York City a parallel administrative track that moves even faster.

## svc_dangerous_dog_proceedings_body

<h2>What the statute means by a dangerous dog</h2>
<p>Agriculture and Markets Law 108(24) sets three alternative descriptions. The first is a dog that without justification attacks a person, companion animal, farm animal or domestic animal and causes physical injury or death. The second is a dog that behaves in a manner a reasonable person would believe poses a serious and unjustified imminent threat of serious physical injury or death. The third covers a dog that without justification attacks a service, guide or hearing dog and causes physical injury or death. Police work dogs are excluded. Subdivision 28 defines physical injury as impairment of physical condition or substantial pain, and subdivision 29 defines serious physical injury to include protracted disfigurement or protracted loss or impairment of the function of a bodily organ.</p>
<h2>How the proceeding starts and how fast it moves</h2>
<p>Any person may make a complaint about an attack to a dog control officer or police officer, who may then bring a sworn complaint before a municipal judge or justice. On probable cause the court may order the dog seized pending the outcome. Section 123 requires the hearing to be held within five days on not less than two days' written notice to the owner. The petitioner must establish the dog is dangerous by clear and convincing evidence, a higher standard than the preponderance used in a civil injury case, which is one reason an adverse finding is useful in a later claim while the absence of one proves comparatively little.</p>
<h2>The orders a judge may impose</h2>
<p>A dangerous-dog finding carries mandatory neutering and microchipping. Beyond that, section 123 lets the court impose one or more further measures: evaluation by a certified applied behaviorist at the owner's expense with any recommended training, secure humane confinement, restraint in public on a leash held by a person at least twenty-one years old, muzzling when in public, or maintenance of a liability insurance policy the statute caps at one hundred thousand dollars for personal injury or death. Euthanasia or permanent confinement is reserved for aggravating circumstances. A euthanasia order is stayed thirty days, and an appeal is taken by filing a notice of appeal within thirty days.</p>
<h2>Defenses the statute writes into the case</h2>
<p>Section 123 recognizes justification. There is no dangerous-dog finding where the injured person was committing a crime against the owner or a member of the owner's family, was tormenting, abusing or assaulting the dog, or had done so in the past, or where the dog was responding to pain or injury, or was protecting itself, its owner, a member of its household, or its offspring. Those defenses shape the underlying facts a civil file needs. If a claim will later be contested on comparative fault under CPLR 1411, the testimony given at a hearing held within days of the incident will already be on the record.</p>
<h2>Penalties, and what they do to the claim</h2>
<p>The penalty ladder runs by injury and history. An owner who negligently permits a dog to bite and cause physical injury faces a civil penalty of up to four hundred dollars. Where the injury is serious physical injury the penalty rises to fifteen hundred dollars, and the statute allows that amount to be reduced by restitution the owner pays for unreimbursed medical expenses, lost earnings and other damages. Where a dog already adjudicated dangerous is negligently permitted to bite and cause serious physical injury, the offense is a misdemeanor punishable by a fine of up to three thousand dollars, up to ninety days, or both. Killing a person lawfully present is a class A misdemeanor.</p>
<h2>The New York City track, and why it is faster</h2>
<p>Inside the five boroughs the Health Code adds an administrative route. Section 161.07 lets the Department make a preliminary determination that a dog is dangerous, weighing the circumstances, the nature and severity of the injuries and any prior bite history, with a behaviorist assessment when needed. The petition and notice of hearing are mailed no later than fifteen days after the dog enters a shelter, and the hearing at the administrative trials office is scheduled no more than twenty days after mailing. A dog that caused severe injury, defined in 161.02 as at least a broken bone or a disfiguring laceration requiring multiple stitches or cosmetic surgery, stays in the shelter until an administrative law judge reports it is not dangerous. Section 123(12) confirms none of this displaces a civil claim.</p>

## svc_dog_attack_wrongful_death_lede

A death from a dog attack produces two separate claims with different beneficiaries and different measures of damages, both brought by the same court-appointed representative. The deadlines are shorter than in an injury case, and one of them starts with a Surrogate's Court appointment rather than with the attack.

## svc_dog_attack_wrongful_death_body

<h2>Who brings the action</h2>
<p>EPTL 5-4.1 provides that the personal representative of a decedent's estate may maintain an action to recover damages for a wrongful act, neglect or default that caused the death, and requires that the action be commenced within two years of the death. A family member cannot file in an individual capacity; the appointment has to come first, which means the Surrogate's Court process runs in parallel with everything else. Where the decedent left no will, the appointment of an administrator is the step that creates standing, and any delay in obtaining it consumes part of a two-year period.</p>
<h2>Two claims, and the difference between them</h2>
<p>The wrongful death action under EPTL 5-4.1 compensates the distributees for their own losses resulting from the death. A survival action under EPTL 11-3.2 is different: it carries forward the claim the decedent held for the injury suffered before death, including conscious pain and suffering, and it belongs to the estate rather than to the distributees directly. The same section provides that punitive damages are not recoverable against a decedent's personal representative. Both claims are usually pleaded together because they draw on the same facts while distributing recovery under different rules.</p>
<h2>What the statute allows as damages</h2>
<p>EPTL 5-4.3 defines the measure as fair and just compensation for the pecuniary injuries resulting from the decedent's death to the persons for whose benefit the action is brought. It permits recovery of reasonable medical and nursing expenses incident to the fatal injury, and funeral expenses paid by the distributees or for which they are responsible, and it provides for interest from the date of death as part of the total sum awarded. Punitive damages are available where the decedent's death occurred on or after September 1, 1982. The phrase "pecuniary injuries" is the limit that surprises families most, because grief itself is outside it.</p>
<h2>How a recovery is divided</h2>
<p>EPTL 5-4.4 provides that damages recovered are exclusively for the benefit of the decedent's distributees and are distributed to them in proportion to the pecuniary injuries each suffered, with the court determining those proportions after a hearing. A verdict or settlement therefore does not conclude the matter. Where a distributee is a minor, the approval mechanism in CPLR 1207 applies to the compromise of that person's claim, and the rule provides that the order has the effect of a judgment and approves the fee of the infant's attorney.</p>
<h2>The deadlines, including the municipal one</h2>
<p>The two-year period in EPTL 5-4.1 is the outer boundary for the wrongful death claim against a private defendant. Where a municipal entity is a potential defendant, General Municipal Law 50-e(1)(a) requires a notice of claim within ninety days, measured in a wrongful death case from the appointment of the representative, and section 50-i(1) requires the action to be commenced within two years after the death. CPLR 306-b then requires service within one hundred twenty days after commencement where the limitations period is two years or more. Each of those clocks runs independently of any insurance discussion.</p>
<h2>The criminal and administrative consequences for the animal</h2>
<p>Agriculture and Markets Law 123 treats a death differently from an injury. Where a dog previously adjudicated dangerous kills a person who is lawfully present, the owner's violation is a class A misdemeanor, and where such a dog is negligently permitted to bite and cause serious physical injury the offense is a misdemeanor punishable by a fine of up to three thousand dollars, up to ninety days, or both. Section 108(24) includes within the definition of a dangerous dog an animal that attacks without justification and causes death, and 108(29) defines serious physical injury to include injury creating a substantial risk of death. Section 123(12) confirms that none of this displaces the civil claim.</p>
<h2>Evidence in the first weeks</h2>
<p>A fatal case is built from records that are created once. The medical examiner's file, the responding officers' reports, any Health Department bite record, and the shelter file if the animal was impounded all exist on their own timetables, and Health Code 161.07(b) requires a shelter to report to the Department no later than the first business day after a dog is admitted or after a sheltered dog injures a person. Where the attack occurred in or around a building, preservation of camera recordings has to be demanded immediately. Under CPLR 4545 a court may reduce an award for economic loss replaced from a collateral source, less premiums paid for the two years preceding accrual, so the insurance and benefits record belongs in the file as well.</p>

## svc_dog_walker_and_handler_liability_lede

The person holding the leash is often not the person who owns the dog. New York's animal statute defines a keeper broadly enough to reach a walker, and the negligence route opened in 2025 reaches how an animal was handled rather than what anyone knew about it beforehand.

## svc_dog_walker_and_handler_liability_body

<h2>The statutory definition reaches beyond the owner of record</h2>
<p>Agriculture and Markets Law 108(15) defines an owner as any person who harbors or keeps any dog, and 108(10) defines harboring as providing food or shelter to a dog. A professional walker with several dogs in hand, a relative caring for an animal for a week, a building superintendent who feeds a dog kept in a basement room, or a boarding arrangement in a private apartment can each fall inside that language. Section 123(10) uses a slightly different phrase, imposing strict liability for medical costs on the owner or lawful custodian of a dangerous dog, which explicitly contemplates someone other than the owner having charge of the animal at the relevant moment.</p>
<h2>Handling, after the 2025 decision</h2>
<p>Before April 17, 2025 a claim in New York arising from a domestic animal ran through the propensity showing alone, because Bard v Jahnke had closed off ordinary negligence. Flanders v Goodfellow overruled Bard to that extent, so an injured person may now plead that the person in charge of the animal failed to use reasonable care in how it was handled, independent of what anyone knew about its history. That matters most in exactly the handler scenario: a walker managing several leashes at a crossing, a handler who let a lead run out on a narrow sidewalk, or a person who took a dog into a crowded vestibule. Decisions predating Flanders that describe New York as recognizing no negligent-handling claim no longer state the current rule.</p>
<h2>Professional knowledge as a factor</h2>
<p>Hewitt v Palmer Veterinary Clinic, decided in 2020, held that a veterinary clinic does not receive the benefit of the vicious-propensities notice requirement and that a negligence claim may proceed against it without that showing. The Court reasoned that clinics possess specialized knowledge of animal behavior and are uniquely well equipped to anticipate and guard against aggressive behavior in their own settings. Cantore v Costantine, decided November 15, 2023, declined to extend that reasoning to all premises owners, and it was decided before Flanders. Read together, the two cases explain why a defendant's professional familiarity with dogs is a live consideration rather than a settled rule.</p>
<h2>The restraint rules a handler works under</h2>
<p>New York City Health Code 161.05(a) requires that a dog in a public place, or in an open or unfenced area abutting one, be restrained by a leash or other restraint not more than six feet long. Section 161.04(b) requires a valid metal license tag on the collar in public. The city penalty schedule sets two hundred dollars for a dog in public without a leash or other restraint, and four hundred on default, with the same amounts for a missing collar and tag and for failing to control an animal to prevent a nuisance. In parks, 56 RCNY 1-04(i) repeats the six-foot ceiling and allows an out-of-control animal to be seized and impounded.</p>
<h2>Numbers, capacity and control</h2>
<p>City data for 2024 records that of the 1,858 bites occurring away from the victim's own residential building, 187 involved a dog that was not leashed and 137 involved a dog that was leashed but not under control. The second figure is the handler category: the restraint was in place and the outcome happened anyway. City guidance on dog runs states that a person may bring up to three dogs into a run. A dangerous-dog order under Agriculture and Markets Law 123 can require that the animal be restrained in public on a leash held by a person at least twenty-one years old, which is the statute acknowledging that capability, not equipment, is the variable.</p>
<h2>Identifying who had charge of the dog</h2>
<p>A handler case turns on facts that vanish quickly. The walker's name, the company if there was one, the number of dogs in hand, the owner of each animal, and the route are the essential items, and they are usually obtainable only at the scene or from a doorman, a neighbor or a building camera. City figures show thirty-nine percent of 2024 bite reports arrived without a complete owner address or telephone number. Where the animal was in someone else's charge, that gap widens, because the person present may not be the person insured.</p>
<h2>Which policy answers, and how to find out</h2>
<p>Two insurance questions follow. Whether a household policy covering the owner responds when a third party had the animal, and whether the handler has separate liability coverage, are both answered by the policy form and its endorsements rather than by a summary. Insurance Law 3420(d)(1)(B) allows an injured person to ask an insurer in writing whether a liability policy was in effect on a specified date and what its limits are, requiring a written answer within sixty days, with a forty-five day route for the insurer to seek further identifying information. Under 3420(d)(2), a disclaimer or denial for bodily injury must be given in writing as soon as is reasonably possible.</p>

## svc_facial_scarring_claims_lede

A facial injury is valued on how it looks after healing finishes, not on the emergency-room note. Two legal definitions in New York attach consequences to disfigurement specifically, and both are satisfied by findings a physician writes in a chart rather than by description after the fact.

## svc_facial_scarring_claims_body

<h2>Two definitions that turn on disfigurement</h2>
<p>New York City Health Code 161.02 defines severe injury as physical injury inflicted by a dog causing at least a broken bone or a disfiguring laceration requiring either multiple stitches or cosmetic surgery, and under 161.07(f) a dog that caused severe injury stays in a shelter until an administrative law judge reports it is not dangerous. Agriculture and Markets Law 108(29) separately defines serious physical injury to include injury causing serious or protracted disfigurement. That definition matters because section 123 raises the civil penalty ceiling from four hundred dollars to fifteen hundred where the injury is serious, and makes a repeat incident involving an already-adjudicated dangerous dog a misdemeanor.</p>
<h2>What the chart has to say</h2>
<p>A useful operative and discharge record describes the anatomic location, the length and depth of the laceration, whether tissue was lost, the closure method and suture material, whether a specialist performed the repair, and any plan for revision. The word "disfiguring" is a legal threshold, so a chart that documents the elements supporting it is more valuable than one that uses the word without them. Photographs belong in the file from the first day: before cleaning, after closure, at suture removal and at intervals afterward, in ordinary indoor light, with the original image files retained rather than versions cropped or filtered for a phone.</p>
<h2>Timing a claim against the healing curve</h2>
<p>Scar appearance continues to change well past the point at which the wound has closed, and revision surgery is normally planned after that process settles rather than during it. That sequencing conflicts with the pace of an insurance discussion, because an early offer prices what the file shows on the day it is made. Under CPLR 3043(a) a defendant may demand a statement of injuries and whether they are claimed to be permanent, so a permanency opinion eventually has to exist in writing. Settling before a treating specialist can give one converts an open question into a closed number.</p>
<h2>The evidence the Court of Appeals worked with</h2>
<p>Flanders v Goodfellow, decided April 17, 2025, involved a postal carrier bitten on December 8, 2018 whose injury was described as a snap tear in a shoulder muscle requiring multiple surgeries and leaving permanent scarring. The decision matters here for two reasons. It confirms that permanent scarring from a dog attack is litigated as a serious injury, and it opened a second route to liability by overruling Bard v Jahnke to the extent that decision barred a common-law negligence claim for harm caused by a domestic animal. A claim can now be pleaded on propensity knowledge, on negligence, or on both.</p>
<h2>Which damages the statutes reach and which they do not</h2>
<p>Agriculture and Markets Law 123(10) imposes strict liability for medical costs on the owner or lawful custodian of a dangerous dog. It stops there. Disfigurement, the emotional consequences of a visible scar, lost income and future revision surgery are not covered by that subdivision, and subdivision 12 makes clear that section 123 does not limit other claims. That division explains why an opening offer that tracks the hospital charges can look reasonable and still be pricing only a fraction of the claim, particularly where the surgical plan has not yet been written.</p>
<h2>Offsets, structures and the arithmetic after a verdict</h2>
<p>Two procedural rules shape what a disfigurement award is finally worth. CPLR 4545 permits a post-verdict reduction of past or future economic loss shown to be replaced from a collateral source, less premiums paid for the two years before accrual and the projected future premium cost, with voluntary charitable contributions excluded. CPLR 5041 governs how a judgment containing substantial future damages is structured, with the court entering judgment for past damages and future damages up to the statutory threshold in a lump sum and the remainder paid periodically. Both operate on economic loss, so the non-economic component of a facial claim has to be documented on its own terms.</p>
<h2>The record about the animal still has to be built</h2>
<p>None of the medical work substitutes for proof about the dog. The propensity showing described in Collier v Zambito reaches any tendency to act in a way that might endanger others, and Agro v Olivieri holds that ordinary canine behavior such as barking at passersby or pulling on a leash will not establish it. So the same file that tracks surgical planning also needs the dated accounts of earlier lunging, the building complaint history, and any Health Department bite record, which under Health Code 161.02 is prima facie evidence that a dog is dangerous.</p>

## svc_homeowners_and_co_op_insurance_claims_lede

Before anyone argues about fault there is a narrower question with statutory answers: does a policy exist, whose name is on it, and what does it say. New York Insurance Law gives an injured person a written route to part of that information and a deadline for the reply.

## svc_homeowners_and_co_op_insurance_claims_body

<h2>A written request for coverage information</h2>
<p>Insurance Law 3420(d)(1)(B) allows an injured person or other claimant to ask an insurer in writing whether a liability policy was in effect on a specified date and what its limits are. The insurer must answer in writing within sixty days. It may instead request additional identifying information within forty-five days, and then has forty-five days after receiving it to respond. That provision exists because the ordinary bar to any settlement discussion is not liability, it is the absence of a policy number. The request should identify the insured, the address and the date of the incident with as much precision as the file allows.</p>
<h2>Notice of a disclaimer has its own standard</h2>
<p>Under Insurance Law 3420(d)(2), where a claim arises from death or bodily injury, an insurer that intends to disclaim liability or deny coverage must give written notice as soon as is reasonably possible to the insured and to the injured person or other claimant. Subdivisions (a)(4) and (a)(5) address late notice of the occurrence, providing that a failure to give timely notice does not invalidate a claim unless the failure prejudiced the insurer. Subdivision (c)(1) places the burden of proving a prejudicial lack of cooperation on the insurer. Each of those provisions is a reason to keep a dated copy of every letter sent and received.</p>
<h2>The direct action, and when it becomes available</h2>
<p>Insurance Law 3420(a)(2) requires liability policies issued or delivered in New York to provide that where a judgment against the insured remains unsatisfied for thirty days after service of notice of entry of the judgment, the injured person may maintain an action directly against the insurer up to the applicable policy limit. Subdivision (a)(6) provides a route where a disclaimer rests on late notice, unless within sixty days the insured or the insurer commences a declaratory judgment action naming the injured person. These are enforcement mechanisms rather than opening moves, but their existence shapes how a carrier evaluates a file.</p>
<h2>Cooperative and condominium structures complicate the coverage map</h2>
<p>A single incident in a co-op or condominium building can implicate more than one policy: a shareholder's or unit owner's own liability coverage, the corporation's or association's general liability program, and any umbrella policy sitting above either. Insurance Law 3425 defines the personal lines category to include residential real property of not more than four dwelling units, personal property, non-business liability where a natural person is the named insured, and personal umbrella liability policies. A large elevator building's own program falls outside that definition, so the protections written for personal lines cancellation and renewal do not apply to it in the same way.</p>
<h2>The declarations page is not the policy</h2>
<p>The document that arrives first is usually a declarations page listing the named insured, the term, the covered address and the limits. The obligations and the exclusions live in the policy form and its endorsements. Requesting the complete form and every endorsement in effect on the date of the incident is routine and it is the only way to know whether an animal exclusion, a breed restriction or a reduced sub-limit applies. A quotation of one clause inside an adjuster's letter is a summary written by an interested party, not a coverage determination.</p>
<h2>Claim handling standards, in writing</h2>
<p>Insurance Law 2601(a) identifies unfair claim settlement practices when committed without just cause and with such frequency as to indicate a general business practice. The list includes misrepresenting pertinent facts or policy provisions, failing to acknowledge pertinent communications with reasonable promptness, failing to adopt reasonable standards for prompt investigation, and failing to attempt in good faith to effectuate a prompt, fair and equitable settlement where liability has become reasonably clear. The same paragraph provides that after a properly executed proof of loss the insurer shall advise of acceptance or denial within thirty working days. Compelling policyholders to litigate by offering substantially less than the amounts ultimately recovered appears on the same list.</p>
<h2>Cancellation and renewal rules that decide whether coverage existed</h2>
<p>Insurance Law 3425 sets a required policy period of three years for a covered personal lines policy from first issuance or voluntary renewal. During the first sixty days a cancellation notice must state the specific reasons. After sixty days, or upon a renewal's effective date, cancellation requires an enumerated ground: nonpayment of premium, conviction of a crime arising out of acts that increased the hazard, discovery of fraud or material misrepresentation in obtaining the policy or presenting a claim, discovery of willful or reckless acts increasing the hazard, physical changes making the property uninsurable under uniformly applied standards, or a determination by the superintendent. A nonpayment notice must state the amount due, and payment within fifteen days after that notice is mailed is timely.</p>

## svc_landlord_and_building_owner_liability_lede

Ownership of a New York building is rarely one name. A deed holder, a managing agent, a co-op corporation, a condominium board and a sponsor can all sit above the same hallway, and only some of them had the power to do anything about the dog on the fourth floor.

## svc_landlord_and_building_owner_liability_body

<h2>Three elements, and the third one decides most cases</h2>
<p>The controlling test for a property owner was restated in Agro v Olivieri on April 10, 2024. The injured person must show that the owner had notice a dog was being harbored on the premises, that the owner knew or should have known the dog had vicious propensities, and that the owner had sufficient control of the premises to remove or confine the animal. The court treated notice as an element of the claim rather than an affirmative defense, which puts the burden where it is hardest to carry. Control is usually the element that separates a viable claim from a dismissed one, because it turns on lease language and governing documents rather than on sympathy.</p>
<h2>Reading control out of the paperwork</h2>
<p>In a rental building the proprietary question is what the lease permitted the owner to do. In a cooperative the shareholder holds a proprietary lease and the corporation holds title, so house rules and the board's enforcement powers matter. In a condominium the unit owner holds the deed and the board governs common elements, which narrows what the board could have confined or removed. Castillo v Berne, decided August 14, 2024, applied the same three-element framework to a tenant's dog that knocked a delivery worker down twice and bit at his clothing, and reminded litigants that identifying the dog's breed does nothing to establish what the landlord was aware of.</p>
<h2>The lease signed with the animal already in view</h2>
<p>The oldest thread in this area concerns the owner who knew before signing. Strunk v Zoltanski, decided July 3, 1984, held that a landlord who leases to a tenant knowing a vicious dog will be kept on the premises, and takes no reasonable precautions available through the lease or otherwise, may answer to a person later bitten there. The duty described is ordinary reasonable care rather than the strict liability imposed on the person harboring the animal. Later decisions treat knowledge acquired during a tenancy the same way where the owner retained the power to require confinement or removal.</p>
<h2>When a building becomes an owner of the dog</h2>
<p>Agriculture and Markets Law 108(15) defines an owner as any person who harbors or keeps a dog, and 108(10) defines harboring as providing food or shelter. A building that lets an animal live in a basement room, a superintendent who feeds a dog in a service corridor, or a management office that keeps a dog on site during the day can move from the premises analysis into the keeper analysis. That distinction changes which test applies and which insurance policy responds, so it is worth resolving early rather than assuming the building is only ever a premises defendant.</p>
<h2>The documents that establish notice</h2>
<p>Notice is proved with dated paper. The useful requests name the incident report prepared after the attack, the porter and doorman logs for that shift, correspondence about the apartment, prior complaints in any channel management actually monitors, and any warning letter sent to the tenant. The 311 service-request history for the address is public. In the city, a Department record of a bite report under Health Code 11.03, or a bite record made by a police officer, is prima facie evidence under 161.02 that a dog is dangerous, and outside the city General Municipal Law 209-cc requires an annual report to the municipal clerk for each address where an adjudicated dangerous dog is kept.</p>
<h2>Which deadline attaches to which owner</h2>
<p>The identity of the owner sets the calendar. Against a private owner, a managing agent or a co-op corporation, CPLR 214(5) allows three years from the date of the bite. Against a public housing authority or another city entity, General Municipal Law 50-e(1)(a) requires a notice of claim within ninety days after the claim accrues and 50-i(1) requires the action within one year and ninety days. Because a title search takes an afternoon and a missed municipal notice is usually fatal, the ownership question belongs at the front of the file rather than after the first settlement discussion.</p>

## svc_negligence_claims_beyond_one_bite_lede

For nineteen years New York recognized one route to an owner: proof of a vicious propensity and notice of it. That changed on April 17, 2025, and the change is recent enough that a great deal of published material describing New York law is now wrong.

## svc_negligence_claims_beyond_one_bite_body

<h2>What the Court of Appeals actually decided</h2>
<p>Flanders v Goodfellow was decided April 17, 2025 in a unanimous opinion by Judge Halligan. The plaintiff, a postal carrier, was bitten on December 8, 2018 while delivering a package. The Court held first that affidavits from two other postal workers describing the dog barking, snarling, baring its teeth and slamming into the window glass raised a triable question of constructive knowledge of a vicious propensity, so summary judgment was improper. It then held that Bard v Jahnke, 6 NY3d 592 (2006), is overruled to the extent it barred a common-law negligence claim for harm caused by a domestic animal.</p>
<h2>Two tracks, drawn from the Restatement</h2>
<p>The framework the Court described draws on sections 509 and 518 of the Restatement (Second) of Torts. Section 509 is the strict liability track: an owner who knows or has reason to know of a dangerous propensity abnormal to the class answers for harm it causes. Section 518 is the negligence track, addressing an animal not known to be abnormally dangerous, where liability turns on a failure to exercise reasonable care to prevent foreseeable harm. An injured person may plead one theory or both. The Court noted that roughly thirty-six states already recognized negligence as an alternative theory as of 2015, so New York's position had become unusual rather than typical.</p>
<h2>The propensity language the Court kept</h2>
<p>Nothing in the decision weakened the strict liability route. It reaffirmed Collier v Zambito, 1 NY3d 444, which describes a vicious propensity as the tendency to do any act that might endanger the safety of persons and property in a given situation, and which held that a triable issue may be raised even without a prior bite where a dog has been known to growl, snap or bare its teeth. It also cited Hastings v Sauve, 21 NY3d 122, for the proposition that a vicious propensity includes any behavior reflecting a proclivity to act in a way that puts others at risk of harm, and Doerr v Goldsmith, 25 NY3d 1114, which confirmed that dogs are not treated as farm animals.</p>
<h2>Why the negligence route matters in a dense city</h2>
<p>The practical difference shows up where the animal has no documented history. Under the older rule, a claim arising from a leash allowed to run out at a crosswalk, an unsecured apartment door, a gate propped open in a vestibule, or a handler managing several dogs at once failed for want of propensity evidence no matter how careless the conduct. On the negligence track those facts are the case, framed by the duty analysis the Court traced back to Palsgraf v Long Island R. R. Co., 248 NY 339. Hewitt v Palmer Veterinary Clinic, 35 NY3d 541, had already allowed a negligence claim against a defendant with specialized animal knowledge.</p>
<h2>Decisions that have to be read with the date attached</h2>
<p>Several Second Department opinions describe New York as recognizing no claim for negligent handling of a dog. Castillo v Berne, decided August 14, 2024, said so while also holding that a dog's breed standing alone does not establish a landlord's awareness of a vicious propensity. Cantore v Costantine, decided November 15, 2023, held that Hewitt did not extend ordinary negligence to all premises owners. Both predate Flanders on the negligence question. Slotnick v Chaikin, decided May 28, 2025, and Agro v Olivieri, decided April 10, 2024, remain useful for what they say about the strict liability elements and about ordinary canine behavior.</p>
<h2>The elements a negligence pleading has to carry</h2>
<p>A negligence theory does not remove the burden of proof, it relocates it. The claim requires a duty, a breach in the form of unreasonable conduct in keeping or handling the animal, causation and damages, and the evidence is behavioral and situational: the length of the lead, the state of a gate or door, the number of animals in one person's charge, the sight lines, prior warnings about the same conduct. Comparative fault remains available to the defense, since CPLR 1411 reduces recovery in proportion to a claimant's own culpable conduct and CPLR 1412 makes it an affirmative defense that must be pleaded and proved.</p>
<h2>What the statute contributes, and what it does not</h2>
<p>Agriculture and Markets Law 123(10) remains a narrow strict liability rule for medical costs against the owner or lawful custodian of a dangerous dog, and subdivision 12 states expressly that nothing in section 123 limits or abrogates any other claim available at common law or by statute. So the medical-cost provision, the strict liability propensity claim and the negligence claim can coexist in one file, each with different proof and a different reach. The filing period for all of them against a private defendant is the three years set by CPLR 214(5).</p>

## svc_nerve_and_tendon_damage_lede

A puncture that closes cleanly can still have divided a digital nerve or a flexor tendon underneath it. Those injuries are measured in function rather than appearance, and the proof they require is a sequence of clinical findings recorded over months.

## svc_nerve_and_tendon_damage_body

<h2>Function is the legal threshold</h2>
<p>Agriculture and Markets Law 108(29) defines serious physical injury to include protracted impairment of health and protracted loss or impairment of the function of any bodily organ, alongside serious or protracted disfigurement. Subdivision 28 defines ordinary physical injury as impairment of physical condition or substantial pain. The difference is not rhetorical: section 123 raises the civil penalty ceiling from four hundred dollars to fifteen hundred where the injury is serious, allows that amount to be offset by restitution for unreimbursed medical expenses and lost earnings, and treats a further incident involving an already-adjudicated dangerous dog as a misdemeanor carrying up to three thousand dollars, up to ninety days, or both.</p>
<h2>Hands, and the reason they are different</h2>
<p>Health Department figures for 2024 record that nine percent of reported bites followed the person handling the animal and eight percent followed play, categories in which the hand and forearm are the exposed structures. A hand injury raises questions a wound photograph cannot answer: whether flexion and extension are complete, whether sensation is intact in each digit, whether grip and pinch strength returned, and whether repair or grafting was required. Those answers come from serial examinations and from therapy notes, not from a single discharge summary, and they are the material a permanency opinion is eventually built on.</p>
<h2>The records that carry a functional claim</h2>
<p>The useful file contains the operative report if repair was performed, the therapist's measurements over time, any electrodiagnostic study, work restrictions in writing, and a treating specialist's statement about what is expected to be permanent. CPLR 3043(a) entitles a defendant to demand a statement of the injuries and whether they are claimed to be permanent, the length of time confined to bed and to the house, the length of time incapacitated from employment, and the special damages claimed. Building those categories contemporaneously is straightforward; reconstructing them from memory two years later is not.</p>
<h2>Infection and delayed complications</h2>
<p>Health Code 11.27(e) requires the owner of a biting dog to observe the animal closely for ten days and report immediately if it dies, becomes ill or escapes, and Health Code 11.03(a) makes an animal bite or an exposure to rabies a condition reportable to the Department. In 2024, eleven percent of bitten residents, 433 people, received post-exposure prophylaxis, up from seven percent in 2015, while city records reflect no rabid dog found in New York City in more than sixty years. Wound complications that develop after discharge belong in the chart with dates, because a later dispute about causation is resolved on the sequence of documented findings.</p>
<h2>Lost earnings, and the offset rule that follows them</h2>
<p>An injury that limits grip or sensation frequently affects work capacity long after the visible wound resolves. CPLR 4545 allows a court, after a verdict, to reduce an award for past or future economic loss where that loss was or will be replaced from a collateral source, less the premiums paid for the two years preceding accrual and the projected future cost of those premiums, with voluntary charitable contributions excluded. Where future damages are substantial, CPLR 5041 governs how the judgment is structured between lump-sum and periodic payments. Both rules operate on documented economic loss, which is another reason to record time out of work as it happens.</p>
<h2>Liability still turns on the animal's history</h2>
<p>Agriculture and Markets Law 123(10) makes the owner or lawful custodian of a dangerous dog strictly liable for medical costs, and no further. Everything else depends on the propensity showing described in Collier v Zambito or, after Flanders v Goodfellow on April 17, 2025, on ordinary negligence in how the animal was kept or handled. Slotnick v Chaikin, decided May 28, 2025, confirms that the severity of an attack does not by itself establish what an owner knew beforehand, so a serious functional injury does not relieve the claim of its evidentiary burden about earlier behavior.</p>
<h2>Two calendars run at once</h2>
<p>CPLR 214(5) gives three years from the bite for a personal injury action, and CPLR 306-b requires service within one hundred twenty days after commencement of an action governed by that period. Where a public entity is involved, General Municipal Law 50-e(1)(a) requires a notice of claim within ninety days and 50-i(1) requires suit within one year and ninety days. A nerve or tendon injury often takes longer than that to reach a stable prognosis, which is why the filing decision and the treatment timeline have to be managed as separate problems rather than one.</p>

## svc_park_and_sidewalk_attacks_lede

Public space is where the rules are most specific and where the identity of the dog is hardest to establish. The leash length, the off-leash window and the exclusion zones are all written down, and whether an incident happened inside one of them can decide the shape of a claim.

## svc_park_and_sidewalk_attacks_body

<h2>The leash rule and its exact dimensions</h2>
<p>New York City Health Code 161.05(a) requires that a dog in any public place, or in an open or unfenced area abutting a public place, be restrained by a leash or other restraint not more than six feet long. The Parks rule, 56 RCNY 1-04(i), is written in parallel: no person may allow an animal to be unleashed, unrestrained or out of control in any park, an out-of-control animal may be seized and impounded, and a licensed dog may enter a park wearing its license tag, vaccinated against rabies and restrained by a leash or other restraint not exceeding six feet. The city penalty schedule sets two hundred dollars for a dog in public without a leash or other restraint, four hundred on default.</p>
<h2>The off-leash window is narrower than most people think</h2>
<p>Under 56 RCNY 1-04(i), off-leash access in designated parks or portions of parks runs from the park's opening until 9:00 a.m. and from 9:00 p.m. until closing. The dog must remain under the owner's control and must not harass or injure any park patron, animal or vegetation. Health Code 161.05(b)(1) sets the outer boundary the Parks Department cannot cross: off-leash rules may not begin earlier than 9:00 P.M. nor extend past 9:00 A.M., and a handler must produce proof of license and rabies vaccination on demand. An incident at 10:30 in the morning with a dog off its leash falls outside every one of those provisions.</p>
<h2>Where dogs are not permitted at all</h2>
<p>The Parks rule excludes dogs from playgrounds, zoos, swimming pools and swimming facilities, bathing areas, adjacent bathing beaches and bridle paths. Beach sand access is described by the Parks Department as a courtesy permitted from October 2 until the Friday before Memorial Day, with borough restrictions between Memorial Day weekend and October 1. A handler must leash the animal immediately when directed by a Police Officer, an Urban Park Ranger, a Parks Enforcement Patrol officer, another Department employee or a Health Department employee, and refusing that direction violates 56 RCNY 1-03(c). Photographing the posted signage the same day is worth more than describing it later, because temporary signs are replaced.</p>
<h2>The geography the data shows</h2>
<p>City figures for 2024 record that forty percent of reported bites occurred away from the victim's own residential building, and that among those 1,858 incidents, 187 involved an unleashed dog and 137 a leashed but uncontrolled one. The largest reported activity category citywide was a person passing by or entering the dog's space, 1,107 incidents, at least 628 of them away from the person's own building. Rates vary sharply by geography: Staten Island had the highest borough rate in 2024 at seventy-seven per hundred thousand residents, with Port Richmond at one hundred and South Beach–Tottenville at eighty-nine, while the Bronx showed the largest increase, from forty-two to sixty-one per hundred thousand.</p>
<h2>Identifying a dog that walks away</h2>
<p>The hardest part of a public-space case is the name. Health Code 161.04(b) requires a valid metal license tag on the collar in public, and the license record is the shortest path from a tag to a keeper. Where no tag is visible, the practical substitutes are a photograph of the animal and the handler, the accounts of other park users, a dog run regular who recognizes the pair, and any fixed camera on an adjacent building or a park facility. City records show thirty-nine percent of 2024 bite reports lacked a complete owner address or telephone number, and 1,500 reports had no contact information at all.</p>
<h2>When the city itself is a potential defendant</h2>
<p>An attack in a park raises the question whether a municipal entity had a role, whether through maintenance of a designated area, enforcement, or handling of an animal already known to it. If a city entity is a potential defendant, General Municipal Law 50-e(1)(a) requires a notice of claim within ninety days after the claim accrues, section 50-e(2) prescribes its contents, and 50-i(1) requires the action within one year and ninety days, with the complaint alleging that thirty days have passed since service of the notice without adjustment. Against a private handler or owner, CPLR 214(5) allows three years.</p>
<h2>The parallel proceeding about the animal</h2>
<p>An unrestrained dog seized in a park may enter the administrative system immediately. Health Code 161.07 lets the Department make a preliminary dangerous-dog determination considering the circumstances, the nature and severity of the injuries and any prior bite history, with the petition and notice of hearing mailed no later than fifteen days after the dog enters a shelter and the hearing scheduled no more than twenty days after mailing. Outside the city, Agriculture and Markets Law 123 requires a hearing within five days on not less than two days' notice, decided on clear and convincing evidence.</p>

## svc_prior_vicious_propensity_evidence_lede

Propensity is proved with documents and sworn recollection, not with adjectives. The useful material is specific and dated: a complaint someone filed, a warning taped in a lobby, a muzzle that appeared one week, a neighbor who changed which stairwell they used.

## svc_prior_vicious_propensity_evidence_body

<h2>The record the Court of Appeals accepted in 2025</h2>
<p>Flanders v Goodfellow, decided April 17, 2025, is the current benchmark for what an evidentiary showing looks like. A postal carrier was bitten on December 8, 2018 while delivering a package. Two other postal workers swore that the same dog barked, snarled and bared its teeth at them, and slammed itself into the window glass as though trying to attack through it. The Court held that this raised a triable question of constructive knowledge, meaning the owners should have known of the propensity even without a prior bite. The carrier's injury was described as a snap tear in a shoulder muscle that required multiple surgeries and left permanent scarring.</p>
<h2>Constructive knowledge, and who can supply it</h2>
<p>Felice v Margolies, decided January 29, 2025, shows how little sworn material it can take to defeat a dismissal. The plaintiff was bitten in December 2018 while walking his own dogs on a Smithtown street, and summary judgment for the defendants was reversed because an affidavit from the plaintiff's mother raised a factual question about constructive knowledge. The lesson for a Manhattan file is that the witness does not have to be a professional, a party or a neighbor with a grievance. A relative who heard about the animal's behavior firsthand, dated and specific, is admissible material.</p>
<h2>Breed and severity are arguments, not evidence</h2>
<p>Two recurring shortcuts fail. In Castillo v Berne, decided August 14, 2024, the Second Department held that the fact a dog was a pit bull, standing alone, was insufficient to raise a triable issue about a landlord's awareness of a vicious propensity. Slotnick reached a similar conclusion about severity, holding that the nature of the attack itself did not establish prior knowledge. Collier is the older illustration: a family dog kept behind a kitchen gate when visitors arrived, with nothing showing the family feared it would hurt anyone, did not support the inference the plaintiff needed after a bite to a twelve-year-old's face.</p>
<h2>The city record that carries statutory weight</h2>
<p>New York City Health Code 161.02 gives one document unusual force. A Department record of a bite report made under section 11.03, or a record of a bite or other injury made by a police officer, is prima facie evidence that a dog is dangerous within the meaning of that article. The same definition covers a dog that menaces, threatens, attacks or bites a person who is peacefully conducting himself in a place where he may lawfully be. That is why the reporting call described elsewhere on this site has consequences beyond rabies follow-up: it creates a dated municipal record about the animal.</p>
<h2>Paper that already exists somewhere</h2>
<p>Several institutions hold propensity material without being asked. Health Code 161.07(l) requires a medical provider to forward records of an injury inflicted by a dog when the Department makes a written request. Health Code 161.07(b) requires a shelter to notify the Department no later than the first business day after a dog is admitted or after a sheltered dog injures a person, with the circumstances, contact information for injured persons, the owner and witnesses, and the types of injury. Outside the city, General Municipal Law 209-cc requires an annual written report to the municipal clerk for each address where a dog adjudicated dangerous is kept, and the clerk forwards it to police, fire and ambulance services.</p>
<h2>Collecting it before it evaporates</h2>
<p>Building material has the shortest life. Elevator and lobby recordings are frequently overwritten within days, porters rotate buildings, and message threads about a barking apartment get deleted when a group chat is cleaned out. A written preservation request naming camera positions and a date window, sent in the first week, is worth more than a subpoena served in month five. Alongside it: the 311 service-request history for the address, the incident report the managing agent prepared, and the names and phone numbers of anyone who has ever crossed the street to avoid that animal.</p>

## svc_renters_insurance_and_tenant_liability_lede

In a city where most households rent, the person who keeps the dog and the person who owns the building answer under different tests and are usually insured under different policies. Which one responds depends on who is named on the declarations page and what the form excludes.

## svc_renters_insurance_and_tenant_liability_body

<h2>The tenant is the keeper in almost every case</h2>
<p>Agriculture and Markets Law 108(15) defines an owner as any person who harbors or keeps any dog, and 108(10) defines harboring as providing food or shelter. A tenant who keeps a dog in an apartment fits both definitions regardless of whose name is on the deed, which is why the strict liability branch of New York animal law is usually aimed at the tenant while the premises analysis is aimed at the building. Agro v Olivieri, decided April 10, 2024, sets out the separate three-element showing required against a property owner: notice a dog was harbored there, knowledge of a vicious propensity, and sufficient control to remove or confine the animal.</p>
<h2>What a renters policy is, in statutory terms</h2>
<p>Insurance Law 3425 defines covered personal lines insurance to include personal property and non-business liability coverage where a natural person is the named insured, along with residential real property of not more than four dwelling units and personal umbrella liability policies. A tenant's policy therefore sits inside that regulated category, with the required three-year policy period and the enumerated cancellation grounds the section prescribes. A landlord's commercial program for a large building generally does not, which is one reason the two claims proceed on different timetables even when they arise from the same incident.</p>
<h2>Reading the form rather than the summary</h2>
<p>The declarations page names the insured, the term, the covered address and the personal liability limit. The obligations sit in the policy form and its endorsements, and animal liability is one of the areas most often modified there through an exclusion, a breed restriction or a reduced sub-limit. Requesting the complete form and every endorsement in effect on the date of the incident is the only reliable way to establish what applies. Where a policy is genuinely absent or coverage is denied, 11 NYCRR 216.6(d) requires the insurer to give written notice with the specific reasons once it determines that no policy was in force or that coverage is disclaimed.</p>
<h2>Guests, subtenants and the person actually holding the leash</h2>
<p>The named insured on a renters policy is often not the only adult in the apartment. A roommate not named on the policy, a guest, a relative staying temporarily or a hired walker may each fall within the statutory definition of a keeper while falling outside the coverage. Insurance Law 3420(b) addresses who may bring an action against an insurer, and 3420(d)(1)(B) gives an injured person the written route to confirm whether a policy was in effect and what its limits are, with a sixty-day deadline for the reply. Establishing who lived there and who was responsible for the animal on that date is a factual investigation, not a coverage question.</p>
<h2>Public housing changes the tenant's obligations</h2>
<p>For a tenant in a housing authority building, the pet policy revised in January 2021 governs alongside the lease. It permits one dog or one cat per household, caps a dog's adult weight at twenty-five pounds, prohibits Doberman Pinschers, Pit Bulls and Rottweilers by breed while exempting assistance animals from the weight and breed limits, and requires registration at the management office within thirty days of acquiring the animal. Leashes must be six feet or less at all times in public areas inside and outside the buildings, and vicious, threatening or biting animals are not permitted on the property. Those requirements bear on notice as well as on compliance.</p>
<h2>Cancellation, nonrenewal and the gap nobody notices</h2>
<p>Insurance Law 3425 controls whether coverage still existed on the date of an incident. Cancellation in the first sixty days requires a notice stating specific reasons; after that, cancellation requires an enumerated ground such as nonpayment, fraud or material misrepresentation in obtaining the policy or presenting a claim, or willful or reckless acts increasing the hazard. A notice of nonrenewal or conditional renewal must be mailed at least forty-five and not more than sixty days before the end of the policy period, with the specific reasons stated. Conditioning continuation on changed limits instead of cancelling requires twenty days' written notice.</p>
<h2>Deadlines run on the injury, not on the coverage discussion</h2>
<p>CPLR 214(5) allows three years from the date of the bite for a personal injury action, and no amount of correspondence with a carrier extends it. Insurance Law 2601(a) provides that after a properly executed proof of loss the insurer shall advise of acceptance or denial within thirty working days, and identifies as an unfair practice the tactic of compelling a claimant to sue by offering substantially less than the amount ultimately recovered. Under Insurance Law 3420(a)(2) an unsatisfied judgment against the insured opens a direct action against the insurer thirty days after service of notice of entry, up to the policy limit.</p>

## svc_statute_of_limitations_new_york_lede

Three years is the headline number and it is the one that misleads people, because at least four other deadlines can attach to the same dog attack. Two of them are shorter than a season, and one of them starts before anyone has finished treatment.

## svc_statute_of_limitations_new_york_body

<h2>The ordinary period, and when it starts</h2>
<p>CPLR 214(5) sets three years for an action to recover damages for a personal injury, which is the period that applies to a bite claim against a private dog owner, a tenant, a private landlord, a managing agent or a co-op corporation. CPLR 203 governs accrual and the relation back of claims, including when a claim asserted in an amended pleading is treated as interposed at the time of the original filing. The date the period runs from is the date of the injury, not the date a diagnosis was confirmed, a surgical plan was written or an insurance claim was denied.</p>
<h2>Ninety days, when a public entity is involved</h2>
<p>If a city agency, a public housing authority or another municipal entity is a potential defendant, General Municipal Law 50-e(1)(a) requires service of a notice of claim within ninety days after the claim accrues. Section 50-e(2) prescribes what the notice must contain, including the nature of the claim, the time, place and manner in which it arose and the items of damage claimed. General Municipal Law 50-i(1) then requires the action to be commenced within one year and ninety days after the event, and requires the complaint to allege that at least thirty days have elapsed since the notice was served, or forty where service was made on the Secretary of State, without adjustment or payment.</p>
<h2>The infancy extension, and its limits</h2>
<p>CPLR 208(a) provides that where a person entitled to bring an action is under the disability of infancy at the time the cause of action accrues, the time within which the action must be brought is extended to three years after the disability ceases. The ten-year outside limit stated in that section does not apply to infancy except in medical, dental and podiatric malpractice actions. The extension protects the filing right and nothing else. It does not preserve building video, keep witnesses in the same apartment, or stop an animal from being rehomed, and it does not extend the ninety-day municipal notice period.</p>
<h2>Two years for a death</h2>
<p>Where a dog attack results in death, EPTL 5-4.1 requires the personal representative of the decedent's estate to commence the wrongful death action within two years of the death. A survival claim for the decedent's own conscious pain and injury before death is brought under EPTL 11-3.2 by the same representative. Where a municipal defendant is involved, General Municipal Law 50-e(1)(a) sets the notice of claim period at ninety days from the appointment of the representative in a wrongful death case, and 50-i(1) sets the action period at two years after the death.</p>
<h2>Service, and the second deadline after filing</h2>
<p>Filing is not the end of the calendar. CPLR 306-b requires that service be made within one hundred twenty days after commencement of an action where the applicable limitations period is two years or more, with the court permitted to extend that time upon good cause shown or in the interest of justice. CPLR 205(a) provides a separate safety valve: where a timely action is terminated other than by a voluntary discontinuance, a failure to obtain personal jurisdiction, a dismissal for neglect to prosecute or a final judgment on the merits, a new action on the same transaction may be commenced within six months after the termination.</p>
<h2>The administrative clocks that move faster than any of these</h2>
<p>The proceedings about the animal run on a much shorter calendar than the injury claim. Agriculture and Markets Law 123 requires a dangerous-dog hearing within five days on not less than two days' written notice, with a euthanasia order stayed thirty days and an appeal taken by notice of appeal within thirty days. Inside New York City, Health Code 161.07(f) requires the petition and notice of hearing to be mailed no later than fifteen days after a dog enters a shelter, with the hearing scheduled no more than twenty days after mailing. Anyone waiting for a civil deadline will have missed those entirely.</p>
<h2>What the deadlines mean for the order of work</h2>
<p>Because the shortest periods attach to entities and animals rather than to the injury, the sequence matters. Identifying the property owner of record settles whether ninety days or three years applies. Confirming whether the animal entered a shelter settles whether an administrative hearing is already scheduled. Requesting coverage information under Insurance Law 3420(d)(1)(B), which requires a written answer within sixty days, runs in parallel. Comparative fault under CPLR 1411 and 1412 is a separate matter for trial, not a timing question, but the facts supporting it are gathered in the same first weeks.</p>

## svc_the_one_bite_rule_in_new_york_lede

The nickname is misleading. Nothing in New York law requires a first bite before an owner answers for the second one, and a dog with a bite already on its record does not automatically lose the case for its owner. What matters is what the owner knew, or should have known, about how the animal behaves.

## svc_the_one_bite_rule_in_new_york_body

<h2>The rule is about knowledge, not about counting bites</h2>
<p>Under the strict liability branch of New York animal law, an injured person shows that the animal had a vicious propensity and that the person who kept it knew or had reason to know about it. Collier v Zambito, decided by the Court of Appeals on February 17, 2004, defined that propensity as the tendency to do any act that might endanger the safety of persons and property in a given situation. The opinion also said in terms that a triable question can exist without any earlier bite at all, where a dog has been known to growl, snap or bare its teeth. So the phrase "one bite" describes an evidentiary habit rather than a statutory threshold.</p>
<h2>Who counts as the keeper of the dog</h2>
<p>Agriculture and Markets Law 108 supplies the vocabulary the rest of the statute runs on. Subdivision 15 defines an owner as any person who harbors or keeps any dog, and subdivision 10 defines harboring as providing food or shelter to a dog. Those two definitions matter more in a dense city than they sound. A relative who feeds the animal on alternate weeks, a superintendent who lets it sleep in a basement room, or an adult child living in a parent's apartment can all fall inside that language depending on the facts. Identifying every person who fits the definition early is what keeps a claim from being aimed at a single defendant who turns out to be judgment-proof.</p>
<h2>What courts have refused to treat as notice</h2>
<p>The reported decisions are unusually specific about behavior that does not establish knowledge. In Agro v Olivieri, decided April 10, 2024, the Second Department listed conduct within normal canine range: running around, pulling on a leash, barking at another dog or at passersby, barking at strangers and chasing animals. Barking when someone knocked at the door did not convert the animal into a guard dog, and a sore ear with a tendency to pull away when petted was not enough either. Slotnick v Chaikin, decided May 28, 2025, applied the same reasoning and rejected an unsupported claim that a dog was kept for protection, adding that the nature and severity of an attack does not by itself prove what the owner knew beforehand.</p>
<h2>The one number the statute does attach to the dog</h2>
<p>Agriculture and Markets Law 123(10) makes the owner or lawful custodian of a dangerous dog strictly liable for medical costs resulting from an injury that animal inflicted on a person, companion animal, farm animal or domestic animal. That provision does not reach lost earnings, scarring or pain, and it applies to an animal that meets the statutory dangerous-dog description rather than to every dog that has bitten someone. Subdivision 12 then says that nothing in section 123 limits any other claim available at common law or under another statute. Read together, the two subdivisions explain why a hospital bill and the rest of a claim travel on separate tracks.</p>
<h2>How the question reaches a judge</h2>
<p>Most of these disputes are resolved on a summary judgment motion under CPLR 3212(b), where the moving party must show entitlement to judgment as a matter of law and the opposing party must produce evidence raising a genuine factual dispute. That procedural posture is why affidavits from neighbors, porters, delivery workers and dog walkers carry weight out of proportion to their length. A single sworn account of an earlier lunge at a stroller in a vestibule can be the difference between a dismissed claim and one set down for trial, which is also why those accounts should be collected while the people who witnessed them still live in the building.</p>
<h2>Where the rule stands after April 2025</h2>
<p>The strict liability branch is no longer the only route. On April 17, 2025 the Court of Appeals decided Flanders v Goodfellow and overruled Bard v Jahnke to the extent it barred a common-law negligence claim for harm caused by a domestic animal. An injured person in Manhattan may now plead knowledge of a propensity, ordinary negligence in how the animal was kept or handled, or both theories together. Guides and summaries written before that date describe New York as a jurisdiction where the propensity showing is the only path, and that description is now out of date.</p>

## hero_promise

## what_happens_when_you_call
Start with the event, not a conclusion. The useful details are the date, location, people or businesses involved, care already received, photographs, incident reports, witnesses and any insurance correspondence. A proper claim review sorts those materials into questions: what duty may have existed, what evidence can still be obtained, whether another person may argue shared fault, and whether a short deadline applies. Do not turn a brief call into a substitute for preserving records. Save the originals, write down names and dates, and keep a simple timeline while it is still fresh.

## what_they_will_ask
Have the basic paper trail nearby: the date and exact Cincinnati or Hamilton County location; a report number if police, a property owner or an employer made one; names and contact details for witnesses; photographs or video; treatment dates and provider names; work missed; and letters, emails or recorded-message requests from insurers. For a defective-product matter, keep the product, packaging, instructions, receipt and serial number if it can be done safely. For a care-related injury, preserve appointment notices and discharge paperwork without altering them. A record that can be located is more useful than a polished account reconstructed months later.

## symptom_1_title
You were hurt on property, but the hazard is already gone

## symptom_1
A wet entryway gets dried, a loose handrail gets tightened, a broken step is replaced, and the visible condition that caused the fall may be gone before anyone asks why it existed. That does not answer the claim questions. The important work is to identify the exact place, the path taken, the condition, who controlled the area, whether there were warnings, and whether a report or camera system may capture the period around the event. In Cincinnati, that can mean separating a sidewalk, apartment common area, retail aisle, parking lot, stairwell or privately managed building rather than treating “the property” as one thing.

Photograph the area from a normal walking height if it can be done without returning to danger. Keep shoes or clothing only if they may genuinely show the condition; do not clean away a detail that could later matter. Ask for an incident-report copy or number, but do not assume a report decides responsibility. It generally records an early account, not a final legal finding. Write down staff names, witnesses and the time of day. If a camera may exist, the need to identify it is immediate because footage retention can be limited.

Ohio’s two-year bodily-injury rule is a starting point, not a reason to wait. Accrual rules and the facts can matter, and a public-body or contract issue can introduce a different framework. A person considering a premises claim needs the site evidence, care records and timeline reviewed together. The practical question is not only “did I fall?” but “what can be shown about the condition, notice, control, injury and timing?”

## symptom_2_title
Care after an accident raised more questions than answers

## symptom_2
A difficult medical outcome is not automatically proof of a medical claim, and an injury that happened in a crash or fall does not become a care-provider claim merely because treatment followed. The first job is to keep the two timelines straight: the original incident, then the care, symptoms, tests, referrals and communications that came afterward. Discharge papers, appointment records, imaging orders, bills and a dated symptom journal can help reconstruct that sequence. They should be preserved as records, not edited into a theory.

Ohio treats medical, dental, optometric and chiropractic claims differently from the ordinary two-year bodily-injury rule. The medical-claim statute generally provides one year after accrual, with a written-notice provision that can extend the time in stated circumstances; it also contains a four-year repose rule and exceptions. That is why guessing from a general injury deadline can be risky. The statute’s definitions and dates need a careful, fact-specific reading, especially when the original injury and later treatment are both part of the story.

A meaningful review asks what treatment was given, what information was available at the time, which person or entity was involved, whether informed-consent or standard-of-care questions are actually supported, and how the alleged harm connects to the care. It may require medical records that belong to more than one provider and an accurate chronology. Do not discard prescription labels, after-visit summaries or messages because they look routine. Routine records often establish dates. If symptoms are urgent, seek medical attention first; a claim question should not delay care.

## symptom_3_title
A product caused an injury, and the item is still in reach

## symptom_3
A product case can involve a design issue, a manufacturing problem, an inadequate warning, instructions, a retailer, a distributor or more than one product in a chain. The injured person should not decide which label applies before the item and its records are located. The immediate preservation task is practical: set the product aside, avoid further use, keep every piece that came apart, save packaging and instructions, photograph condition and labels, and retain the receipt, order confirmation or service record. Do not repair, discard or send it back without understanding whether that would remove evidence.

The date matters in more than one way. Ohio Revised Code section 2305.10 generally gives bodily-injury and product-liability actions two years after accrual, but product-liability claims also have a ten-year limit measured from delivery to the first qualifying purchaser or lessee, with listed exceptions. Those rules make a product’s age, model, delivery history and prior alterations more than background details. A recalled item or a later warning may matter, but it does not by itself settle causation or liability.

For a Cincinnati household item, workplace tool or consumer product, begin by recording where it came from and who handled it after purchase. Save screenshots of online listings and product pages with the date captured. If an employer, landlord or insurer holds the product, identify that in writing and preserve the chain of custody. The final question is evidence-driven: can the condition, warning, use, injury and responsible parties be connected without relying only on memory?

## symptom_4_title
The injury may change work, mobility or day-to-day care

## symptom_4
A serious injury claim should not be reduced to the first emergency-room bill. When mobility, cognitive function, personal care, household tasks or the ability to work changes over time, the records need to show that change accurately. Keep treatment plans, restrictions, therapy notes, assistive-device information, payroll records and a dated account of help needed at home. Describe what has actually changed rather than predicting an outcome. A calendar can be useful for appointments, missed work and the days a family member had to step in.

Ohio law separates economic loss from noneconomic loss in many tort actions. Economic loss includes wages and medical or rehabilitation expenditures; noneconomic loss includes items such as pain, suffering, disfigurement and mental anguish. Section 2315.18 generally limits recoverable noneconomic damages to the greater of $250,000 or three times economic loss, subject to stated per-plaintiff and per-occurrence ceilings. But the statute also identifies categories of permanent, severe physical harm for which that limitation does not apply, and it excludes medical claims from its definition of tort action. It is not a one-number formula for every serious injury.

Shared-fault issues can also affect compensatory damages. Ohio’s comparative-fault rule permits recovery only when the claimant’s fault is not greater than the combined fault of the other relevant people, and it reduces damages in proportion to the claimant’s percentage. That makes scene evidence, witness accounts and the sequence of events important even when the injury is severe. Long-term cases need careful records, not inflated forecasts or a generic damages calculator.

## qa_1_question
Does Ohio give every personal injury claim two years?

## qa_1_answer
No. Ohio Revised Code section 2305.10 generally sets a two-year period for bodily injury and product-liability claims, measured from accrual, but the section has specific rules and exceptions. Medical claims have their own statute, section 2305.113, which generally uses a one-year accrual period and contains notice, repose and exception provisions. Claims involving public entities, contracts, employment or a minor may need different analysis. Preserve the dates and ask a qualified attorney to apply the correct rule to the actual facts; this page cannot determine a deadline for a particular case.

## qa_2_question
What does the 51% rule mean in Ohio?

## qa_2_answer
Ohio Revised Code section 2315.33 does not use the shorthand “51%,” but its effect is that a claimant may recover only if the claimant’s contributory fault was not greater than the combined tortious conduct of the other relevant people. Any compensatory damages are reduced by the claimant’s percentage of fault. The percentage is determined under the statutory process, not by whichever account sounds most confident at the scene. Photographs, reports, witness contact details, road or property conditions and time-stamped records can all matter to that factual question.

## qa_3_question
Where are Cincinnati-area personal-injury lawsuits filed?

## qa_3_answer
The answer depends on the claim, the parties and the amount or relief sought. The Hamilton County Clerk of Courts’ Civil Division maintains records for lawsuits including personal injury, and Common Pleas pleadings may be filed through its e-filing system. A lawyer can determine the proper court and venue after reviewing the facts. The courthouse location is not a substitute for deciding jurisdiction, service requirements or a limitations deadline. Keep the address, incident location and names of every potential party precise from the beginning.

A dryer pushes hot, wet air and fine lint through its duct every time it runs. The lint screen stops most of the lint, but not all of it, and what gets past sticks to the walls of the duct, gathers at elbows and joints, and packs into the outlet. As the duct narrows, the drum stays humid longer, cycles stretch out, and the dryer runs hotter to compensate. That is the slow version of the problem. The fast version is a fire.

### What the fire data show

Federal numbers are blunt. Across 2008 through 2010, the USFA's topical study counted roughly 2,900 home dryer fires a year reported to fire departments, killing five people a year, hurting a hundred, and destroying some $35 million of property. In 34 percent of them the recorded contributing factor was simply that nobody had cleaned the dryer or its duct, and dust, fiber and lint topped the list of what caught first, ahead of clothing. None of that requires an old dryer or a careless household, only a duct that nobody has opened in a few years.

### How Ocala houses are vented

The answer depends on the house. In a block home with the laundry in the garage, the duct is often short and goes straight out a wall. In a single-story house with the laundry in a central utility room, the duct goes up into the attic, crosses it, and exits through a cap on the roof. In a two-story house the laundry may be upstairs with a run through a floor joist bay to a sidewall. And in a manufactured home the duct should run out past the skirting, though too often it stops underneath. Each of those is cleaned differently, which is why the first question is always where the vent comes out.

### What a full cleaning includes

The dryer is pulled away from the wall and disconnected. The transition hose is checked for crushing, tears and material: Florida's residential code requires a listed transition duct no longer than 8 feet that is not concealed in the wall, and white plastic hose has no place behind a dryer. The duct is then cleared end to end with rotary brushes on flexible rods, working toward the outlet while a vacuum or blower carries the lint out instead of packing it further down. The outlet is opened and cleared. The damper is checked so it lifts while the drum turns and drops shut the moment it quits, because Florida's code requires a backdraft damper at the termination and forbids screens there. Lint that drifted into the dryer's own cabinet, below the screen slot, is vacuumed out as well. Finally the dryer goes back on its hose and runs while someone stands at the outlet feeling for strong, warm flow.

A cleaning is also the moment to measure. Florida caps the duct at 35 feet from the transition duct connection to the outlet, minus 5 feet for each 4-inch-radius mitered 90-degree elbow and 2 feet 6 inches for each mitered 45. A run across a wide attic with three elbows can be over the limit, and an over-length duct will clog again quickly no matter how well it is cleaned. If that is the case, you hear it, along with the options: shortening or rerouting the run, or a booster fan where the fan maker's instructions allow the longer length.

A roof exit is the hardest kind of dryer vent to keep clear, and in Ocala it is common. ### Why the cap is the usual culprit

The roof cap has a flap or damper that the exhaust has to push open. When lint builds up on the flap's hinge or inside the hood, the flap opens less and less, until the cap is catching lint rather than releasing it. The sign is visible from the yard: a gray fuzz on the shingles around the cap. Florida's residential code forbids screens at the duct termination for exactly this reason, because a screen turns the outlet into a lint filter. A cap with a screen added later should have it removed.

### Two ends, not one

A roof vent cannot be properly cleaned from the laundry room alone. Brushing from the dryer end pushes lint toward the cap, and if the cap is packed, it stays there. A proper job opens the cap from the roof, clears it by hand, and brushes down from the top while a vacuum pulls from below, or brushes up from the bottom with the cap removed so the lint has somewhere to go. Then the cap is refitted, the flap is checked so it swings freely, and the airflow is tested with the dryer running.

Roof work is where most of the risk in vent maintenance sits. In a county where three in ten residents are 65 or older, a roof cap is the part of the job most worth handing off. The attic run is usually the longest part of the duct. Over time it can sag between supports, separate at joints, or get crushed when someone walks across the attic to reach something else. To a small bird, a dryer outlet looks like a perfect cavity: sheltered, dry, and warm every time the laundry runs. ### Signs there is a nest

Chirping from the laundry wall or the ceiling above it, especially in the morning. A bird flying in and out of the outlet. Straw or grass sticking out of the hood. ### What federal policy says

Most native birds are protected under the Migratory Bird Treaty Act. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's 2025 nest policy draws the key line. A nest with viable eggs or nestlings is in use, and destroying an in-use nest of a protected species requires a permit. A nest that is still being built, has no eggs, or has been abandoned after the young have fledged is inactive, and it can be destroyed without a permit as long as nobody keeps the nest or its contents. The first step is a look, not a pull. The outlet is opened and the nest is checked, by eye or camera, for eggs or young. If it is an in-use nest of a protected species, the right answer is usually to wait until the young have fledged, keep the dryer off in the meantime, and then remove the empty nest. ## svc_dryer_vent_repair_body

Some dryer vents cannot be fixed with a brush. The hose behind the dryer has been flattened against the wall, the duct in the attic has pulled apart, the run was built from plastic or foil, or the whole route is longer than the code allows. Cleaning those ducts helps for a few weeks and then the problem comes back. A repair fixes the duct itself.

### The transition hose

The short flexible section between the dryer and the wall is the most common failure. Every time the dryer is pushed back after a cleaning or a delivery, the hose can crush or kink, and a crushed hose restricts airflow as badly as a duct full of lint. Florida's residential code requires the transition duct to be a single listed length of no more than 8 feet, not concealed inside a wall or ceiling. White vinyl hose, and thin foil hose that tears easily, should be replaced. A recessed dryer box in the wall lets the dryer sit closer without crushing the hose.

### Separated and screwed joints

Joints in the duct should be sealed and lined up so each section's end points with the airflow, and the code says they may not be joined with screws, because screw tips inside the duct snag lint and start a blockage. In an attic run, joints that were taped rather than sealed often let go in the heat. A separated joint dumps hot, damp, lint-laden air into the attic or the wall cavity. The repair is to reconnect it properly, support the run at least every 12 feet, and seal it.

### Plastic and foil duct

The code requires the duct itself to be metal with a smooth inside and a 4-inch nominal diameter. Ribbed foil and plastic duct do not meet that, and their ridges catch lint far faster than smooth metal does. Where a previous installer ran flexible duct through an attic or wall, the fix is to replace it with smooth rigid duct.

### Manufactured homes

About one Marion County home in six is a mobile home, and the federal standard for them is explicit: the dryer must exhaust to the outside, the duct shall not end beneath the home, and no screws or fasteners may stick into the duct. A dryer duct that stops under the floor fills the crawl space with heat, moisture and lint. The repair is to extend the duct in proper material out past the skirting to a hood with a damper, and to clear whatever lint has piled up underneath.

### Runs that are too long

Florida caps a dryer duct at 35 feet from the transition duct connection to the outlet, with 5 feet subtracted for each 4-inch-radius mitered 90-degree elbow and 2 feet 6 inches for each mitered 45. A run that exceeds it will clog quickly however often it is cleaned. The fixes are a shorter route, such as a sidewall exit instead of a roof exit where the house allows; long-radius smooth fittings that count for much less than mitered elbows; or a booster fan where its manufacturer's instructions allow the length. Any run longer than 35 feet equivalent has to carry a permanent label within 6 feet of the duct connection saying how long it is.

### Outlets in the wrong place

A dryer duct must end outside the building with a backdraft damper and no screen. Where the dryer maker does not specify otherwise, the outlet should sit at least 3 feet from openings into the building, including ventilated soffits. A duct that ends in the attic, blows into a soffit vent, or stops just short of the roof deck is moving lint and moisture into the house. Rerouting it to a proper outlet is the repair.

### What it costs

Repairs are priced after the run has been seen, because the scope varies so much: a new transition hose is small, while rebuilding an attic run or extending a mobile home duct past the skirting is a bigger job. ### The once-a-year figure, and where it comes from

The yearly interval is the National Fire Protection Association's own advice. Its clothes dryer safety sheet says to clean lint out of the vent pipe once a year, or more often if drying takes longer than normal, and to clean the lint filter before or after each use. The reason it bothers to say so is in the federal fire numbers: the U.S. Fire Administration's study of 2008 to 2010 found failure to clean was the leading factor behind residential dryer fires, at 34 percent. A screen catches most lint. The rest rides the airflow into the duct and stays there.

### The calendar is a floor, not a target

A year is the longest a typical household should go, and plenty of Ocala homes should not wait that long. The signals that the duct needs attention now are practical ones: towels still damp after a full cycle, a dryer cabinet that feels hotter than usual, a laundry room that turns humid while the machine runs, or an outside flap that barely lifts when the drum is turning. Any of those means airflow has already dropped, and the date on the last receipt stops mattering.

### Barn laundry clogs a duct faster

Marion County holds roughly 75,000 horses, more than any other county in the nation, and a lot of that work comes home in the laundry basket. Saddle pads, barn towels and work jeans carry hair, hay chaff and arena dust that slips past the screen and sticks to the inside of the duct. A household washing barn clothes several times a week falls squarely under the "more often" half of the fire association's advice, and so does a home with several shedding dogs.

### Long attic runs and mobile homes

How the house is vented changes the schedule too. A single-story Ocala home with the laundry in the middle of the floor plan often sends its duct through the attic to a roof cap, and Florida's residential code trims the 35-foot length allowance by 5 feet for every mitered 90-degree elbow. Long, bent runs are where lint settles first, so a roof exit tends to need cleaning more often than a short wall run in a garage. In a manufactured home, about one Marion County housing unit in six, federal standards say the duct must not end beneath the home; one that stops under the floor never really gets clean, because the lint simply piles up in the crawl space.

### The cooler months

The USFA study also found residential dryer fires ran higher in fall and winter, peaking in January at 11 percent of the year's total. That makes late summer or early fall a sensible time to book the yearly cleaning, before the heavier bedding and longer drying cycles of the cooler season.

### An outlet you can check from the ground

Nearly three in ten Marion County residents are 65 or older, and ladders and roof caps are where vent maintenance goes wrong. Between cleanings, the easiest test is to stand at the outside hood while the dryer runs and feel for a strong, warm stream of air. A weak stream is the cue, whatever the calendar says.

### Doing the subtraction

The 35-foot figure comes from Section M1502 of the 2023 Florida Building Code, Residential, and it shrinks quickly once the duct starts turning. Three 90-degree elbows take away 15 feet, which leaves only 20 feet of straight pipe. Two 45-degree bends cost another 5. The short flexible hose between the dryer and the wall, which the code calls the transition duct, is not counted toward the 35 feet, but it has its own rules: a single listed length no longer than 8 feet, and never hidden inside a wall or floor.

### When the dryer manual sets the number

The code offers a second route. Under M1502.4.5.2, the size and maximum length can be taken from the dryer manufacturer's installation instructions instead, and the fitting table applies only if those instructions give no fitting calculations. Those instructions have to be handed to the building inspector at the concealment inspection, before the duct disappears behind drywall. That is why the same 30-foot attic run can pass for one dryer and fail for another: the manual for the machine actually installed is the document that counts.

### Booster fans for runs that cannot be shortened

Where a duct genuinely has to be long, the code allows a dryer exhaust duct power ventilator, a booster fan installed in the run. It must conform to UL 705 and be installed to its maker's instructions, and the maximum length then follows the fan manufacturer's figures rather than the 35-foot table. A booster is a fix for a duct that cannot be rerouted, not a reason to skip measuring.

### A label belongs near the dryer

When a duct's equivalent length is over 35 feet, the code requires that length to be written on a permanent label or tag within 6 feet of where the exhaust duct connects. A missing label on an obviously long run is worth noticing during a home purchase, because it suggests nobody checked the length when the duct went in.

### The rest of the rules for the run

Length is only part of it. The duct must be metal with a smooth interior, at least 28 gauge, 4 inches nominal, supported at least every 12 feet and never joined with screws or similar fasteners. It has to end outside the building with a backdraft damper and no screen, and, if the dryer maker does not say otherwise, at least 3 feet from any opening into the house.

### Why Ocala ducts run long

Ocala's single-story homes with central laundry rooms are the classic over-length case: the duct climbs into the attic, crosses it with two or three elbows, and exits through the roof. Manufactured homes have the opposite problem. Federal standards at 24 CFR 3280.708 forbid a dryer duct from ending beneath the home, so a duct that stops under the floor needs extending past the skirting, which adds length that has to be counted.

### What to do with a duct that is too long

Measure it, count the elbows, and check the dryer manual. If the run is over, the choices are rerouting to a shorter exit, swapping sharp elbows for gentler bends, or a listed booster fan. That work is [dryer vent repair](../dryer-vent-repair/); a long run that is merely dirty is [dryer vent cleaning](../dryer-vent-cleaning/) or, through the roof, [roof dryer vent cleaning](../roof-dryer-vent-cleaning/).

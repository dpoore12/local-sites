## symptom_1_title
The thermostat is set to heat, but the house is still cold

## symptom_1
Confirm the thermostat sits in heat mode, is set above room temperature, and still has a live display. Then stand near the furnace and note what follows the request: silence, a draft fan, a clicking start attempt, a short flame, or a status light. Write that order down. Skip the repeated breaker resets, because each one wipes out the pattern a technician needs.

## symptom_2_title
The furnace starts, then shuts off before it heats the house

## symptom_2
Either the burners lit or they never did, and that one fact splits the diagnosis in half. From a safe distance, watch whether the draft fan spins up, whether the igniter glows, whether flame appears, and how many seconds it survives before the cabinet goes quiet. Count the attempts before lockout. Keep hands away from burners, wiring, and the vent connection.

## symptom_3_title
The blower runs, but the vents are pushing cool air

## symptom_3
Check the fan switch first. Left on, it pushes room-temperature air between heat cycles and imitates a breakdown. If the switch reads auto and the supply air is still cool, the burners either never lit or dropped out early while the fan kept cooling the heat exchanger. Look at the filter, leave every panel closed, and note which rooms went cold.

## symptom_4_title
The furnace is making a new bang, scrape, rattle, or squeal

## symptom_4
Timing narrows a noise down faster than the noise itself. Note whether it arrives with the fan, during light-off, mid-burn, or at shutdown, and which cabinet or duct area seems to carry it. A squeal or scrape that begins exactly as air starts moving points toward the blower assembly. Shut the system off if a bang comes with soot or a gas odor.

## pricing_body
### Why one furnace call has two prices

A furnace that shuts down in February produces one symptom and a dozen candidate causes. A dirty flame sensor and a cracked heat exchanger both end with a cold house, and they sit a factor of thirty apart on the invoice. That is why the bands here are wide at the top: diagnosis decides the row, and it happens with a meter on the control board, not from the driveway.

Position inside a band tracks three things: whether the part is proprietary or a stocked universal, whether nearby fittings crumble when touched, and whether the call lands on a quiet Tuesday or in the first hard freeze.

### Altitude, and the derate nobody mentions

This city sits above 6,000 feet, and gas equipment ignores its sea-level nameplate here. Input has to be derated as elevation climbs, so a furnace sized off square footage alone arrives oversized, short cycles all winter, and wears out its igniter, inducer and board early. On a repeat failure, ask whether the part broke because the equipment never matched the elevation.

### What the building department charges

Replacing a furnace is permitted work, and the Pikes Peak Regional Building Department fee schedule states the numbers plainly: $50 for a furnace or boiler replacement, $75 when the vent goes with it, $40 for a water heater alone. Failed inspections escalate faster than the permit does, at $50, then $100, then $200, with the third re-inspection blocked for two working days. Heating and gas piping permits in one and two family dwellings go to Mechanical Contractor B and Gas Piping Contractor classes, so a changeout bid from an outfit that cannot pull a permit is not comparable to one that includes it.

### What an honest changeout estimate names

It states the manufacturer and model, the AFUE, the input rating at this elevation rather than at sea level, the stages, the blower type, the vent material and route. It also states where the condensate goes and how it stays unfrozen, whether the gas line and circuit are adequate, the haul-away, the permit line and the inspection. The rebate belongs on the sheet as a separate line, not blended into the price: the utility pays $900 on a qualifying ENERGY STAR furnace.

### The replacement push, said plainly

A cracked heat exchanger is a genuine reason to stop repairing a furnace. The tell is being told about a crack that nobody shows you. A camera photograph, a combustion reading in parts per million, or a marked location takes two minutes, and condemning an exchanger without one asks for four figures on faith. Igniters, flame sensors, pressure switches, inducers and boards are repairs, not death sentences. The other pattern worth naming is the tune-up that turns into an emergency: that visit is a $99 to $249 job here, and it should end with a written condition report.

### What a national calculator misses in this county

It prices equipment and a generic install. It does not carry the permit, a possible re-inspection, the chimney liner an orphaned water heater needs once a condensing furnace stops sharing the masonry flue, a sidewall vent through brick or stucco, gas line resizing, or altitude derating. On a mid 1980s house, the median vintage here, it also skips the undersized return that has been cooking blower motors for years.

## svc_furnace_repair_body
### Reading the shutdown in the order it happened

A gas furnace is a sequence, and every sequence has a place where it quits. The draft inducer spins, a pressure switch confirms it, the igniter heats, the gas valve opens, burners establish, a sensor proves the flame is real, and only then does the blower push warm air into the ducts. Naming the step that failed is most of the diagnosis. Silence after a heat request sends testing to power, the door interlock and the thermostat circuit. A start that dies four seconds after light-off sends it to flame proving. Heat that never reaches the far bedroom sends it to airflow and duct delivery.

A blinking board light is a pointer, not a verdict. It says which circuit reported trouble; it does not say whether a switch failed or was telling the truth about a blocked drain.

### What 6,035 feet does to the gas input

The city's own budget profile puts the elevation at 6,035 feet. Fuel gas code language allows an appliance to use its nameplate input only to 2,000 feet. Above that, input must be reduced: four percent for each 1,000 feet above sea level before equipment is selected, or by a method the building department accepts, or as the manufacturer's instructions direct. Run the first version at this elevation and roughly a quarter of the nameplate rating disappears, so an 80,000 Btu-per-hour furnace is really delivering closer to 61,000. Several manufacturers write the rule as four percent per 1,000 feet above 2,000 feet, which lands nearer 67,000 on the same cabinet.

That gap matters when someone sized a replacement off square footage and a sea-level sticker. The result is an oversized furnace that satisfies the thermostat in short bursts, and short bursts are hard on igniters, inducers and control boards. Rheem's high altitude kit instructions add a second wrinkle worth knowing here: on ninety-plus furnaces installed above 5,000 feet, the pressure switch is supposed to be exchanged for the high altitude switch, and burner orifices may need changing depending on elevation and the heating value of the gas.

### The mechanical room that cannot breathe

Combustion needs air, and thin air carries less oxygen per cubic foot. Where the leakage rate of a house is unknown, the standard indoor-air calculation gives 50 cubic feet of room volume for every 1,000 Btu per hour of appliance input. Older homes usually satisfied that by accident. A newer or tightly sealed house frequently cannot, which is why the code turns to outdoor openings sized on input and count, or to a mechanical supply of at least 0.35 cubic feet per minute per 1,000 Btu per hour, interlocked to the equipment.

The regional building department lists a mechanical room without combustion air among the five mistakes its inspectors see most, and ties it directly to venting and carbon monoxide trouble. Federal researchers make the same point with numbers: a 2,000-square-foot house at 18,000 cubic feet, moderately tight, running one 300-cubic-foot-per-minute exhaust fan, can be pulled negative enough that no natural draft appliance drafts safely. A range hood is not a furnace problem until the furnace shares its air.

### A cracked heat exchanger needs proof, not a story

Condemning an exchanger is a four-figure conversation, so it deserves evidence: a photograph through a scope, a marked location, a measured temperature rise against the data plate, a combustion reading in parts per million. Manufacturer literature explains the failure path plainly. When input is not reduced for altitude, combustion turns inefficient or incomplete and excessive temperature rise can bring on premature heat exchanger failure. The same documents forbid derating by dialing manifold pressure below the nameplate value, because low air density and low pressure together stop the orifice from pulling in enough primary air.

### Carbon monoxide is its own decision

Colorado Springs Utilities describes a healthy burner flame as roughly ninety percent blue and calls yellow a sign the appliance is not working right. If an alarm sounds or people feel sick, the guidance is to get outside and call 911 or the utility's around-the-clock line, then leave the equipment alone until the source is found. The building department requires a working detector on any level with a bedroom whenever mechanical equipment is installed or replaced.

## svc_furnace_tune_up_body
### What a measured visit records

Maintenance earns its keep when it leaves numbers behind. The plate on the furnace states an allowable temperature rise across the heat exchanger, and the reading either falls inside that window or explains itself: a loaded filter, a closed return, a blower speed tap chosen for cooling and never revisited. Manifold pressure gets checked against the plate rather than adjusted by feel. Flame signal, draft, burner appearance, wiring, drainage on condensing equipment and the condition of accessible vent joints all belong on the same sheet.

Federal guidance for putting gas equipment into service lists the same steps a good annual visit repeats: adjust the input, including the derate for high altitude, adjust primary air, then check safety shutoffs, ignition devices, limit controls and draft. That order is worth noticing. The safety devices come after the combustion setup, because a furnace burning wrong will keep tripping devices that are working correctly.

### Depressurization, and the radon fan in the same room

Basements here often hold two systems that both move air: the furnace, and a radon fan. El Paso County carries the highest zone designation on the federal radon map, meaning a predicted average indoor screening level above 4 picocuries per liter, and the state health department reports that about half of Colorado homes exceed that action level. Soil gas gets in because the house sits warmer and at lower pressure than the ground around it, drawing air through the joint between wall and slab, sump openings, construction joints and plumbing penetrations.

That pressure relationship is the link back to combustion. Code air-tightness limits in this climate zone reach three air changes per hour at 50 pascals, and a mitigation fan, a dryer, a range hood and a bath fan all pull in the same direction. A tune-up that ignores the pressure the appliance actually operates under has skipped the interesting part. Sealing a combustion air opening to cure a draft complaint is exactly the wrong move.

### Filters, dust and the dry season

Dry air, sanding drywall in a basement finish, a new dog, an open window in April: filter loading has no calendar. What matters is using the size and orientation the rack was built for, and looking at the filter more often during long runtime stretches. A dense filter forced into a slot designed for a thinner one restricts air and can trip the same limit switch a technician was called out to explain.

### What should be on paper afterward

A condition report, not a verdict. Readings taken, parts cleaned or replaced, anything found on the vent or drainage side, and any recommendation with a reason attached. If a tune-up turns into a safety finding, that changes the visit from maintenance to inspection, and it should be said out loud rather than buried in a line item.

## svc_furnace_ignition_repair_lede
Ignition work covers draft, light-off and flame proving. A furnace that clicks and never lights, lights and drops out in seconds, or locks out after three tries has stopped at a specific step, and that step decides which part is actually involved.

## svc_furnace_ignition_repair_body
### Where the sequence stopped

Watching one heat call from across the room answers more than a phone description ever will. Did the draft inducer spin at all? Did the igniter glow? Did burners carry across, and for how long? Did the cabinet lock out after a set number of attempts? A furnace that never reaches the igniter is reporting a problem with demand, power, the door switch, a rollout or limit circuit, the inducer, the pressure switch or the board. A furnace that lights and quits has already proved the igniter and the gas valve work.

### Flame proving is an electrical measurement

The sensor in the burner path is not a temperature device. Flame conducts a tiny current, the control measures it in microamps, and if the number falls short the valve closes because unproven gas is unacceptable. A weak signal comes from a coated sensor, a poor ground, a cracked porcelain, damaged wiring, misaligned burners or an incorrect combustion picture. Cleaning a sensor and calling it finished is only honest when the signal was measured before and after and the rest of the cycle was watched again.

### Running rich at altitude wears out ignition parts

Thin air is the local complication. Manufacturers require the input to come down as elevation rises, and they are specific about how. One high altitude kit steps main burner orifices down from 2.15 millimeters to 2.05 in its first band above 2,000 feet, supplies one orifice per 20,000 Btu per hour of input, and calls for 50 inch-pounds of torque on installation. The same manual bans the shortcut of lowering manifold pressure below the specified value, because reduced air density plus reduced pressure at the orifice prevents proper aspiration of primary air. Another maker ships a high altitude pressure switch that has to go into ninety-plus furnaces above 5,000 feet, and prints a table stepping the natural gas orifice drill number up, meaning a smaller hole, as elevation climbs.

A furnace burning rich shows it in the parts that fail repeatedly: sooted burners, a sensor that needs cleaning every few weeks, a flame that lifts and drops. Replacing that sensor a third time treats the symptom of a setup problem.

### Pressure switch faults and the weather outside

The switch that proves draft is a reporter. When it opens, the useful question is what it saw: an inducer losing performance, a plugged port or tube, water standing in a condensate trap, a screen packed with debris, or a vent termination that took a hit. Wind matters here because gusts of 60 to near 100 mph occur in and near the foothills around this city, and the airport recorded a 92 mph gust during the extreme wind event on December 15, 2021. If the trouble started the morning after a windstorm, say so when the visit is scheduled and let someone look at the whole vent path from the ground up.

### Short cycling is a finding, not a quirk

Three-minute burns that satisfy nothing are worth chasing. A furnace oversized for the house, or sized on a sea-level rating in a city 6,035 feet up, spends the winter starting and stopping. Every start is a thermal shock to the igniter and a load on the inducer and board. Add a restricted filter or an undersized return and the high limit begins ending cycles too, which drops the blower into a cool-down run that homeowners describe as cold air. The fix is rarely the last part that broke.

### What not to try from the hallway

Do not jumper a safety switch, tape a pressure tube, or keep cycling power to force another attempt. Lockout is the control protecting the house, and repeated resets destroy the evidence a technician would have used. A gas odor changes everything: leave, do not stop to open windows, avoid light switches, garage door openers and anything else that can spark, and call 911 or the utility's twenty-four-hour number from outside. The utility responds to indoor and outdoor gas odor calls at no charge, and its own guidance notes that a meter still turning with every appliance off means gas is going somewhere.

### How the visit runs

Expect a controlled heat call, a meter on the control board and the flame circuit, a look at burners and the drainage and vent path, and a check of the setup against the data plate for this elevation. The regional building department also requires the manufacturer's installation instructions to stay with the equipment, and that booklet in the cabinet is often the fastest route to the model's own high altitude table. You should get the failed component named, the reason it failed, and the number before the work goes ahead.

## svc_furnace_blower_motor_repair_lede
Blower work is about delivery. The burners can be perfect while rooms stay cold, the limit keeps tripping, the fan squeals at startup, or air keeps moving long after the flame is out. The motor is one candidate among several.

## svc_furnace_blower_motor_repair_body
### Heat made and heat delivered are two different problems

Start by separating them. If flame establishes and holds, the furnace is making heat, and cold rooms point at what happens after that: filter, returns, blower wheel, motor, capacitor or module, duct leakage and register balance. If the fan runs when nothing is burning, look at the thermostat fan setting, a control protecting the cabinet after a limit event, or a board output stuck on. The order of questions saves a wasted part.

### Thin air carries less heat per cubic foot

Air at 6,035 feet is less dense than the air a sea-level rating book assumes, and less dense air holds less heat for the same volume. To move a given number of Btu into the house, the blower has to shift more cubic feet per minute than the same equipment would at sea level. That is the quiet reason airflow problems bite harder here. A filter one month past due, a return grille behind a sofa, or a flex duct crushed against a joist takes a system that had no margin and pushes temperature rise past the range printed on the data plate. The high limit then opens, burners stop, the fan keeps running to cool the exchanger, and the complaint arrives as cool air rather than as an airflow fault.

Manufacturers describe the same physics from the combustion side: the induced draft blower keeps moving nearly constant volume while the oxygen in that volume drops with elevation. Air quantity and heat capacity are not the same thing, and altitude separates them.

Northgate sits at the top of the city, near an Air Force Academy campus whose cadet area is at 7,258 feet, and hillside addresses toward Broadmoor Hills often put equipment in crawlspaces with tight service access.

None of that diagnoses a motor. It does tell a technician where to look for the restriction before condemning one.

### Sounds, and exactly when they appear

A squeal or scrape that starts the instant air begins moving belongs to the blower assembly: bearings, a wheel rubbing its housing, a loose mount. A thump usually means debris or an out-of-balance wheel. A hum with no rotation points at a starting component or a jammed motor. A rattle that only shows up at full airflow is often a cabinet panel or a duct transition, and panels transmit sound so well that the noise you hear rarely sits where you think it does.

### The trap on a motor replacement

The second-visit failure in this trade is a new motor working against the same restriction that killed the first one. Static pressure across the air handler is the measurement that separates a bad motor from a bad duct system, and it takes minutes. Skipping it produces an expensive part swap, a furnace that still trips on limit in February, and a homeowner who now believes the furnace itself is finished. A variable-speed module and a fixed-speed motor also fail in different ways and are not interchangeable by eye; the wiring, the taps or the programming have to match what the cabinet actually shipped with.

### Filters, returns and the things people cover up

Filter and return access is the part a homeowner controls. Use the dimensions the rack was designed around, keep the airflow arrow pointed at the blower, and leave return grilles uncovered. Basement finishes are a frequent culprit in this market: a framed wall closes off the old return chase, a door gets weatherstripped, and the equipment loses the air it was commissioned with. Say so when scheduling if a remodel, new flooring or a filter cabinet change came shortly before the trouble started.

### What the repair covers, and what moves the number

Testing includes motor electrical readings, wheel and housing condition, capacitor or module operation, filter and return restriction, temperature rise and static pressure. Cleaning a wheel, replacing a capacitor, correcting a wiring or speed-tap error, fitting a compatible motor, or opening up a strangled return are separate scopes with separate costs. Access, equipment age, parts availability and how much duct correction the job needs move the figure more than the motor itself does. Whichever it turns out to be, the finding and the price come before the work.

## q_how_long_does_a_furnace_last_lede

A gas furnace runs 15 to 20 years in most of the country. In Colorado Springs the heating season is long enough that the same equipment reaches those hours sooner.

## q_how_long_does_a_furnace_last_body

### The number is hours, not birthdays

Manufacturers quote 15 to 20 years, and that figure assumes an average heating
season. Colorado Springs does not have one. Heat runs here from roughly October
into May, and a furnace that would coast to 20 years in a mild climate is doing
considerably more cycles per winter to hold the same indoor temperature. Age in
years is a rough proxy; hours of run time is what actually wears the ignitor,
the blower bearings and the heat exchanger.

### What fails first, and in what order

Ignitors and flame sensors are consumables and get replaced several times over a
furnace's life. Blower motors and control boards are mid-life repairs. The heat
exchanger is the part that ends a furnace, because a crack in it is a combustion
safety problem rather than an efficiency one, and replacing the exchanger alone is
rarely worth doing on an older unit.

### The altitude detail that shortens the list

The International Fuel Gas Code, section 303.3, requires appliance input to be
derated 4 percent for every 1,000 feet above 2,000 feet. Colorado Springs sits at
about 6,035 feet, which is roughly 4,035 feet above that threshold and a derate
near 16 percent. A furnace rated 100,000 BTU here delivers closer to 84,000. If a
unit was sized on its nameplate rather than its derated output, it has been
running longer cycles than intended for its whole life, and it will show its age
early.

### When repair stops making sense

The usual test is whether the repair costs more than a third of a replacement on
a unit past its expected life. Two other things override that arithmetic: a
cracked heat exchanger, and a furnace whose parts are no longer manufactured. On
a 20-year-old unit both become likely at once.

### What tells you where yours actually is

The data plate carries a manufacture date. That plus the current condition of the
burners, the exchanger and the blower is what an inspection is for. Ductwork,
filter history and whether the unit was correctly derated at install matter more
to remaining life than the year on the plate.

### What a 20-year-old furnace is worth inspecting for

Three findings change the answer from repair to replace: a cracked heat
exchanger, a burner assembly that has sooted from running rich, and a blower
motor drawing above its rated amperage. Any one of those on a unit past its
expected life makes further repair a bet against arithmetic.

## q_furnace_blowing_cold_air_lede

A furnace blowing cold air is usually still trying to run. The blower is doing its job and the burner side is not, and the reason is almost always one of six things.

## q_furnace_blowing_cold_air_body

### The blower is fine, the heat is not

Cold air at the registers with the fan running means the circulating side works
and the combustion side has stopped. That is a narrower problem than it feels
like, and the causes sort into a short list.

### Start with the thermostat setting

A fan switch left on ON rather than AUTO runs the blower continuously, including
between burner cycles. The result is exactly this symptom, and it is not a fault.
Check that before anything else, because it costs nothing.

### Flame sensor, the most common real cause

The flame sensor is a thin metal rod that confirms a flame is present. It carbons
over with use. When it can no longer detect the flame, the control board shuts the
gas within a few seconds as a safety response, but the blower keeps running to
clear residual heat. The tell is a burner that lights, runs briefly, then quits
while air keeps moving. Cleaning or replacing the sensor is a routine repair.

### Ignition failure and lockout

If the ignitor has failed the burner never lights at all. Most control boards
retry a set number of times, then lock out and stop trying. Some will still run
the blower. Cycling power resets the lockout, but a furnace that locks out again
has a fault, not a glitch.

### Overheating on a dirty filter

A restricted filter starves the blower of return air. The heat exchanger runs hot,
the high limit switch opens, and the gas shuts off while the fan continues in order
to cool the exchanger. That sequence is the safety working correctly. In Colorado
Springs a filter is doing more work than in a milder climate simply because the
system runs more hours.

### Condensate and gas supply

On a high-efficiency furnace, a blocked condensate drain trips a float switch and
stops the burner. And an interrupted gas supply, whether a valve, a regulator or
a supply issue, produces the same cold-air result. Both are checked at the unit
rather than guessed at from the thermostat.

### What separates these without guessing

The order of the startup sequence does it. A furnace that never lights has an
ignition fault. One that lights and quits within seconds has a flame sensor. One
that runs several minutes and then quits while the fan continues has overheated
on airflow. Those three are distinguished by watching one cycle, which is why the
diagnosis happens at the unit rather than over the phone.

## q_how_to_reset_a_furnace_lede

Most furnaces have no reset button. What people mean by resetting one is clearing a safety lockout, and that is worth doing once and not worth doing twice.

## q_how_to_reset_a_furnace_body

### What a reset actually is

A modern furnace locks out after a set number of failed ignition attempts. That
lockout is deliberate: the board has counted a fault and stopped trying rather
than continuing to send gas to a burner that will not light. Clearing it means
cutting power so the board restarts its count.

### The sequence

Set the thermostat to off. Cut power at the furnace switch, which usually looks
like a light switch on or near the unit, or at the breaker labeled for it. Leave
it off for about thirty seconds so the board fully discharges. Restore power, then
set the thermostat to call for heat. The furnace will run its normal startup
sequence, which includes a purge before ignition.

### The blower door counts

Most units have a safety switch behind the blower door. If that door is not seated
the furnace will not start at all, and it will look identical to a dead unit. If a
panel was removed recently, check that it is fully closed before anything else.

### Once is diagnosis, twice is a fault

A furnace that locks out once and then runs normally may have had a single failed
ignition. A furnace that locks out again is telling you a component has failed,
usually the ignitor, the flame sensor or the pressure switch. Repeated resets do
not fix it and they put a burner through repeated ignition attempts.

### When not to reset at all

Do not reset a furnace after a gas smell, after water has reached it, or if the
lockout followed a loud noise. Do not bypass or tape a limit switch or a door
switch. Those switches exist because the alternative failure modes involve
combustion products, and in a house at this altitude a combustion problem develops
faster than at sea level.

### What a technician checks that a reset does not

A reset clears a count. It does not tell anyone why the count reached its limit.
The checks that follow are the ignitor's resistance, whether the flame sensor is
carboned, whether the pressure switch is closing, and whether the vent is clear.
On a house at 6,035 feet the vent matters more than most people expect, because a
partially obstructed flue shows up as intermittent lockouts long before it shows
up as anything dramatic.

### The one that is not a fault at all

A furnace that stopped during a power flicker may simply need the sequence above
and nothing more. Brownouts are hard on control boards, and a board that reset
mid-cycle can sit waiting rather than restarting on its own.

## q_furnace_leaking_water_lede

Water under a furnace usually means it is a high-efficiency condensing unit whose drain is blocked, not that a pipe has burst. Which type you own decides the whole answer.

## q_furnace_leaking_water_body

### First, which kind of furnace is it

High-efficiency condensing furnaces, generally 90 percent AFUE and above, extract
enough heat from the exhaust that water vapor condenses. That water is a normal
byproduct and a drain carries it away. These units have PVC exhaust piping rather
than metal. A standard-efficiency furnace vented in metal produces no condensate
at all, and water near one is coming from somewhere else.

### On a condensing furnace, it is the drain

The usual cause is a blocked condensate drain or trap. The line runs full,
backs up into the collector box, and overflows. Algae and dust build up over a
season. Most units carry a float switch that shuts the burner off when the water
rises, which is why this often arrives as no heat rather than as a puddle.

### On a standard furnace, look upward and sideways

Water at a non-condensing furnace comes from a humidifier connected to the system,
from the evaporator coil of an air conditioner sitting in the same cabinet, or from
a flue problem letting condensation form in the venting. That third one matters:
an improperly sized or sloped flue can condense inside the metal, which corrodes it
from the inside.

### Why the venting angle is worth checking here

Colorado Springs runs long, cold heating seasons at altitude, and the combination
puts flue temperatures nearer to their condensing point on marginal installations.
A flue that performs adequately in a milder climate can be the source of water in
one that runs harder for more months.

### What gets done about it

The drain and trap are cleared and flushed, the float switch is tested, and the
collector box is checked for cracks. If the water turns out to be flue
condensation the fix is in the venting rather than in the furnace, and that is a
different conversation than a blocked drain.

### Why it often arrives as no heat instead of a puddle

Most condensing furnaces carry a float switch in the collector box or the drain
line. When water backs up, that switch opens and shuts the burner down before the
cabinet overflows. The homeowner sees a furnace that will not fire, not water on
the floor. If a unit stopped heating and there is any dampness around it, the
drain is the first thing to look at rather than the ignitor.

### The seasonal pattern worth knowing

Condensate problems cluster at the start of the heating season, after the drain
has sat unused through summer and accumulated growth. A furnace that worked in
April and leaks in October usually has a drain that dried, crusted and blocked
rather than a component that failed.

## q_furnace_wont_ignite_lede

A furnace that will not ignite has failed at one of three points: it is not being told to start, it has no fuel, or a safety is refusing to let it. Each looks different at the unit.

## q_furnace_wont_ignite_body

### Watch the startup sequence

A modern furnace starts in a fixed order. The inducer fan spins up, a pressure
switch confirms it is moving air, the ignitor glows, the gas valve opens, the
flame sensor confirms a flame, and the blower starts. Watching which step it
reaches narrows the fault immediately, and it is the reason a technician stands
and watches rather than replacing parts.

### If the inducer never runs

Nothing is calling for heat, or the board is not responding. That is a
thermostat, a wiring, a door switch or a board problem. It is not an ignition
problem, and replacing an ignitor will not touch it.

### If the inducer runs but the ignitor never glows

The pressure switch may not be closing, which usually means a blocked vent, a
disconnected hose, or a failed switch. Or the ignitor itself is open. A hot
surface ignitor is a brittle carbide element that cracks with age; a cracked one
often looks intact.

### If it glows and no flame follows

Gas is not arriving or is not being released. That is a gas valve, a supply
problem, or a control fault. It is also the step where lockout happens, because
the board will retry a set number of times and then stop.

### If it lights and then dies

That is the flame sensor. It carbons over and stops confirming a flame that is
plainly there, so the board closes the gas within seconds. It is among the most
common furnace faults and among the cheapest to fix.

### The altitude note

Combustion air is thinner at 6,035 feet, and a furnace that was never derated for
altitude at installation runs richer than intended. That shows up as marginal
ignition and sooted burners long before it shows up as a failure, and it is worth
confirming on any unit with a history of ignition trouble.

### What gets replaced, and what only gets cleaned

Flame sensors are usually cleaned before they are replaced, and often that is the
entire repair. Hot surface ignitors are replaced rather than cleaned, because
handling the element with bare fingers shortens its life. Pressure switches are
tested before replacement, since the switch is frequently reporting a real vent
problem rather than failing itself.

### Lockout is information, not a malfunction

A board that has locked out has counted several failed attempts and stopped. That
count is diagnostic: a furnace locking out on the first cold night of the season
behaves differently from one locking out intermittently for weeks, and the two
point at different parts.

## q_furnace_not_turning_on_lede

Before any part is suspected, a furnace that is completely dead is usually a power, thermostat or door problem. Those three account for most of these calls and none of them is a repair.

## q_furnace_not_turning_on_body

### Power first, and there are two switches

There is a breaker, and there is a switch on or beside the furnace that looks
exactly like a light switch. That second one gets flipped by people looking for a
basement light and it is the single most common cause of a furnace that appears
dead. Check both before anything else.

### The blower door

Nearly every furnace has an interlock behind the blower access panel. If the panel
is not fully seated the furnace will not start, and there is no error indication to
explain why. If anyone has had a panel off recently, seat it properly.

### The thermostat

A thermostat with dead batteries, set to cool, or set below room temperature will
never call for heat. On a system with a C wire a loose connection produces
intermittent behavior that looks like a furnace fault. Setting the thermostat well
above room temperature and listening for a click at the furnace separates the two.

### The condensate float on a high-efficiency unit

If the furnace has PVC exhaust piping it makes condensate, and a full drain trips a
float switch that cuts the burner. A unit that stopped working with no other
symptom, particularly mid-season when the drain has had months to accumulate, is
worth checking there.

### What is left

If power is present, the door is shut, the thermostat is calling and the drain is
clear, the fault is in the board, the transformer or the low-voltage wiring. That
is where diagnosis starts costing something, and it is also where guessing gets
expensive.

### The pattern that points at the board

A furnace that starts sometimes, or that runs after a reset and then dies days
later, is behaving differently from one that is simply dead. Intermittent behavior
points at a board, a loose connection or a marginal transformer.

### Why it is worth ruling out the free causes first

Power, door and thermostat cost nothing to check and account for a large share of
these calls. Working through them before a visit means a technician arrives to
diagnose the remaining possibilities rather than to flip a switch.

### Before calling, note what it does

Whether the inducer spins, whether anything glows, whether there is a click at
the thermostat, and whether any status light on the board is flashing a code. Those
four observations shorten a visit considerably, and the flashing code in particular
often names the fault outright.

## q_furnace_pilot_light_out_lede

A standing pilot that will not stay lit is nearly always a thermocouple. Newer furnaces have no pilot at all, so the first question is which kind of furnace this is.

## q_furnace_pilot_light_out_body

### Only older furnaces have one

A standing pilot is a small flame that burns continuously. Furnaces built in
roughly the last twenty-five years use electronic ignition instead and have no
pilot to relight. If there is no pilot assembly, the problem is an ignitor or a
flame sensor and the advice for pilots does not apply.

### What the thermocouple does

The thermocouple is a probe sitting in the pilot flame. Heat generates a small
voltage, and that voltage holds the gas valve open. It is a safety: if the pilot
goes out, the voltage disappears and the gas shuts off. When the thermocouple
weakens with age, it stops producing enough voltage to hold the valve, and the
pilot dies within seconds of releasing the button. That exact symptom is the
classic signature.

### Drafts, dirt and the pilot orifice

A pilot that burns weak or yellow rather than sharp and blue is not heating the
thermocouple properly. The orifice may be partially blocked, or the flame may be
getting disturbed by a draft. A pilot that will not stay lit in windy weather but
holds otherwise points at venting rather than at the thermocouple.

### Why this matters more at altitude

Thinner air means less oxygen for the same flame. A pilot that would be adequate
at sea level can burn lazily at 6,035 feet, particularly if the furnace was never
adjusted for altitude when it was installed. Pilot problems on Colorado Springs
equipment are worth checking against the altitude setup rather than treating as a
worn part alone.

### When to stop

Relighting a pilot once is reasonable and the instructions are usually printed
inside the furnace door. If it will not hold, or there is any gas odor, stop.
Repeated relighting attempts on a valve that is not holding put unburned gas into
a cabinet.

### Replacing it is routine, adjusting it is not

A thermocouple is an inexpensive part and a straightforward replacement. The part
of the job that takes judgment is confirming the pilot flame is the right size and
shape afterward, because a correctly replaced thermocouple in a poor flame will
fail again on the same schedule.

### What replacing it does not fix

If the gas valve itself is failing, a new thermocouple changes nothing, because the
valve is not holding regardless of the voltage reaching it. That distinction is
made by measuring the thermocouple's output rather than by swapping the part and
seeing what happens.

## q_furnace_blower_not_working_lede

When the burner lights but no air moves, the fault is on the circulating side: the blower motor, its capacitor, or the board that switches it. Each fails with a different signature.

## q_furnace_blower_not_working_body

### Heat without air is a short list

If the burner lights and the heat exchanger warms but nothing comes out of the
registers, the combustion side is fine. That leaves the blower motor, the run
capacitor that starts it, the control board relay that switches it, or a wiring
fault between them.

### The capacitor is the usual suspect

A run capacitor stores the charge that gets a motor turning. When it weakens, the
motor hums but does not start, or starts only after a nudge. The hum is the tell.
Capacitors are inexpensive and they fail far more often than motors do, which is
why a technician tests one before condemning the other.

### The motor itself

A blower motor that has seized, or whose bearings have worn, draws current above
its rating and heats. Many have internal thermal overloads that shut them down and
reset once cool, which produces a blower that works for a while and then stops.
Intermittent blower operation with a burning smell points here.

### The board and the fan limit

The control board switches the blower on a timer after the burner lights, and off
after the heat exchanger cools. A failed relay on the board leaves the burner
working and the blower dead. On older units a mechanical fan limit switch does the
same job and fails the same way.

### Why the safety shuts the burner down anyway

A furnace running with no airflow overheats the heat exchanger within minutes. The
high limit switch opens and cuts the gas, so what usually gets reported is short
bursts of heat rather than a permanently silent blower. That is the safety working,
and running the furnace repeatedly in that state is how exchangers crack.

### The altitude and duct angle

At 6,035 feet a blower moves less mass of air per revolution than the same blower
at sea level. A system that was marginal on duct sizing at installation has less
margin here, and a motor working against restricted return air runs hotter and
fails sooner than its rating suggests.

### What gets tested, in what order

The capacitor is measured against its rated microfarads, the motor's amp draw is
compared to its nameplate, and the board's blower output is checked for voltage
during a call for heat. That order matters because it moves from cheapest to most
expensive, and it is the difference between a capacitor repair and a motor
replacement quoted on a guess.

## q_what_size_furnace_do_i_need_lede

Furnace sizing in Colorado Springs is not the nameplate number. Code requires the input to be derated for altitude, and a unit sized on its label is undersized once installed.

## q_what_size_furnace_do_i_need_body

### The code rule, stated plainly

The International Fuel Gas Code, section 303.3, requires appliance input ratings to
be derated 4 percent for every 1,000 feet of elevation above 2,000 feet. Colorado
Springs is around 6,035 feet. That is 4,035 feet above the threshold, roughly four
increments, and a derate near 16 percent.

### What that does to a real number

A furnace with a 100,000 BTU nameplate input delivers approximately 84,000 BTU of
input here. Output is lower again, because efficiency is applied after that: at 80
percent AFUE, the same unit produces roughly 67,000 BTU of usable heat. A house
that genuinely needs 80,000 BTU of output is not served by a 100,000 BTU nameplate
in this city.

### Why oversizing is not the safe answer

The instinct is to buy larger. An oversized furnace satisfies the thermostat
quickly, shuts off, and restarts a few minutes later. That is short cycling, and
it wears the ignitor and the blower faster while heating the house unevenly,
because the air never runs long enough to mix. It also costs more to buy and more
to run.

### What a real calculation uses

A load calculation, commonly a Manual J, works from the actual building: square
footage, insulation values, window area and orientation, ceiling height, air
leakage and the local design temperature. Colorado Springs has a cold design
temperature and a long season, so the load is real, but so is the derate, and the
two are separate adjustments applied to different numbers.

### The question worth asking about an existing furnace

If the current unit was replaced like for like at some point, it may have inherited
a size chosen before anyone considered the altitude derate, or chosen by rule of
thumb from square footage alone. Confirming what the house actually requires
matters more at replacement time than matching what came out of it.

### Two-stage equipment and the sizing question

A two-stage furnace runs a lower output most of the time and a higher one when
conditions demand it. In a long, cold season that is a genuine advantage, because
it lengthens run times without oversizing the peak. It does not remove the need
for a load calculation - it widens the range of loads a correctly sized unit can
serve comfortably.

### What to ask when a size is quoted

Whether the number quoted is nameplate input or derated input, and whether a load
calculation was performed on this house rather than estimated from its square
footage. Those two questions separate a sized system from a guessed one.

## q_furnace_smells_like_burning_lede

A burning smell from a furnace is either dust on a heat exchanger at the start of the season, which passes, or something electrical, which does not. Telling them apart is a matter of how long it lasts.

## q_furnace_smells_like_burning_body

### The one that is normal

The first time a furnace runs each autumn it burns off dust that settled on the
heat exchanger over summer. The smell is dusty rather than acrid, it appears
within minutes of the first firing, and it fades over an hour or two of running.
In Colorado Springs that first firing usually comes in October, and this is the
most common version of this question.

### The one that is not

An electrical smell is sharp, chemical and often described as burning plastic. It
does not fade. It points at overheating wiring, a failing blower motor, or a
control board component. That is a shut-it-off situation rather than a wait-and-see
one, because the failure mode is inside a cabinet full of wiring.

### Burning oil or hot metal

A motor whose bearings have worn produces a hot-metal or hot-oil smell as it runs
above its intended temperature. It is frequently accompanied by a change in the
sound of the blower and, eventually, by intermittent operation as a thermal
overload cuts in and resets.

### A filter that has gone too long

A severely restricted filter starves the return and lets the heat exchanger run
hot enough to scorch dust in the cabinet. The smell reads as burning, and the
underlying condition is the same one that eventually cracks exchangers. Changing
the filter is worth doing before anything else is considered.

### Anything that smells like rotten eggs

That is not burning and it is not a furnace diagnosis question. Natural gas is
odorized with a sulfur compound specifically so it is unmistakable. Leave, then
call the gas utility from outside the house.

### What a visit checks

Burner condition, wiring and connections at the board and motor, blower amp draw,
the heat exchanger surface, and the filter and return sizing. The purpose is to
separate the seasonal dust answer from the electrical one with evidence rather
than by waiting to see whether it goes away.

### The timing test

Dust burn-off appears on the first firing of the season and is gone within a couple
of hours of run time. Anything that appears mid-season, persists across days, or
returns every cycle is not dust and should be treated as electrical until proven
otherwise.

### Why waiting is the wrong instinct

The seasonal answer is common enough that people assume it. An electrical smell
that gets waited out is a wiring fault continuing to heat inside a closed cabinet,
and the cost of being wrong in that direction is considerably higher than the cost
of a visit.

## q_furnace_keeps_shutting_off_lede

A furnace that starts, runs briefly, and shuts down is usually being stopped by a safety switch rather than failing on its own. Which safety is doing it decides the repair.

## q_furnace_keeps_shutting_off_body

### The furnace is not quitting, it is being stopped

Modern furnaces carry several switches whose only job is to interrupt the burner
when a condition is out of range. When one opens, the furnace shuts down cleanly.
That behavior looks like a failure and is usually the system protecting the heat
exchanger.

### The high limit switch and airflow

The most common cause is restricted airflow. A dirty filter, a closed return, or
undersized ductwork means the blower cannot carry heat away fast enough. The
exchanger climbs past its limit, the switch opens, the gas stops, and the blower
keeps running to cool it. Cycle length gets shorter as the restriction worsens.

### The flame sensor

If the burner lights and dies within a few seconds, every time, that is the flame
sensor rather than a limit. The board is not detecting the flame and closes the gas
valve as designed. Cleaning the sensor is a routine repair and frequently the whole
job.

### The pressure switch and venting

The pressure switch confirms the inducer is moving exhaust before the burner is
allowed to light. A partially blocked flue, a disconnected sensing hose, or a
switch that has drifted out of calibration causes shutdowns during the startup
sequence rather than mid-cycle.

### Condensate on a high-efficiency unit

If the exhaust piping is PVC, the furnace makes condensate, and a blocked drain
trips a float switch that stops the burner. This one is seasonal: the drain sits
unused all summer and blocks on the first sustained run of autumn.

### Why it matters here specifically

A furnace at 6,035 feet is already working with thinner combustion air, and one
that was never derated at installation runs closer to its limits than the
nameplate suggests. Repeated limit trips on such a unit are not a nuisance to be
reset around; they are the condition that cracks heat exchangers.

### What the board will tell you

Most furnaces flash a diagnostic code on a small LED behind the access panel, and
the legend is usually printed inside the door. That code names which safety opened,
which turns a guessing exercise into a specific repair.

### The check that costs nothing

Replace the filter and open every supply and return register in the house, then run
a full cycle. If the shutdowns stop, the fault was airflow and the filter interval
needs shortening for this climate rather than a part needing replacement.

## q_cracked_heat_exchanger_signs_lede

A cracked heat exchanger is the failure that ends a furnace rather than generating a repair. The signs are indirect, which is why it gets found during inspection rather than reported by a homeowner.

## q_cracked_heat_exchanger_signs_body

### What the part does and why a crack matters

The heat exchanger is the metal barrier between the burner flame and the air that
goes into the house. Combustion happens on one side, breathable air passes over the
other, and the two never mix. A crack removes that separation, which is why this is
treated as a safety condition rather than an efficiency one.

### It is rarely visible

Cracks form at stress points inside the exchanger, often at bends and welds, and
they frequently open only when the metal expands at operating temperature and close
again when it cools. A visual inspection of a cold furnace can miss one entirely.
That is why technicians use combustion analysis and inspection cameras rather than
looking through the burner opening.

### The indirect signs

A flame that changes shape or flickers when the blower starts is the classic one:
air is moving through a passage it should not reach. Soot around the burners,
persistent corrosion streaks on the exchanger surface, and a strong chemical or
formaldehyde-like odor during operation all point the same direction.

### Carbon monoxide is a symptom, not the test

A CO alarm sounding near a furnace is a reason to leave and call, but a silent
alarm does not clear a furnace. Household alarms are calibrated to alert at levels
and durations set for occupant safety, not to detect the low-level spillage an
early crack produces.

### Why age and altitude both matter

Exchangers crack from thermal cycling, so a furnace that has run more cycles has
had more chances. Colorado Springs has a long heating season, and a unit never
derated for 6,035 feet has been running richer and hotter than intended for its
whole service life. Both push the same part toward the same end.

### What happens when one is found

A cracked exchanger is not patched. On a unit still under warranty the part may be
covered while the labor is not, which frequently still argues for replacement. On
an older furnace the repair rarely makes economic sense against a new unit.

### What to do in the meantime

If a crack is suspected the furnace is shut off at the switch rather than run until
a decision is made. That is inconvenient in a Colorado winter, and it is the correct
sequence.

### The inspection is worth doing on schedule

Because the early signs are invisible and the late ones are not subtle, this is the
single strongest argument for an annual inspection on a furnace past ten years old.

## q_furnace_short_cycling_lede

Short cycling means the furnace runs briefly, shuts off, and restarts a few minutes later. It is either oversized for the house or overheating, and the two have opposite fixes.

## q_furnace_short_cycling_body

### What counts as short cycling

A furnace in steady cold weather should run long, even cycles, typically ten to
fifteen minutes or more. Cycles of three to five minutes, repeating, are short
cycling. It matters because most of the wear on an ignitor and a blower happens at
startup, so a furnace doing four cycles an hour ages far faster than one doing two.

### The oversizing cause

A furnace with more capacity than the house needs satisfies the thermostat quickly
and shuts down before the air in the ducts and rooms has mixed. Then the
thermostat drops and it fires again. Nothing is broken. This pattern is present from
the day of installation and persists in mild and cold weather alike.

### The overheating cause

A restricted filter, closed registers or undersized return ductwork prevents the
blower from carrying heat away. The exchanger reaches its limit, the switch opens,
and the burner stops while the fan continues. This one gets worse over a season as
the filter loads and is often absent right after a filter change.

### Telling them apart

Overheating cycles get shorter as the filter dirties and improve immediately after
a change. Oversizing does not respond to filters at all and is worst in mild
weather, when the load is smallest relative to the furnace's output. That
difference is diagnostic and costs nothing to observe.

### Thermostat placement, the overlooked one

A thermostat in direct sun, above a supply register, or on an exterior wall reads a
temperature the rest of the house is not experiencing. It satisfies early and calls
again quickly. Moving it is sometimes the whole repair.

### Flame sensor and pressure switch faults

A furnace shutting down within seconds of lighting, repeatedly, is not short
cycling in the sizing sense. That is a flame sensor or a pressure switch stopping
the burner, and it belongs to a different diagnosis.

### The altitude connection

Sizing here should account for the code derate of 4 percent per 1,000 feet above
2,000 feet, which at 6,035 feet is roughly 16 percent. A unit chosen on nameplate
capacity without that adjustment is more oversized in practice than the label
suggests, and short cycling in mild weather is the visible result.

### What a fix actually looks like

Airflow problems are corrected with filters, register positions and, where needed,
return duct changes. Oversizing is not correctable on existing equipment; it is
managed with a two-stage or modulating unit at replacement time, which runs longer
cycles at lower output.

## q_furnace_is_loud_lede

Furnace noise is diagnostic. Each type of sound comes from a specific part, and describing it accurately narrows the repair before anyone opens the cabinet.

## q_furnace_is_loud_body

### Banging or booming at startup

A delayed ignition. Gas accumulates for a moment before it lights, and the sound is
that small volume igniting at once. It is caused by dirty burners, a weak ignitor,
or a gas pressure problem, and it is worth attention rather than tolerance: repeated
delayed ignition stresses the heat exchanger directly.

### Metallic scraping

A blower wheel contacting its housing, or a worn motor bearing letting the wheel
sit off center. This one gets worse quickly and can damage the wheel and housing if
run, so it is the noise most worth shutting off for.

### Squealing

Usually a belt on older belt-driven blowers, or dry bearings on a direct-drive
motor. On a belt system the belt is replaced and tensioned. On sealed bearings the
motor is the repair, since they are not serviceable.

### Rattling

Loose panels, unsecured ductwork, or a screw that has backed out. Frequently the
cheapest fix in this list, and worth checking before assuming anything internal.

### Buzzing or humming

Electrical rather than mechanical. A transformer, a contactor, or a blower motor
that is receiving power and not turning because its capacitor has failed. A motor
that hums without starting is the classic capacitor signature.

### Whistling

Air moving through a restriction, most often a dirty filter or a return that is too
small for the blower. It is the audible version of the airflow problem that trips
high limit switches, and the two frequently arrive together.

### Rumbling after shutdown

Burners continuing to burn off residual gas, or an expanding and contracting
exchanger. Some of this is normal on cooldown; rumbling that continues well after
the burner has stopped is not.

### What to record before calling

When the noise happens in the cycle - at startup, during the run, at shutdown, or
continuously - and whether it has changed over time. That timing narrows the list
faster than any description of the sound itself.

### Which noises mean stop, and which mean schedule

Scraping metal and any banging at ignition mean shut the furnace off and have it
looked at. Rattling panels, whistling filters and mild hum are worth scheduling but
not worth losing heat over in a Colorado winter.

### Why noise changes at altitude

A blower moving thinner air runs at a different load point than the same unit at
sea level, and duct velocities that would be quiet elsewhere can whistle here. It
is worth knowing before assuming a new noise means a new fault.

## q_furnace_not_blowing_hot_air_lede

Air moving without heat means the blower works and the burner side does not. It is the same family of faults as cold air at the registers, and the sequence at the unit tells you which one.

## q_furnace_not_blowing_hot_air_body

### Confirm it is actually not heating

A furnace early in its cycle moves air that has not warmed yet, and on a cold
Colorado morning the difference between lukewarm and cold is easy to misjudge. Let
a full cycle run before diagnosing. If the air warms after a few minutes, nothing
is wrong.

### The fan setting

A thermostat fan switch left on ON runs the blower continuously, including when
the burner is off. That produces room-temperature air at the registers between
cycles and is the most common non-fault explanation.

### The flame sensor

If the burner lights and then goes out within seconds while the fan continues, the
flame sensor has carboned and can no longer confirm the flame. The board closes the
gas as a safety response. Cleaning it is routine and often the entire repair.

### Ignition failure and lockout

If the burner never lights at all, the ignitor or the gas supply is the issue. After
a set number of attempts the board locks out and stops trying, though on many units
the blower still runs. That combination reads as a furnace blowing cold air
indefinitely.

### Overheating and the limit switch

A restricted filter or closed returns let the exchanger overheat. The limit switch
opens, the gas stops, and the blower keeps running to cool the metal. This produces
brief warmth followed by a long stretch of cool air, repeating.

### Duct losses in an unconditioned space

Air that leaves the furnace warm can arrive cool if the ductwork runs through an
unheated crawlspace or attic and is poorly sealed or insulated. That shows as
noticeably cooler air at the registers furthest from the unit while nearer ones are
fine.

### What to note before a visit

Whether the burner lights at all, how long heat lasts before it stops, and whether
the pattern changed after a filter replacement. Those three answers separate
ignition faults from airflow faults without opening anything.

### Why this one is worth a visit rather than a wait

Every cause on this list except the fan setting involves the burner side shutting
down, and two of them - overheating and repeated ignition attempts - put stress on
the heat exchanger each time they repeat. A furnace doing this daily is not stable,
it is cycling through a fault.

## q_furnace_buzzing_lede

A buzz is electrical almost every time. It comes from a transformer, a contactor, or a motor drawing power without turning, and the last of those is the one that gets expensive if ignored.

## q_furnace_buzzing_body

### Buzzing is not the same as humming or rattling

A rattle is loose sheet metal. A rumble is combustion. A buzz is the sound of
alternating current in a component, and it narrows the search to the electrical
side of the furnace immediately.

### The transformer

Every furnace has a transformer stepping line voltage down for the controls. An
aging transformer buzzes continuously whenever the furnace has power, whether or
not it is heating. Continuous buzzing that does not follow the heating cycle points
here.

### A motor that will not start

This is the important one. When a run capacitor fails, the blower or inducer motor
receives power but cannot begin turning. It draws heavily and buzzes. Left running,
it heats and can burn out, turning an inexpensive capacitor into a motor
replacement. Buzzing that coincides with a call for heat, with no air movement,
should be shut off rather than left running.

### The gas valve

Some gas valves buzz audibly when energized. On many units this is normal and has
been present since installation. A valve that has recently started buzzing, or
buzzes louder than it used to, is worth checking.

### Loose electrical connections

A connection that has worked loose arcs slightly under load, which produces both a
buzz and heat. This is the version of the problem worth taking seriously, because
the failure mode is inside a cabinet with wiring and combustion.

### How to place it in the cycle

Note whether the buzz is constant, or starts when the thermostat calls, or starts
when the blower engages. Constant points at the transformer. Buzzing at the call
with no inducer movement points at that motor or its capacitor. Buzzing when the
blower should start points at the blower capacitor.

### What not to do

Do not run a furnace that buzzes with no corresponding air movement, and do not
keep resetting it. The motor is heating each time, and the cheap repair becomes the
expensive one on roughly that schedule.

### What testing looks like

Capacitors are measured against their rated microfarads, motors against their
nameplate amp draw, and connections are checked under load rather than cold. That
order finds the cheap fault first, which is the point.

### The seasonal timing

Buzzing that appears on the first cold run of the season often means a capacitor
that weakened over summer and no longer has enough charge to start a motor against
a cold start. It is a common autumn call in this climate.

## q_what_furnace_repair_costs_lede

Furnace repair cost is driven by which part failed, whether it is a stocked item, and whether the visit happens in normal hours. Those three things move the number far more than the brand on the cabinet.

## q_what_furnace_repair_costs_body

### The parts that are cheap, and why

Flame sensors, thermocouples, capacitors and ignitors are small, commonly stocked,
and quick to change. They are also the most frequent furnace failures, which means
the most common repair on a furnace is at the low end of the range. A technician
carrying these on the truck resolves the majority of no-heat calls in a single
visit.

### The parts in the middle

Blower motors, inducer motors, gas valves, pressure switches and control boards are
larger, more expensive, and sometimes model-specific. Availability matters here: a
part in stock is a same-day repair, and one that has to be ordered turns a single
visit into two, which changes the labor as well as the timeline.

### The one that ends the conversation

A cracked heat exchanger is not repaired. On a unit still inside its parts warranty
the exchanger itself may be covered while the labor to replace it is not, and that
labor is substantial because the furnace is largely disassembled to reach it. On an
older furnace the arithmetic almost always points at replacement.

### What actually moves the price

Whether the part is stocked, whether the call is during normal hours or overnight
on the coldest night of the year, how accessible the unit is, and whether
diagnosis found one fault or a chain of them. A furnace that failed because of a
restricted return has two problems, and fixing only the visible one guarantees a
second visit.

### The age threshold worth applying

A common test is whether a repair costs more than about a third of a replacement
on a unit past its expected service life. That is a guideline rather than a rule,
and it gets overridden in both directions by warranty status and by whether parts
are still manufactured for the model.

### What a quote should tell you

Which part failed, why it failed, whether anything caused it, and the price before
work begins. A quote that names a price without naming a cause is describing a part
swap rather than a repair.

### Where the actual figures are

Local cost ranges, including what the city charges when a job requires a permit,
are set out on the pricing page for this market rather than estimated here.

### Diagnosis is not the same as the repair

Most of what a technician does on a no-heat call is determining which of a dozen
things failed. That is the part that prevents a wrong part being fitted, and it is
why a price quoted before anyone has seen the furnace is a guess.

## q_furnace_fan_wont_turn_off_lede

A blower that runs continuously is usually a thermostat set to ON rather than AUTO. If the setting is right, the cause is a fan limit switch or a control board relay.

## q_furnace_fan_wont_turn_off_body

### Check the thermostat setting first

Every thermostat has a fan option with ON and AUTO. On ON, the blower runs
continuously regardless of whether the furnace is heating. Some people set it
deliberately for air circulation and filtration and forget. This accounts for most
of these questions and it is not a fault.

### The normal cooldown run

After the burner stops, the blower keeps running for a period to move remaining
heat out of the exchanger. That is designed behavior and protects the metal. It
typically lasts a minute or two. A fan that runs for five or ten minutes after
every cycle is at the long end of normal rather than broken.

### The fan limit switch

On older furnaces a mechanical fan limit switch turns the blower on once the
exchanger warms and off once it cools. When it sticks closed, the blower never
stops. These are adjustable and replaceable, and a stuck one is a common cause on
equipment of that generation.

### The control board relay

Newer furnaces do the same job with a relay on the control board. A relay whose
contacts have welded closed leaves the blower energized permanently. That is a
board repair, and it is diagnosed by checking whether the board is still sending
voltage to the motor with no call for heat.

### Wiring at the thermostat

A shorted or miswired G terminal, the one that controls the fan, energizes the
blower continuously. This turns up after a thermostat replacement more often than
at any other time, so a fan that started running non-stop right after a new
thermostat went in has a likely cause.

### Is it harmful to leave

Continuous operation is not dangerous, but it wears the blower motor, uses
electricity, and in a dry Colorado winter it moves a great deal of air across the
house, which affects humidity noticeably. It is worth fixing rather than living
with.

### Which one it is, quickly

Set the thermostat fan to AUTO and turn the system off entirely. If the blower
keeps running with the system off, it is the limit switch, the relay or the
wiring, and not the setting.

### If it turns out to be the setting

Running the fan continuously is a legitimate choice for filtration and for evening
out temperatures between floors. If that was deliberate, the only thing worth
revisiting is the filter interval, because a continuously running blower loads a
filter considerably faster.

## q_bad_furnace_ignitor_signs_lede

A failed hot surface ignitor is the most common no-heat call of the winter. It is identified by watching whether it glows and by measuring its resistance, not by looking at it.

## q_bad_furnace_ignitor_signs_body

### What the part is

Modern furnaces light the burner with a hot surface ignitor, a small silicon
carbide or silicon nitride element that glows orange when energized. It is
consumable. It cracks with thermal cycling and with handling, and most fail within
a few years of normal use rather than lasting the life of the furnace.

### The primary test is visual, during startup

Watch the furnace through a cycle. The inducer fan runs, then the ignitor should
glow bright orange for several seconds before the gas valve opens. If nothing
glows, the ignitor is open, has lost power, or the board is not energizing it. That
one observation separates an ignitor fault from everything downstream of it.

### A cracked ignitor frequently looks fine

Hairline cracks in the element are often invisible. That is why an ignitor is
tested with a meter rather than judged by appearance. A good one reads a specific
resistance range that varies by type; an open circuit reads infinite and is
conclusive.

### Lockout is the usual symptom

The board attempts ignition a set number of times, gets no flame, and locks out.
The furnace goes quiet and stays quiet until power is cycled. A unit that runs
after a reset and then locks out again the same evening is behaving exactly like a
failed ignitor.

### Do not handle the element

Oil from skin creates a hot spot on the element surface and shortens the life of a
new ignitor considerably. They are handled by the ceramic base or with a cloth,
which is a small detail that decides whether a replacement lasts years or months.

### What else produces the same symptom

A pressure switch that is not closing stops the sequence before the ignitor is ever
energized, which looks identical from across the room. So does a blocked vent. That
is why the sequence is watched rather than assumed, and why the ignitor is
confirmed rather than replaced first.

### Why they fail sooner here

An ignitor's life is measured in cycles. A long Colorado heating season puts more
cycles on the part each winter than a mild climate does, and a furnace that short
cycles puts on several times more again. An ignitor failing every year or two is
usually reporting a cycling problem rather than a defective part.

### Replacing it is routine

The part is inexpensive and the job is short. What decides whether it lasts is
handling it correctly and confirming nothing upstream caused the failure.

## q_furnace_not_blowing_air_lede

No air at the registers with the furnace apparently running is a blower problem, a filter problem, or a duct problem. The three are separated by whether the blower can be heard at all.

## q_furnace_not_blowing_air_body

### Listen at the furnace first

Stand at the unit during a call for heat. If the blower is audibly running, air is
being moved and the problem is between the furnace and the rooms. If it is silent
or humming without turning, the problem is the blower itself. That single check
splits the diagnosis in half.

### If the blower is not running

A failed run capacitor is the most common cause, and it produces a hum without
rotation. A seized motor or a worn bearing does the same. A control board relay
that has failed leaves the motor unpowered and silent. All three present as heat
with no air, and the furnace will shut itself down on its high limit shortly after,
because an exchanger with no airflow overheats within minutes.

### If the blower is running

Then air is moving and something downstream is stopping it. A filter so loaded that
it has collapsed against its frame is the leading cause and the cheapest to rule
out. Closed or blocked registers, a closed damper, or a disconnected duct in a
crawlspace or attic will do the same.

### The disconnected duct

A flexible duct that has come off its collar dumps conditioned air into an
unconditioned space. The blower runs normally, the furnace behaves normally, and
one part of the house gets nothing. It is worth suspecting when the problem affects
some rooms and not others.

### Return air, the half people forget

Supply registers get attention and returns rarely do. A blocked or undersized
return starves the blower just as effectively as a dirty filter, and the symptom is
the same weak airflow everywhere.

### Why it escalates rather than persisting

A furnace running with restricted airflow overheats, trips its limit, and shuts the
burner down. So this problem usually arrives combined with short cycling or with
intermittent heat, and the airflow is the underlying cause of both.

### The check before calling

Replace the filter, open every supply and return in the house, and run a full
cycle. If airflow returns, it was the filter, and in this climate the interval
needs shortening. If it does not, the blower or the ductwork is the fault.

### Why airflow is the fault worth chasing

Restricted airflow is upstream of overheating, short cycling and, over enough
seasons, heat exchanger cracking. Fixing it is cheap now and expensive later, which
is an unusual combination worth acting on.

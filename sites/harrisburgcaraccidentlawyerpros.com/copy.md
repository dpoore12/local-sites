## symptom_1_title

The rear driver says the impact was minor, but your body says otherwise

## symptom_1

A rear-end collision often looks simple from the outside: one vehicle stopped or slowed, another vehicle struck it from behind. The insurance file may treat it the same way, especially when there is modest visible damage. That can be a mistake. The force on the bumper and the force on a person’s neck, shoulder, back, or head do not always tell the same story. Symptoms can appear after the adrenaline wears off, and a gap in treatment gives an insurer room to argue that something else caused the problem.

Start with the things that are hardest to recreate later. Save photographs of both vehicles before repairs or disposal. Keep the report number, tow and repair paperwork, and names of anyone who saw the impact or stopped afterward. Write down the traffic condition, whether you were stopped at a light or in a queue, and whether the other driver was distracted, following too closely, or reacting to an earlier collision. If there was a passenger, their account matters too.

Medical documentation is not just a stack of bills. It records when symptoms began, what care was recommended, whether work or daily activities changed, and whether the course of care is improving. Follow through with appropriate medical advice and keep a calendar of missed work, restricted movement, prescriptions, and follow-up appointments. Do not fill holes in the record by guessing later.

Then check your own Pennsylvania policy. A driver who chose limited tort may still recover out-of-pocket losses, but pain-and-suffering recovery can depend on the policy choice and any applicable exception. Full tort preserves broader rights to sue. That distinction makes the declaration page as important as the rear bumper photograph. Before signing a release, make sure the record reflects the collision, the treatment, the coverage, and the consequences that are actually known.

## symptom_2_title

The driver left, or their insurance information leads nowhere

## symptom_2

A hit-and-run crash creates two immediate jobs: report the event and preserve every identifying detail while it is still fresh. Call law enforcement from the scene when it is safe to do so. Record the direction the other vehicle traveled, its color, make, body style, plate characters, damage, stickers, cargo, or anything else distinctive. Ask nearby businesses or homes whether exterior cameras may have captured the roadway; video is often overwritten quickly. A witness name and a clear statement taken early can matter far more than a later memory of a dark sedan.

Do not assume there is no claim just because the other driver has disappeared. Your own policy may include uninsured-motorist coverage, which can be relevant when a responsible driver cannot be identified or has no usable insurance. The actual policy language matters. Keep the declarations page, all correspondence, the police report number, repair estimates, photographs, and the medical record in one place. Notice from an insurer is not the same thing as a complete claim file.

There can also be coverage questions that have nothing to do with a hit-and-run driver’s intent. The vehicle might have been borrowed. The apparent owner may not be the person behind the wheel. A commercial delivery vehicle, rideshare vehicle, or work vehicle can introduce additional records and policies. That is why the first report should preserve details instead of narrowing the story to a license plate search.

Harrisburg drivers should also resist the urge to settle vehicle damage and injury issues in one rushed conversation. Repairs can be priced quickly. Physical symptoms, lost time, and future treatment may not be clear for weeks. A release may be final. Get the crash facts and coverage information organized before accepting language that ends the claim. ## symptom_3_title

The other driver has little insurance, or none at all

## symptom_3

“The other driver is uninsured” is a starting point, not a complete answer. First verify what coverage actually exists. A driver may have a policy that lapsed, limits that are quickly exhausted, a vehicle owned by someone else, or a job-related use that triggers another layer of insurance. The facts at the scene matter: who owned the vehicle, who was driving, why the vehicle was being used, and whether there were passengers or a business involved.

Next, look at your own insurance declarations rather than relying on a memory of buying a policy years ago. Pennsylvania auto policies can include uninsured-motorist and underinsured-motorist coverage. Those are separate coverage questions with their own notice requirements, definitions, offsets, and consent provisions. The page that lists your limits and selections is important evidence. Save it, along with every letter, email, recorded-statement request, estimate, bill, and explanation of benefits.

This is also where Pennsylvania’s tort selection deserves careful attention. Limited tort does not erase every loss. The Commonwealth’s insurance guide explains that it still allows recovery of out-of-pocket medical and other expenses, while limiting certain non-economic damages unless an exception applies. Full tort retains unrestricted rights to bring suit against a negligent party. No webpage can determine which rule controls a particular collision, but the choice should be identified before someone tells you that there is “nothing to pursue.”

Coverage problems become harder when the record is thin. Preserve the crash report, obtain vehicle photographs, keep provider records and work documentation, and write down the names of every insurer involved. Do not give up a claim because the first adjuster says the responsible driver has no insurance. The question is not merely whether one person carried a card at the scene. It is what policies, people, vehicles, and facts connect to the loss, and what your own contract says about a claim.

## symptom_4_title

The insurer’s first offer does not match the crash’s impact on your life

## symptom_4

An early offer can feel like progress after weeks of appointments, missed shifts, calls from repair shops, and unanswered mail. It may also arrive before the full picture exists. A settlement release commonly ends the claim, even if more treatment, a specialist referral, or time away from work comes later. The useful question is not whether an offer is better than nothing. Build the claim from records, not from a single demand number. If you could not perform normal work or household tasks, document the dates and the specific limitation instead of relying on a general recollection months later.

Liability still matters in an offer discussion. A rear-end crash, an intersection collision, a lane-change dispute, or a multi-car chain reaction may involve different versions of events. The report is important, but it is not the whole file. Photographs, witness accounts, vehicle data, roadway video, phone records when appropriate, and the damage pattern can all shape the evidence.

## qa_1_question

What does limited tort versus full tort mean after a Pennsylvania crash?

## qa_1_answer

Pennsylvania is unusual because the choice on an auto policy can shape a later injury claim. The Pennsylvania Insurance Department says limited tort generally preserves recovery of out-of-pocket medical and other expenses but restricts certain damages, including pain and suffering, unless a statutory exception applies. With full tort, the policyholder retains unrestricted rights to sue a negligent party. The answer in any particular crash depends on the policy, the people and vehicles involved, and the facts. Find the declarations page before you accept anyone’s description of your rights. It is a document to preserve with the crash report and medical record, not a detail to reconstruct after a release is signed.

## pricing_lede

Pennsylvania puts one hard percentage in its fee law, and it is not the one drivers expect: twenty percent belongs to workers' compensation, while an auto claim in Dauphin County is governed by a reasonableness rule with no ceiling at all, alongside statutory court costs and a required medical benefit that are fixed to the dollar.

## pricing_body

### The auto claim has no ceiling, and that is the finding

Pennsylvania Rule of Professional Conduct 1.5(a) says a lawyer shall not enter into an
agreement for, charge, or collect an illegal or clearly excessive fee. Eight factors decide
propriety. Is the fee fixed or does it ride on the result. How much time, labor, novelty
and skill did the matter demand. Did accepting it shut out other engagements. What does
comparable work command locally. How does the size of the claim compare with the outcome
produced. What deadlines did the client or the situation force. How deep and how long has
the professional relationship run. What is the lawyer's standing and ability. A comment to the rule acknowledges that other law may impose a
ceiling on the percentage. For a Harrisburg collision claim, no such law does.

### The twenty percent everyone half-remembers

Section 442 of the Workers' Compensation Act is where the number lives. Counsel fees
agreed between a claimant and counsel must be approved by the workers' compensation judge
or the board, and may not exceed twenty per centum of the amount awarded. The same section
caps fees at a fifth of a compromise and release settlement, and it lets a judge award a
reasonable fee without regard to any percentage where counsel's work produces a favorable
result with no immediate award, as in a termination or suspension fight.

That figure gets repeated on the street as though it governed every injury in
Pennsylvania. It does not. If a Camp Hill delivery driver is hit on Cameron Street while
working, the compensation side of that same crash is capped at a fifth and the third-party
claim against the other driver is not capped at all. Same crash, two rulebooks.

### The MCARE question, answered plainly

Pennsylvania's medical malpractice statute, the MCARE Act of 2002, is often assumed to cap
attorney fees the way California's malpractice statute does. It was read for this page. It
does not. Section 509 discusses periodic payment of future medical damages after the
proportionate share of counsel fees and costs is paid, and sets a $100,000 line below which
a claimant may object to periodic payments. Nowhere does the act state a percentage limit.

### What the paperwork must contain

Rule 1.5(b) requires that the basis or rate of the fee be communicated in writing to a
client the lawyer has not regularly represented. Rule 1.5(c) requires a contingent fee
agreement to be in writing stating how the fee is determined, the percentages for
settlement, trial and appeal, which case outlays are charged against the money collected,
and the order of operations between subtracting them and figuring the fee. It also requires
something people forget to ask for: at the conclusion of the matter, a written statement of
the outcome that shows the remittance and how it was determined. Request that statement
rather than a lump-sum check with a handwritten note.

### Court costs in Dauphin County are statutory, and small

A claim inside the $12,000 civil limit belongs before a magisterial district judge, where
the 2026 cost table set under 42 Pa.C.S. 1725.1 runs $68.50 for actions of $500 or less,
$91 above $500 through $2,000, $114 above $2,000 through $4,000, and $171 from $4,001 to
$12,000. An order of execution costs $51.50 and an objection to levy $23. Postage and
registered mail sit outside those figures and fall on the plaintiff. A larger claim moves
to the Court of Common Pleas at the courthouse on Market Street, where the prothonotary's
own fee schedule governs and should be checked at the counter before filing.

### The coverage number that decides who pays first

Every Pennsylvania auto liability policy must carry medical benefit coverage of at least
$5,000 under 75 Pa.C.S. 1711. That is the money treating an Allison Hill fender-bender in
the first weeks, long before any liability question is settled, and it is why a bill can
land in collections while a claim is open. Ask how the first-party benefit is being
coordinated, and get the answer in writing.

### What actually deserves scrutiny

Whether the percentage steps up, and at which docket event. Whether expenses are itemized
and whether an estimate precedes an expert retention. Whether the closing written statement
is promised. Whether a compensation claim is running beside the liability claim, because
the twenty percent limit applies to one of them and not the other, and a single blended
figure across both is a warning rather than a convenience.

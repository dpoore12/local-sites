## pricing_body

### The auto claim has no ceiling, and that is the finding

Pennsylvania Rule of Professional Conduct 1.5(a) says a lawyer shall not enter into an
agreement for, charge, or collect an illegal or clearly excessive fee. Eight factors decide
propriety. Is the fee fixed or does it ride on the result. How much time, labor, novelty
and skill did the matter demand. Did accepting it shut out other engagements. What does
comparable work command locally. How does the size of the claim compare with the outcome
produced. What deadlines did the client or the situation force. How deep and how long has
the professional relationship run. What is the lawyer's standing and ability. A comment to the rule acknowledges that other law may impose a
ceiling on the percentage. For a Harrisburg collision claim, no such law does.

### The twenty percent everyone half-remembers

Section 442 of the Workers' Compensation Act is where the number lives. Counsel fees
agreed between a claimant and counsel must be approved by the workers' compensation judge
or the board, and may not exceed twenty per centum of the amount awarded. The same section
caps fees at a fifth of a compromise and release settlement. It also lets a judge award a
reasonable fee without regard to any percentage where counsel's work produces a favorable
result with no immediate award, as in a termination or suspension fight.

That figure gets repeated on the street as though it governed every injury in
Pennsylvania. It does not. If a Camp Hill delivery driver is hit on Cameron Street while
working, the compensation side of that same crash is capped at a fifth and the third-party
claim against the other driver is not capped at all. Same crash, two rulebooks.

### The MCARE question, answered plainly

Pennsylvania's medical malpractice statute, the MCARE Act of 2002, is often assumed to cap
attorney fees the way California's malpractice statute does. It was read for this page. It
does not. Section 509 discusses periodic payment of future medical damages after the
proportionate share of counsel fees and costs is paid, and sets a $100,000 line below which
a claimant may object to periodic payments. Nowhere does the act state a percentage limit.

### What the paperwork must contain

Rule 1.5(b) requires that the basis or rate of the fee be communicated in writing to a
client the lawyer has not regularly represented. Rule 1.5(c) requires a contingent fee
agreement to be in writing stating how the fee is determined, the percentages for
settlement, trial and appeal, and which case outlays are charged against the money
collected. The agreement must also fix the order of operations between subtracting those
outlays and figuring the fee. The rule requires
something people forget to ask for: at the conclusion of the matter, a written statement of
the outcome that shows the remittance and how it was determined. Request that statement
rather than a lump-sum check with a handwritten note.

### Court costs in Dauphin County are statutory, and small

A claim inside the $12,000 civil limit belongs before a magisterial district judge, where
the 2026 cost table set under 42 Pa.C.S. 1725.1 runs $68.50 for actions of $500 or less,
$91 above $500 through $2,000, $114 above $2,000 through $4,000, and $171 from $4,001 to
$12,000. An order of execution costs $51.50 and an objection to levy $23. Postage and
registered mail sit outside those figures and fall on the plaintiff. A larger claim moves
to the Court of Common Pleas at the courthouse on Market Street, where the prothonotary's
own fee schedule governs and should be checked at the counter before filing.

### The coverage number that decides who pays first

Every Pennsylvania auto liability policy must carry medical benefit coverage of at least
$5,000 under 75 Pa.C.S. 1711. That is the money treating an Allison Hill fender-bender in
the first weeks, long before any liability question is settled, and it is why a bill can
land in collections while a claim is open. Ask how the first-party benefit is being
coordinated, and get the answer in writing.

### What actually deserves scrutiny

Whether the percentage steps up, and at which docket event. Whether expenses are itemized
and whether an estimate precedes an expert retention. Whether the closing written statement
is promised. Whether a compensation claim is running beside the liability claim, because
the twenty percent limit applies to one of them and not the other, and a single blended
figure across both is a warning rather than a convenience.

<h2>Reading the impact before anyone assigns blame</h2>
<p>Rear-end impacts accounted for 20,825 of the 110,765 crashes reported on Pennsylvania roads in 2024, and 81 of the deaths, according to PennDOT's crash facts volume. Frequency is not the same as simplicity. Look at your own vehicle before it leaves on a flatbed. Damage at both ends puts you in the middle of a chain reaction, and that position is the one neither of the other drivers can describe reliably. Check whether the seat back moved, whether the head restraint sat behind your skull or your shoulder blades, and whether the steel bar behind the plastic bumper cover is bent. A salvage yard has no reason to preserve the only physical record of the force involved.</p>
<h2>Where the queues form around the Capital Beltway</h2>
<p>A rear-end impact needs a fast road that stops without warning, and this valley builds those in two shapes. The first is the I-83 Capital Beltway. PennDOT's master plan for it covers roughly 11 miles from the New Cumberland Interchange at Exit 40B to the I-81 junction at Exit 51, through the Paxton Street, Eisenhower and Derry Street interchanges, split into four project sections, three of them inside this county. The second shape is a signal head. PennDOT counted 1,112 intersection crashes in Dauphin County in 2024, 427 of them at signalized intersections, which is where a queue on Cameron Street or Paxton Street ends in a line of stopped bumpers.</p>
<h2>The renewal notice that decides your damages</h2>
<p>Pennsylvania hands drivers a choice almost no other state does. Under 75 Pa.C.S. 1705, an insurer must send a standardized notice offering full tort and limited tort, and the statute's own words tell a limited tort buyer that recovery for pain and suffering is off the table unless the injury meets the policy's definition of serious injury. Section 1702 defines that as death, serious impairment of body function, or permanent serious disfigurement. Two details catch people. The election carries forward through every renewal and replacement policy until the insurer receives a signed form choosing the other option. And a driver who has no financial responsibility at all is deemed by the statute to have chosen limited tort.</p>
<h2>Five days, fifteen days, two years</h2>
<p>Three clocks start at the guardrail. If no police agency investigated a crash that had to be reported, 75 Pa.C.S. 3747(a) gives the driver five days to send PennDOT a written report, which is what the AA-600 form is for. State Police crash reports cost $22 and the application tells you not to send it until 15 days have passed since the crash. The long clock is 42 Pa.C.S. 5524, which allows two years to bring an action for injury caused by another's wrongful act or neglect. Two years feels generous in week one and disappears while a claim file is still open.</p>
<h2>What sinks these files in week one</h2>
<p>Two habits do most of the damage. The first is answering a recorded statement with estimates: how many seconds the car ahead had been stopped, how fast you were closing, how far back you were. A guess becomes a quoted fact later, and "I did not measure that" is an honest answer. The second is releasing the vehicle before the bumper structure, seat backs and restraint positions are photographed. Add a third if you live off Allison Hill or Midtown, where doorbell and storefront cameras sit close to the intersection: that footage usually overwrites itself within days, so ask the same week.</p>
<h2>The percentage argument the adjuster will make</h2>
<p>Expect a theory that you contributed. Pennsylvania uses a modified comparative rule at 42 Pa.C.S. 7102(a): contributory negligence does not bar recovery where it was not greater than the causal negligence of the defendants, but damages shrink in proportion to the share assigned to you. Past 50 percent the claim is gone, which makes the argument about brake lamps or following distance worth money to the other side. State regulation at 31 Pa. Code 146.9 pushes back. An insurer applying comparative negligence must disclose the factual or legal basis for the offer or denial, and may not use standards that compel claimants to litigate by offering substantially less than what is due.</p>
<h2>After first contact, and how a fee gets set</h2>
<p>The first pass is a list rather than a story: crash date and location, report number and agency, photographs, witness contacts, both declarations pages, and a dated treatment timeline. Requests then go out in parallel for the report, for any camera footage still alive, and to each carrier. On money, no percentage is fixed anywhere in Pennsylvania law for a crash claim. Rule of Professional Conduct 1.5(a) prohibits an illegal or clearly excessive fee and supplies eight measuring factors, while 1.5(c) demands a written contingent agreement plus a closing statement showing the outcome and the remittance.</p>

## svc_uninsured_driver_claim_lawyer_lede

## svc_uninsured_driver_claim_lawyer_body

<h2>Nobody in this file is arguing about blame</h2>
<p>Liability is usually conceded or beside the point here. The argument is about what coverage exists and whether it answers. Three versions arrive in this county. A driver produces no card at all. A driver produces a card and the carrier reports the policy lapsed before the crash. Or the limit is genuine but small, because Pennsylvania's financial responsibility definition in 75 Pa.C.S. 1702 sets the floor at $15,000 for injury to one person, $30,000 for two or more, and $5,000 for property damage. A single ambulance ride and an emergency scan can pass that first figure.</p>
<h2>What the statute puts in your policy, and what a signature takes out</h2>
<p>Section 1731 requires that uninsured and underinsured motorist coverages be offered with every liability policy issued for a vehicle registered or garaged in the Commonwealth, though buying them is optional. Rejecting either one takes a specific written form whose wording the statute prints in full, signed and dated by the first named insured. A form that does not comply specifically with the section is void. If the insurer cannot produce a valid rejection, the statute says the coverage must equal the bodily injury liability limits. That is why the paper matters more than anyone's recollection of a phone call at renewal.</p>
<h2>Stacking is the quirk worth counting</h2>
<p>Most states let an insurer confine you to the single limit shown on the page. Pennsylvania starts from the opposite rule. Under 75 Pa.C.S. 1738, when more than one vehicle is insured, the stated uninsured or underinsured limit applies separately to each vehicle, and the coverage available to an insured is the sum of the limits for every vehicle on which that person is an insured. A named insured may waive stacking, but only through the rejection wording the statute prints, signed and dated by the first named insured. Count the vehicles on the declarations page, then look for that waiver.</p>
<h2>An uninsured defendant can hand back full tort rights</h2>
<p>Here is where the tort election turns friendly. Section 1705(d) lists exceptions that let someone otherwise bound by limited tort recover as though full tort had been chosen. They include a driver convicted of, or accepting Accelerated Rehabilitative Disposition for, driving under the influence in that crash. They include a driver operating a vehicle registered in another state, a person who intended the injury, and a driver who has not maintained financial responsibility as the chapter requires. The last one matters most in an uninsured file, because the very fact that killed the liability claim may reopen the damages a limited tort policy had closed.</p>
<h2>The first week when the other driver is gone</h2>
<p>Report the crash to police from the scene. Then write down what a plate search cannot recover: direction of travel, color, body style, damage, stickers, roof racks, cargo, lettering on a door. Canvass for exterior cameras the same day rather than the next month. Keep the declarations page, every letter, every estimate and every explanation of benefits together, and note that an apparent owner is not always the driver. A borrowed car, a work vehicle or a delivery run can add a policy nobody mentioned at the scene.</p>
<h2>What your own carrier is required to send</h2>
<p>Pennsylvania regulates the pace of a claim. Under 31 Pa. Code 146.5, an insurer must acknowledge notice of a claim within 10 working days and reply within 10 working days to other communications that reasonably suggest an answer is expected. Section 146.6 requires the investigation to be completed within 30 days of notification, and if that is impossible, the insurer must give a written explanation for the delay and say when a decision may be expected, then repeat that every 45 days. Silence past those marks is a fact worth recording in your own file.</p>
<h2>After first contact, and where the fee rules bite</h2>
<p>Work in an uninsured file starts with reading your own contract: limits, the number of vehicles, any rejection or waiver form, notice provisions and consent-to-settle language. Requests go to the police agency for the report, to your carrier for the full policy, and to providers for records. Remember 75 Pa.C.S. 1722, which precludes recovering the amount of benefits paid or payable under your first-party coverage, in a tort action and in an uninsured or underinsured proceeding alike. On fees, no Pennsylvania statute caps a percentage in a motor vehicle case. Rule 1.5(a) supplies the standard and 1.5(b) requires the basis of the fee in writing for a new client.</p>

<h2>How you know the offer landed early</h2>
<p>The signal is not the size of the number. It is what is missing behind it. <h2>The damages column under a limited tort policy</h2>
<p>Pennsylvania splits the column in a way that surprises people. Section 1702 defines that phrase as death, serious impairment of body function, or permanent serious disfigurement. So the same collision, the same treatment and the same lost wages can produce two very different offers depending on a box checked at a renewal. The declarations page is the document that answers it, not the adjuster's summary.</p>
<h2>The subtraction nobody explains at the start</h2>
<p>Every Pennsylvania auto liability policy must include a medical benefit of at least $5,000 under 75 Pa.C.S. 1711, and that money pays for early treatment while fault is still unsettled. Then 1722 does something people rarely expect. A person eligible for those first-party benefits is precluded from recovering the amount of benefits paid or payable in an action against the driver at fault, and in an uninsured or underinsured proceeding as well. Ask which bills went through the first-party coverage and which did not, and get the coordination answer in writing before comparing offers.</p>
<h2>Fifty-one percent, and the paperwork behind it</h2>
<p>The other side's cheapest tool is a percentage. Section 7102(a) of the Judicial Code allows recovery where the injured person's negligence was not greater than the causal negligence of the defendants, and reduces damages in proportion to the share assigned. Once your share climbs past the defendants' share, the recovery is nothing, which is why even a thin fault theory gets argued hard. Pennsylvania regulates the tactic too. Under 31 Pa. Code 146.9, an insurer applying comparative negligence must fully disclose the factual or legal basis for its offer or denial, and must keep a record in the claim file of the evidence and its evaluation.</p>
<h2>What the claim regulations require in writing</h2>
<p>Chapter 146 of the insurance regulations sets deadlines that are easy to check. Acknowledgment of a claim is due within 10 working days under 146.5. <h2>If it does not settle: arbitration in this county</h2>
<p>Smaller cases here do not begin in front of a jury. Dauphin County's local rules send every action at issue where the amount in controversy is $50,000 or less, except disputes over title to real estate, to a board of arbitration, and the parties may not stipulate around it. <p>Practical work looks like assembly. ## svc_comparative_negligence_in_pennsylvania_lede

Fault in Pennsylvania is divided, not assigned. A percentage attached to the injured person reduces the recovery, a percentage above a statutory line eliminates it, and a separate rule decides whether one defendant can be made to pay for another's share.

## svc_comparative_negligence_in_pennsylvania_body

<h2>The rule that divides recovery</h2>
<p>Comparative negligence in Pennsylvania is set out at 42 Pa.C.S. 7102. Subsection (a) allows an injured person to recover where that person's own negligence was not greater than the causal negligence of the defendants against whom recovery is sought, and requires that any damages awarded be diminished in proportion to the share of negligence attributed to the injured person. Two consequences follow. Below the line, a share reduces the award arithmetically. At the point where the injured person's share exceeds the combined share of the defendants, the recovery is nothing at all. That is why an argument that seems modest, a few percentage points about speed or attention, is pursued so persistently.</p>
<h2>Joint liability, several liability, and the sixty percent rule</h2>
<p>Where more than one defendant is responsible, subsection (a.1) governs how much each can be made to pay. The general position is that each defendant's liability is several rather than joint, meaning each pays its own apportioned share. The statute then lists situations where joint liability still applies, including intentional misrepresentation, an intentional tort, a defendant found liable for sixty percent or more of the total liability, and specific claims arising under the Hazardous Sites Cleanup Act and the Liquor Code. In a multi-vehicle collision, that sixty percent threshold can change the practical value of a claim substantially, because it determines whether one solvent defendant can be reached for the whole verdict.</p>
<h2>Parties who settled, and parties who were never sued</h2>
<p>Subsection (a.2) deals with the apportionment problem created by settlement. Where a person has entered into a release with the injured party but is not a defendant at trial, that person's share of liability is still considered in apportioning the total, and the plaintiff's recovery against the remaining defendants is reduced accordingly. The statute also preserves the immunity an employer holds under the Workers' Compensation Act, so an employer's conduct is treated differently from that of an ordinary third party. Anyone considering an early partial settlement in a case with several potential defendants is making a decision that touches this subsection.</p>
<h2>How percentages get argued in a crash file</h2>
<p>Apportionment arguments in vehicle cases are usually built out of small, checkable facts: closing speeds, seat belt use, whether headlights were on, whether a turn signal operated, whether a driver had a clear sight line, the condition of tires and brakes, and how long a hazard was visible before impact. Because each of those is documentary or physical, the strongest response to a percentage argument is generally evidence rather than assertion. Photographs, maintenance records, vehicle data, and independent witness accounts are what move a number.</p>
<h2>What an insurer has to disclose when it uses the rule</h2>
<p>Pennsylvania regulates the use of comparative negligence during claim handling rather than leaving it to negotiation. The Insurance Department's unfair claims settlement regulations require an insurer that applies comparative negligence to explain the factual and legal basis for its position, and to keep the supporting evaluation in the claim file. A written request for that explanation converts a vague statement about shared fault into a specific position that can be examined and, where the evidence permits, answered.</p>
<h2>Where the tort election sits alongside fault</h2>
<p>Pennsylvania stacks two separate reductions, and they operate independently. The tort election under 75 Pa.C.S. 1705 determines which categories of damages are available in the first place. Comparative negligence then reduces whatever is available by the injured person's share. A file can clear the tort threshold and still be reduced by an apportionment argument, and a file with clean liability can still be limited by the election. Analyzing one without the other produces a misleading estimate.</p>
<h2>Multi-vehicle collisions and chain reactions</h2>
<p>Chain collisions on the interstates around Harrisburg are where these provisions collide. Several drivers, several insurers, and often a commercial carrier are all involved, each with a different exposure depending on the sequence of impacts. Reconstructing that sequence is a physical evidence exercise, and it needs to begin before the vehicles are repaired or salvaged.</p>

## svc_concussion_and_head_injury_claims_lede

A concussion can happen without a head striking anything and without a single minute of unconsciousness. The symptoms that matter most in a claim often appear days later, when the crash file has already been opened and closed.

## svc_concussion_and_head_injury_claims_body

<h2>The national picture</h2>
<p>The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reports that there were about 214,110 hospitalizations related to traumatic brain injury in the United States in 2020, more than 586 a day, and 68,663 deaths related to traumatic brain injury in 2023, more than 190 a day. The agency notes that adults aged seventy-five and over accounted for roughly thirty-two percent of those hospitalizations and about twenty-eight percent of the deaths. It also states plainly that these estimates exclude brain injuries treated only in emergency departments, primary care, or urgent care, and those that were never treated at all. Most concussions after a collision fall into the excluded categories.</p>
<h2>Why the injury is missed at the scene</h2>
<p>Emergency response after a crash prioritizes visible and immediately dangerous injuries. A person who is walking, answering questions, and declining transport is generally recorded as uninjured, and that notation lands in the crash report. Concussion symptoms frequently develop or intensify over the following days: headache, light and noise sensitivity, difficulty holding attention, word-finding problems, sleep disruption, irritability, and a delayed recognition that ordinary tasks now require effort. Because the onset is gradual, the first medical documentation may be a week old by the time anyone connects it to the collision.</p>
<h2>What documentation closes that gap</h2>
<p>Three practical steps do most of the work. Getting evaluated promptly once symptoms appear, and telling the clinician about the collision by date, creates a record with a mechanism attached. Keeping a dated symptom log, written by the injured person or a family member, produces contemporaneous evidence of severity that memory cannot reproduce later. And recording specific functional consequences, such as shifts cut short, tasks reassigned at work, a semester dropped, or driving avoided after dark, provides the kind of detail Pennsylvania's threshold analysis examines.</p>
<h2>The treatment categories covered by first-party benefits</h2>
<p>Head injury care is often not just physical. Section 1712 of the Vehicle Code, which lists the first-party benefits an insurer must make available, expressly includes psychiatric and psychological services, speech pathology and audiology, occupational therapy, and vocational rehabilitation within the medical benefit, alongside hospital and surgical care. That list matters because concussion recovery frequently involves exactly those services rather than a procedure. Coverage for them is available without limitation as to time where, within eighteen months of the accident, further expense is ascertainable with reasonable medical probability.</p>
<h2>How the tort threshold treats cognitive symptoms</h2>
<p>Under the limited tort election, damages for pain and other nonmonetary detriment require a serious injury, defined in section 1702 to include serious impairment of body function. Pennsylvania courts examine which function was impaired and whether the impairment was serious, considering how extensive it was, how long it lasted, and what treatment was needed, with permanence not required. Cognitive and vestibular symptoms fit that framework, but only where the record describes function rather than sensation. Neuropsychological testing, vestibular assessment, and documented work or school accommodations are the sort of evidence that speaks to it.</p>
<h2>Time away from work, and how it is proven</h2>
<p>Head injury often interferes with work in ways that are hard to describe on a form, because the person can physically do the job and cannot sustain the concentration it takes. Pennsylvania's income loss benefit under section 1712 covers eighty percent of actual loss of gross income, and it does not begin until five working days have been lost after the accident. That structure rewards precise records: dates missed, shifts shortened, duties reassigned, and any written accommodation from an employer. Where a clinician has restricted screen time, driving, or hours, the restriction should say so with dates rather than in general terms, because a general instruction is difficult to convert into a documented loss months afterward.</p>
<h2>Children, older adults, and repeat injury</h2>
<p>Two groups deserve separate attention. For a minor, section 1721 measures the four-year period for first-party benefit actions from the date the minor reaches eighteen, and separately the tolling provisions of the Judicial Code affect when a personal injury action must be brought. For older adults, the CDC data showing the concentration of hospitalizations and deaths among people seventy-five and over is a reason to take a mild presentation seriously rather than a reason to discount a claim. A prior concussion history is relevant clinical information and should be disclosed to the treating clinician rather than omitted.</p>

## svc_distracted_driving_crashes_lede

Pennsylvania stopped regulating only texting and started regulating the act of holding a phone. Paul Miller's Law added a new section to the Vehicle Code, defined use in physical terms, and attached the offense to the sentencing rules for vehicular homicide.

## svc_distracted_driving_crashes_body

<h2>What the 2024 act changed</h2>
<p>Act 18 of 2024, approved on June 5, 2024 and known as Paul Miller's Law, amended Title 75 in several places. It rewrote the definition in section 102, replacing the older phrase about wireless communications devices with the term interactive mobile device, and expanded what that covers to include browsing, gaming, taking or transmitting images, recording or broadcasting video, and creating or sharing social media, alongside calling and messaging. It kept exclusions for devices used exclusively for navigation, devices operated in a hands-free manner including systems integrated into the vehicle, devices affixed to a mass transit vehicle or school bus, licensed amateur radio, emergency notification use, emergency responders on duty, and commercial drivers acting within federal regulations. The act stated that most of its provisions took effect twelve months after approval.</p>
<h2>The new section, and what use means</h2>
<p>Section 5 of the act added section 3316.1, which prohibits a driver from using an interactive mobile device while driving a motor vehicle, subject to an exception for communicating with law enforcement or emergency services to prevent injury to persons or property. The definition is physical rather than functional. Use includes holding the device with at least one hand or supporting it with another part of the body, dialing or answering by pressing more than a single button, and reaching for the device in a way that requires the driver to move out of a seated, belted driving position. Driving is defined to include the moments when the vehicle is temporarily stationary because of traffic, a traffic control device, or a momentary delay, and to exclude a vehicle that has been moved off the highway and safely halted.</p>
<h2>Penalties, the warning period, and the older texting section</h2>
<p>A violation of section 3316.1 is a summary offense carrying a fifty-dollar fine, and the section included a transition: for the first twelve months after its effective date, a driver could be issued only a written warning. Section 3316, the earlier prohibition on text-based communications, remains in the Code as amended, also with a fifty-dollar fine, and both sections state that a driver may not be cited under both for the same conduct at the same time and place. Both sections preempt municipal ordinances on the subject, and neither authorizes the seizure of a device.</p>
<h2>The sentencing consequence in serious cases</h2>
<p>The act also reached the serious offense provisions. Sections 3732 and 3732.1, covering homicide by vehicle and aggravated assault by vehicle, were amended so that a person convicted under those sections who is also convicted under section 3316 or the new section 3316.1 may be sentenced to an additional term of confinement, and the act directed the Commission on Sentencing to provide a sentencing enhancement in those circumstances. That is the clearest signal in the statute about how the legislature views a phone in a fatal crash.</p>
<h2>What the crash data shows</h2>
<p>PennDOT's 2024 driver-error table lists 9,687 crashes involving a distracted driver, with 43 people fatally injured. For Dauphin County, the department's workbook lists 218 distracted driver crashes in 2025 and 229 in 2024. Those figures depend on what an investigating officer was able to determine at the scene, which is a well-known limitation in distraction data everywhere. Distraction is usually inferred rather than observed, so the recorded counts should be read as a floor rather than a measurement.</p>
<h2>The evidence that establishes distraction</h2>
<p>Because distraction leaves no skid mark, these cases are built from records held by others. Carrier billing and usage records show call and data activity by timestamp. Application-level data may show interaction. Vehicle event data can show whether any braking occurred before impact. Independent witnesses sometimes describe a driver looking down. Roadside cameras occasionally capture the interior. Every one of those sources is subject to retention limits or requires a formal request, which is why the useful step in the first days is preservation rather than argument.</p>
<h2>How this connects to the civil claim</h2>
<p>A citation under either section is not a finding of civil liability, and a conviction is not required for negligence to be established. What the statutory framework provides is a defined standard of conduct that a jury can understand, plus documentary evidence generated by the enforcement process. The damages available in the resulting claim still depend on the injured person's tort election under section 1705 and on the apportionment rules in the Judicial Code.</p>

## svc_hit_and_run_and_phantom_vehicle_claims_lede

A driver who leaves does not end the claim, but the clock that governs it starts running differently. Pennsylvania attaches duties to everyone involved in a crash and adds two conditions before an unidentified vehicle can be treated as an uninsured one.

## svc_hit_and_run_and_phantom_vehicle_claims_body

<h2>The duties a departing driver breaks</h2>
<p>The Vehicle Code sets out what a driver has to do after a crash, and leaving is not on the list. Section 3742 makes it an offense for a driver involved in an accident involving injury or death to fail to stop and remain at the scene, or to fail to return and remain until the statutory requirements are met. Section 3744 spells out what remaining means in practice: giving name, address, and registration information, showing a driver's license on request, and rendering reasonable assistance to an injured person, including arranging transport for treatment where it is apparent that treatment is needed or where an injured person asks for it. Section 3745 covers collisions with unattended vehicles and other property, requiring the driver to locate the owner or leave written notice.</p>
<h2>Reporting the collision to police</h2>
<p>Section 3746 requires immediate notice to the nearest police department when an accident results in injury, death, or a vehicle needing to be towed, and it also requires that reported accidents be investigated and that each driver be given a signed statement showing the accident was reported. In a hit and run, the driver who stayed is usually the only person who can trigger that process. Making the report immediately does more than satisfy the statute; it fixes the date, time, and location in an official record while memory of a partial plate, a vehicle color, or a direction of travel is still fresh.</p>
<h2>The phantom vehicle condition most people never hear</h2>
<p>Where the other vehicle is never identified, the claim usually moves to uninsured motorist coverage, and 75 Pa.C.S. 1702 sets the terms. The definition of an uninsured motor vehicle includes a vehicle whose identity cannot be ascertained, but only if two things happen. The accident must be reported to the police or proper governmental authority. And the claimant must notify the insurer within thirty days, or as soon as practicable afterward, that the claimant or a legal representative has a legal action arising out of the accident. Both conditions are procedural, both are easy to satisfy in the first week, and neither can be fixed retroactively once the deadline has passed unnoticed.</p>
<h2>Why the thirty-day notice deserves a calendar entry</h2>
<p>A person recovering from a collision is rarely thinking about notice provisions. The practical consequence is that phantom vehicle claims fail on paperwork more often than on facts. Written notice to the insurer, sent in a form that produces proof of delivery, and a police report number recorded in the same file, cover both statutory conditions. The notice does not have to resolve who was at fault or what the injuries are worth. It has to exist, be timely, and be provable, which is a much smaller task than the claim itself.</p>
<h2>What can still identify a vehicle after it drives away</h2>
<p>Identification is not hopeless. Partial plate characters narrow a registration search. Paint transfer and specific damage patterns on the remaining vehicle indicate the make, model year range, and the striking surface. Body shops within a region sometimes see the matching repair. Cameras at fuel stations, warehouses, and residences along the departure route hold footage on retention cycles that are frequently short, so a written request within days is worth more than a phone call in a month. Where the vehicle is eventually identified, the claim changes character entirely, because a liability policy may now be available.</p>
<h2>The overlap with a limited tort election</h2>
<p>Pennsylvania's tort election interacts with these claims, and the interaction is not intuitive. Section 1705(d) lists circumstances in which a person who selected limited tort is not precluded from seeking noneconomic loss, and one of them concerns a driver of a vehicle that has not maintained financial responsibility. Whether that provision reaches a given hit and run depends on facts about the other driver that may not be known for months, or ever. The point is that the tort election analysis and the uninsured motorist analysis run in parallel, and neither answer disposes of the other.</p>
<h2>Documents worth assembling in the first week</h2>
<p>Six items cover most of what a hit and run file needs at the start: the police report number and the signed statement that the accident was reported, dated photographs of the damage before repair, the written thirty-day notice to the insurer, the declarations page for every policy in the household, contact details for any independent witness, and dated medical records beginning with the first visit. Assembled early, they are records. Assembled late, they are recollections.</p>

## svc_intersection_and_left_turn_crashes_lede

Intersections concentrate everything that can go wrong on a road into a few square yards: opposing traffic, turning paths, signal timing, and two drivers who each believe they had the right of way. Pennsylvania law has specific rules about who yields, and one evidentiary shortcut worth knowing.

## svc_intersection_and_left_turn_crashes_body

<h2>How often this happens in Dauphin County</h2>
<p>PennDOT's county workbook puts the pattern in numbers. For 2025 it lists 1,069 intersection crashes in Dauphin County, of which 373 were at signalized intersections and 376 at stop-controlled locations, along with 118 crashes involving a driver running a red light. Statewide, the department counted 42,108 intersection crashes in 2024, which it reported as 38.1 percent of all reportable crashes that year. In other words, close to four in ten crashes in Pennsylvania happen where roads meet, and in this county the split between traffic lights and stop signs is nearly even.</p>
<h2>The left-turn duty in the Vehicle Code</h2>
<p>The rule for turning across traffic is in 75 Pa.C.S. 3322. A driver intending to turn left within an intersection, or into an alley, private road, or driveway, must yield the right of way to a vehicle approaching from the opposite direction that is either already in the intersection or close enough to it to constitute a hazard. The duty is framed around hazard, not around distance in feet or the color of a light. That is why left-turn cases so often become disputes about closing speed and sight lines rather than disputes about the law, and why the physical evidence tends to decide them.</p>
<h2>The prima facie rule at a yield sign</h2>
<p>Section 3323 deals with stop signs and yield signs, and subsection (c) contains a provision that changes the shape of an argument. Where a driver drives past a yield sign without stopping and then collides with a vehicle in the intersection, or with a pedestrian or vehicle in an adjacent crosswalk, the statute treats that collision as prima facie evidence of a failure to yield. The collision itself becomes proof of the violation, which shifts what the other side has to explain. Anyone dealing with a crash at a yield-controlled approach should note the type of traffic control at that specific leg of the intersection, because the legal consequence differs.</p>
<h2>Angle collisions and their severity</h2>
<p>Crash type matters to injuries as much as to fault. PennDOT recorded 32,969 angle crashes statewide in 2024 with 247 people fatally injured, making the broadside geometry one of the largest single crash categories in the state's data. Two driver behaviors in the department's 2024 error table sit close to this pattern: improper or careless turning appeared in 13,313 crashes with 78 fatalities, and proceeding without clearance appeared in 8,971 crashes with 53 fatalities. The energy in a side impact reaches the occupant compartment more directly than in most other configurations, which is part of why these files often involve serious injury questions.</p>
<h2>What resolves two honest accounts</h2>
<p>Both drivers in an intersection crash are frequently sincere and irreconcilable. Documentation is what separates the accounts. The location and direction of the crush on each vehicle indicates the approach angles. Debris fields and final resting positions indicate speed and point of impact. Signal timing plans held by the road authority establish how long a phase lasted, and whether a protected arrow existed at the time. Nearby businesses and residences sometimes hold camera footage on short retention cycles measured in days. Independent witnesses who were behind or beside one of the vehicles often give the most useful account, and their contact information is easy to lose in the first hour.</p>
<h2>The paperwork trail that starts at the scene</h2>
<p>Investigating officers write intersection geometry into the crash report, and the diagram is often the only contemporaneous drawing anyone makes. Where police investigate the collision, section 3746 requires that each driver involved be given a signed statement showing the accident was reported, and that statement is the practical way to confirm a report exists before a copy can be obtained. Where no officer investigates, section 3747 puts the reporting duty on the driver instead, within a fixed number of days set by the statute.</p>

## svc_limited_tort_vs_full_tort_lede

Two words on a declarations page decide what a Pennsylvania driver is allowed to ask for after a crash. The choice is made once, usually in a hurry, and then it follows the policy quietly through every renewal until somebody signs a form saying otherwise.

## svc_limited_tort_vs_full_tort_body

<h2>What the election actually changes</h2>
<p>Pennsylvania lets a private passenger vehicle owner pick between two tort options, and the statute that governs the pick is 75 Pa.C.S. 1705. The short version is that the limited tort option keeps a driver eligible to seek what the crash cost in money, while closing the door on damages for pain and other nonmonetary detriment unless the injury clears a statutory threshold. Full tort keeps that door open. Nothing about the collision changes. The medical records do not change, the property damage does not change, and the fault picture does not change. Only the list of damages available to the injured person changes, which is why the declarations page belongs in the same folder as the crash report.</p>
<h2>The notice an insurer is required to send</h2>
<p>Section 1705(a) does not leave the wording to the company. It requires insurers to send a standardized notice explaining both options in a form the statute itself spells out, and it puts a clock on the response. If the named insured does not return a choice within twenty days, the insurer has to follow up with a notice identified as the second and final one. The default matters more than most people realize: if the form comes back without a signature, the statute treats the buyer as having the full tort option. So an unanswered mailing and an affirmatively chosen limited tort election are not the same thing, and the file should show which one happened.</p>
<h2>Why a choice made years ago still governs</h2>
<p>The part that surprises people is durability. Under section 1705, an election stays in force on renewals, on reinstatements, on substitute or replacement policies, and on supplementary coverage, and it keeps operating until the insurer receives a properly executed form electing the other option. There is no annual reset and no automatic expiration. A driver who chose one option during a first policy purchase in a different decade can still be bound by it after a crash on Cameron Street, unless a signed change is in the insurer's hands. That is a document question, not a memory question, and the answer usually sits in the underwriting file rather than the insurance card in the glove box.</p>
<h2>When two policies in one household disagree</h2>
<p>Section 1705 also handles the situation where the injured person is covered by more than one policy and those policies carry different tort options. The statute binds the person to the option on the policy covering the private passenger vehicle occupied at the time of the accident. If the injured person was not in a vehicle covered by any of the conflicting policies, the statute leaves the full tort option in place. Households with several vehicles, several insurers, and adult children sharing cars can therefore produce a different damages picture for the same passengers, depending on whose car everyone was riding in that afternoon.</p>
<h2>People a limited tort election does not reach</h2>
<p>Not everyone injured on a Dauphin County road is bound by anyone's election. Section 1705(b)(3) says an individual who does not own a currently registered private passenger vehicle, and who is not an insured under an applicable policy, is not precluded from claiming noneconomic loss. Subsection (d)(3) addresses another common scenario, treating the occupant of a vehicle that is not a private passenger motor vehicle differently from the occupant of a family sedan. Passengers, pedestrians, and people riding in commercial or work vehicles all sit somewhere on that map, and the position is worth confirming before assuming a threshold applies at all.</p>
<h2>The documents that answer the question</h2>
<p>Three items settle most tort election disputes without argument. The first is the declarations page in force on the date of the crash, which names the option. The second is the signed election notice itself, which shows whether a choice was affirmatively made or defaulted. The third is the policy history, which shows whether a later signed form replaced an earlier choice. Insurers keep all three. A request in writing, made early, avoids the version of this dispute where an adjuster describes the coverage over the phone and nobody can produce the paper months later.</p>
<h2>Local scale, and where to read more</h2>
<p>PennDOT's county crash workbook lists 2,888 reportable crashes in Dauphin County for 2025, which is roughly the volume of collisions in which somebody's tort election ends up mattering here in a single year. Every one of those files eventually reaches the same question about which damages column applies. ## svc_medical_bills_and_first_party_benefits_lede

Somebody has to pay for treatment while fault is still an open question. Pennsylvania answers that with first-party benefits, a priority order for deciding which policy pays, a deadline for payment, and a rule that subtracts those benefits from any later recovery.

## svc_medical_bills_and_first_party_benefits_body

<h2>Which policy pays, and in what order</h2>
<p>The order is set by 75 Pa.C.S. 1713, and it is not a matter of negotiation. For a named insured, the policy on which the person is the named insured pays first. For an insured, it is the policy covering that insured. For occupants of an insured vehicle, it is the policy on that vehicle. For a person who was not an occupant of any vehicle, such as a pedestrian or cyclist, it is the policy on any vehicle involved in the accident, with the statute adding that a parked and unoccupied vehicle does not count as involved unless it was parked so as to create an unreasonable risk of injury. Where two policies share equal priority, the insurer approached first must process and pay as if wholly responsible and then seek proportional contribution.</p>
<h2>The benefits an insurer must make available</h2>
<p>Section 1712 sets out the menu. Beyond the medical benefit, it includes an income loss benefit consisting of eighty percent of actual loss of gross income, plus reasonable expenses actually incurred to hire a substitute for self-employment services or special help that allows a person to work. That benefit does not begin until five working days of work have been lost after the accident. The section also provides an accidental death benefit where injury causes death within twenty-four months of the accident, a funeral benefit for expenses incurred within the same period, a combination benefit, and extraordinary medical benefits for amounts above one hundred thousand dollars.</p>
<h2>When a payment becomes overdue</h2>
<p>Section 1716 puts a clock on the insurer. Benefits are overdue if not paid within thirty days after the insurer receives reasonable proof of the amount, and where proof supports only part of a claim, the supported portion still runs on that thirty-day clock. Overdue benefits carry interest at twelve percent per year from the date they became due. Where an insurer is found to have acted unreasonably in refusing to pay benefits when due, the statute adds a reasonable attorney fee based on actual time expended. The practical significance is that dated proof of submission is what starts the clock, so a submission log is worth keeping.</p>
<h2>The subtraction and the subrogation rule</h2>
<p>Two sections work in opposite directions and are easy to confuse. Section 1720 addresses subrogation, generally barring subrogation or a reimbursement claim out of a tort recovery with respect to benefits paid under the sections that create first-party coverage, subject to later legislative changes affecting workers' compensation. Section 1722 addresses the injured person's recovery instead, precluding recovery of the amount of benefits paid or payable in an action against a tortfeasor or in an uninsured or underinsured proceeding. Read together, the insurer that paid does not chase the recovery, and the injured person does not collect the same expense twice.</p>
<h2>Where health insurance fits</h2>
<p>Many Pennsylvania households carry auto policies coordinated with health coverage, and the coordination determines which carrier is billed first and what the deductible looks like. That choice was made when the policy was purchased, and it appears in the policy documents rather than in the crash file. Bills routed to the wrong payer at the beginning generate collection notices months later that are avoidable. Asking, in writing, which coverage is being treated as primary is a small step that prevents a large administrative problem.</p>
<h2>What a clean benefits file looks like</h2>
<p>Five categories cover it: itemized bills with the explanations of benefits that correspond to them, a dated record of what was submitted to which insurer and when, wage documentation adequate to support an income loss calculation at eighty percent of gross, the declarations pages establishing the coverages in force, and the correspondence in which the insurer states its position. Where a payment is late, the interest provision applies from the date it became due, which can only be established if the submission date is documented.</p>

## svc_pedestrian_and_cyclist_collisions_lede

A person on foot or on a bicycle has no crumple zone, and Pennsylvania writes their protections into specific traffic rules: a yielding duty at crosswalks, a limit on passing a stopped vehicle, and a minimum passing distance measured in feet.

## svc_pedestrian_and_cyclist_collisions_body

<h2>The scale of the problem in the state and the county</h2>
<p>PennDOT counted 3,035 pedestrian-related crashes statewide in 2024, with 174 pedestrians fatally injured, and 1,271 crashes involving bicycles, with 19 bicyclists fatally injured. In Dauphin County, the department's county workbook lists 67 pedestrian crashes and 50 bicyclist crashes for 2025, along with 123 crashes involving a vulnerable road user and 125 involving a non-motorist. The county bicyclist figure for 2025 is roughly double the 24 listed for 2024, which is the kind of movement worth noticing in a county whose seat has been adding bicycle infrastructure.</p>
<h2>Crossing the road is where it happens</h2>
<p>The state data identifies the dominant pattern without ambiguity. Crossing the roadway accounted for 75.4 percent of crash-related pedestrian actions in 2024 and 61.5 percent of fatal pedestrian actions, involving 2,426 pedestrians and 107 fatalities. By road type, 60.3 percent of pedestrian fatalities occurred on non-interstate state highways and 44.5 percent of pedestrian injuries occurred on local roads. In practical terms, the highest-risk situation is a person crossing a state-maintained arterial through a city, which describes several corridors in and around Harrisburg.</p>
<h2>The crosswalk duties in the Vehicle Code</h2>
<p>Section 3542 sets the core rule. Where traffic-control signals are not in place or not in operation, a driver must yield the right of way to a pedestrian crossing within a marked crosswalk, or within an unmarked crosswalk at an intersection. Subsection (b) puts a duty on the pedestrian too, prohibiting suddenly leaving a curb or other place of safety into the path of a vehicle so close as to be a hazard. Subsection (c) addresses a common and dangerous situation: where a vehicle is stopped at a crosswalk to let someone cross, a driver approaching from behind may not overtake and pass the stopped vehicle. A violation of the yielding rule is a summary offense carrying a fifty-dollar fine.</p>
<h2>Crossing outside a crosswalk</h2>
<p>Section 3543 covers the rest of the road. Where someone crosses outside a marked crossing, or outside an unmarked one at an intersection, the pedestrian owes the yielding duty to vehicles already on the roadway. The section also restricts crossing between adjacent intersections at which traffic-control signals are in operation in an urban district other than in a marked crosswalk, and it prohibits crossing an intersection diagonally except where authorized. These provisions matter because a pedestrian's own conduct becomes a comparative negligence argument, and the statutory framing is where that argument starts.</p>
<h2>The four-foot rule for passing a bicycle</h2>
<p>Pennsylvania states a numeric passing distance. Section 3303(a)(3) requires the driver of a motor vehicle overtaking a pedalcycle proceeding in the same direction to pass to the left of it at not less than four feet and at a careful and prudent reduced speed. Section 3505 sets out corresponding rules for cyclists, including provisions about riding on the roadway and shoulder and limits on riding more than two abreast where traffic is impeded. Together they define what a lawful pass looks like, which is the reference point when a sideswipe or a close pass ends in a collision.</p>
<h2>Evidence in a case with no vehicle damage</h2>
<p>These files are evidentially different from vehicle-to-vehicle crashes. There may be little or no damage to the striking vehicle, so the physical record is often limited to the point of contact on the bumper or mirror, the resting position of the person and the bicycle, and the location of personal items. Crosswalk markings, signal timing, pedestrian signal phases, street lighting condition, and sight lines obstructed by parked vehicles all become relevant, and several of those are documented only by photographs taken soon afterward or by records held by the road authority.</p>
<h2>The coverage question for someone not in a car</h2>
<p>A person injured while walking or cycling is not left outside the first-party benefits system. Section 1713 directs that for a person who was not an occupant of a motor vehicle, first-party benefits come from the policy on any vehicle involved in the accident, with a parked and unoccupied vehicle excluded unless it was parked so as to create an unreasonable risk of injury. Section 1705(b)(3) is also relevant for anyone who owns no currently registered private passenger vehicle and is not an insured under an applicable policy.</p>

## svc_police_crash_reports_and_evidence_lede

The crash report is the document everyone wants and few people read carefully. Pennsylvania caps what a police department may charge for it, sets a deadline for forwarding it, and then says the copy is not admissible as evidence in a damages case.

## svc_police_crash_reports_and_evidence_body

<h2>What the statute requires of the police</h2>
<p>Section 3751 of the Vehicle Code governs police reporting. A department that investigates a reportable crash and prepares a written report, whether at the scene or later by interviewing participants or witnesses, must forward an initial written report to the department within fifteen days of the accident, with a supplemental report to follow if the initial one is incomplete. That fifteen-day figure is the reason a report is often unavailable in the first two weeks. It reflects a statutory processing window rather than an office being slow.</p>
<h2>Who may obtain a copy, and what it costs</h2>
<p>Subsection (b) requires police departments, on request, to furnish a certified copy of the full report of the investigation to any person involved in the accident, to that person's attorney or insurer, and to a list of governmental recipients. The cost of furnishing a copy may not exceed fifteen dollars, except in a city of the first class, where the ceiling is twenty-five dollars. Harrisburg is not a city of the first class, and the City of Harrisburg's own records information states a fifteen-dollar charge for a crash report, payable by check or money order, with copies obtainable at the records window at 123 Walnut Street on the second level during posted hours.</p>
<h2>Where state police crashes are handled</h2>
<p>Collisions investigated by the Pennsylvania State Police follow a separate request process. The Commonwealth's service page states that crash reports are available fifteen days after the date of the crash and that the fee is twenty-two dollars, payable to the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, with requests accepted online or by mailing the signed application form. The application, form SP 7-0015, asks for the crash date and time, the case number, the county and municipality, the route and location, the reason for the request, and the requester's relationship to the crash. The mailing address is the Crash Reports Unit at 1800 Elmerton Avenue in Harrisburg, which is also where the state police headquarters sits.</p>
<h2>The admissibility rule almost nobody expects</h2>
<p>Subsection (b)(4) contains the provision that reframes how the report should be used. The copy of the report is not admissible as evidence in any action for damages or criminal proceeding arising out of the motor vehicle accident. The report is therefore an investigative summary and a roadmap, not an exhibit. What it does is identify the people, vehicles, insurers, witnesses, and diagrams that the underlying admissible evidence can be built from. Anyone treating the officer's fault notation as the decisive document is relying on a paper the statute keeps out of the damages case.</p>
<h2>When the department can decline, and what remains</h2>
<p>Subsection (b)(5) permits a department to refuse to furnish the complete investigation copy while criminal charges are pending against anyone involved, unless the Rules of Criminal Procedure require production. In that situation the file has to be built from other sources. Section 3746 helps, because where an accident is reported and investigated, each driver is to be given a signed statement showing the accident was reported, which confirms a report exists even before a copy is released.</p>
<h2>The driver's own report</h2>
<p>Where no police officer investigated the crash, the duty shifts. Section 3747 requires the driver to submit a written report within the period the statute fixes, and the same section makes those reports confidential and limits their evidentiary use, allowing a certificate that a report was filed to be admitted for the fact of filing. Confidentiality is a feature rather than an obstacle: the report satisfies a legal obligation without becoming a statement to be argued over later.</p>
<h2>Evidence the report will not preserve for you</h2>
<p>A report records what an officer observed and was told. It does not preserve footage from the convenience store across the intersection, the damaged vehicle before repair, the shoulder debris before it was swept, the maintenance history of a commercial vehicle, or the phone number of the witness who left before the officer arrived. Those items are held by third parties on their own schedules, several of them measured in days. Written preservation requests sent early are the mechanism, and they cost nothing but attention.</p>

## svc_rideshare_and_delivery_vehicle_crashes_lede

When a rideshare driver is involved in a crash, the coverage question turns on what the app was doing at that moment. Pennsylvania sets one set of limits for a driver waiting for a request and a much larger one for a ride already underway.

## svc_rideshare_and_delivery_vehicle_crashes_body

<h2>The statute that governs the coverage</h2>
<p>Transportation network companies operating in Pennsylvania are regulated under Title 66, and the financial responsibility requirements sit at 66 Pa.C.S. 2603.1. The section requires that a driver, or the company on the driver's behalf, maintain primary automobile insurance that recognizes the driver as a transportation network company driver, or otherwise uses a vehicle to carry passengers for compensation, and that covers the driver both while logged on to the digital network and while engaged in a prearranged ride. Those two states are treated differently, and the difference is substantial.</p>
<h2>Logged on but not carrying a passenger</h2>
<p>While a participating driver is logged on to the network and available to receive requests but is not engaged in a prearranged ride, the statute requires primary automobile liability insurance of at least fifty thousand dollars for death and bodily injury per person, one hundred thousand dollars for death and bodily injury per incident, and twenty-five thousand dollars for property damage. It also requires first-party medical benefits including twenty-five thousand dollars for pedestrians and five thousand dollars for the driver. The requirement may be satisfied by insurance maintained by the driver, by the company, or by a combination of the two.</p>
<h2>Engaged in a prearranged ride</h2>
<p>While the driver is engaged in a prearranged ride, the required primary automobile liability insurance rises to at least five hundred thousand dollars for death, bodily injury, and property damage. First-party medical benefits at that stage are required as provided by 75 Pa.C.S. 1711 on a per-incident basis for incidents involving the driver's operation of a personal vehicle during a prearranged ride, including twenty-five thousand dollars for passengers and pedestrians and five thousand dollars for the driver. As with the lower tier, the coverage may come from the driver's policy, the company's policy, or both together.</p>
<h2>Coverage that does not wait for a denial</h2>
<p>Two provisions in the same section remove common obstacles. Coverage under a policy maintained under the section is primary, and the statute states that it is not dependent on a personal automobile insurer first denying a claim, nor is a personal policy required to deny a claim first. And where the insurance a driver was supposed to maintain has lapsed or does not provide the required coverage, insurance maintained by the transportation network company must provide the required coverage beginning with the first dollar of a claim, with the company's insurer owing a duty to defend. Together these provisions are meant to prevent an injured passenger or pedestrian from being passed between carriers.</p>
<h2>Drivers who do more than one thing</h2>
<p>The section includes a provision for dual motor carrier drivers using personal vehicles to provide transportation network services, requiring the same insurance coverage as taxis, and it allows the Public Utility Commission to increase the requirements for dual motor carriers and taxis in the public interest. Insurance required for a company must be evidenced by a certificate of insurance filed with the commission by the carrier, in the form the commission specifies. That filing requirement is useful, because it means a coverage question has a documentary answer somewhere other than in the driver's glove box.</p>
<h2>Establishing which state the app was in</h2>
<p>Because the limits turn on app status at the moment of the crash, the decisive evidence is electronic and held by a company that was not present at the scene. Trip records, the timestamp when a request was accepted, the pickup and dropoff times, and the driver's log-on history all sit in company systems. A written notice to the company identifying the date, time, location, and driver, sent as early as possible, is the practical step that puts the question on record while the data still exists.</p>
<h2>Delivery drivers, and where the analysis differs</h2>
<p>Food and package delivery using personal vehicles does not necessarily fall under the transportation network service provisions, which are written around prearranged rides for passengers. Coverage for a delivery crash may instead depend on the driver's own policy and its business-use provisions, on any commercial policy carried by the platform, and on whether the driver was performing work at the time. The analysis is contract driven and fact specific, and it should not be assumed to mirror the rideshare limits above.</p>

## svc_serious_injury_exception_lede

Under a limited tort policy, one phrase in the Vehicle Code decides whether pain and suffering stay on the table. Pennsylvania courts do not measure it by the diagnosis on the discharge paperwork. They measure it by what a body function can no longer do.

## svc_serious_injury_exception_body

<h2>The statutory phrase, and its three branches</h2>
<p>The definition sits in 75 Pa.C.S. 1702, and it is short enough to read in one breath. A serious injury means death, serious impairment of body function, or permanent serious disfigurement. Two of those three branches tend to resolve themselves on the record. Death is documented. Permanent serious disfigurement is usually visible and photographable, and treating physicians describe it in ordinary clinical language. The middle branch is where nearly every argument happens, because impairment is a functional idea rather than a category of injury, and the statute deliberately leaves the measuring to the fact finder.</p>
<h2>The two questions Pennsylvania courts ask</h2>
<p>In Washington v. Baxter, decided in 1998, the Pennsylvania Supreme Court adopted the analysis used in the Michigan decision DiFranco v. Pickard for testing this threshold. The inquiry runs in two steps. First, which body function was impaired by injuries from the motor vehicle accident. Second, was that impairment serious. The court directed attention away from the injury itself and toward the effect of the injury on the function, and it listed the considerations that make an impairment serious: how extensive it is, how long it lasted, and what treatment was required to correct it, along with any other relevant factor. The court also made clear that an impairment does not have to be permanent before it can be serious.</p>
<h2>Why the framing matters in practice</h2>
<p>Framing decides which records carry weight. A file organized around diagnoses produces a list of code numbers. A file organized around function produces something a fact finder can evaluate: a shoulder that cannot reach overhead to stock a shelf, a knee that stops a delivery route at the third flight of stairs, a grip that drops tools, a neck that cannot hold a position long enough to finish a shift. Both files may describe the same patient. Only the second one answers the question the statute asks, which is why the wording in a treating physician's notes often matters more than the volume of the records.</p>
<h2>What the medical proof has to establish</h2>
<p>Baxter also acknowledged the practical point that medical testimony is generally needed to establish the existence, extent, and permanency of an impairment. That has consequences for how treatment is documented while it is still happening. Work restrictions with dates and specific limits are more useful than a general instruction to take it easy. Objective findings tied to a functional loss carry further than pain ratings alone. Gaps in treatment invite an argument that the impairment resolved, whether or not it did, so the reason for any gap is worth recording as it occurs rather than reconstructing later.</p>
<h2>The exceptions that skip the threshold entirely</h2>
<p>The threshold is not the only route. Section 1705(d) lists circumstances in which a person who chose limited tort keeps the right to seek noneconomic loss without proving a serious injury, and those circumstances turn on facts about the other driver, the vehicle involved, or the nature of the conduct rather than on the severity of the injury. Because those exceptions are fact specific and are analyzed alongside the election documents themselves, they get separate treatment on the tort election page of this site. The point here is that a threshold argument is not always the only argument available.</p>
<h2>How this looks against statewide numbers</h2>
<p>PennDOT reported 4,713 suspected serious injuries statewide in 2024, out of 110,765 reportable crashes and 48,795 injury crashes for the year. Those figures come from a crash reporting form completed at the scene, not from a court's later reading of the tort threshold, and the two categories do not line up. An officer's suspected serious injury classification is one piece of evidence about severity. It is not the statutory test, and neither the presence nor the absence of that box being checked resolves the question a limited tort file has to answer.</p>

<p>The functional record is built during treatment, and it is far harder to reconstruct after a claim closes. Statements about what a person can no longer do are strongest when they were written down while it was true. It is general information rather than guidance about any individual injury, and specific questions belong with the attorney advertising on this site or another licensed Pennsylvania attorney.</p>

## svc_spinal_and_disc_injury_claims_lede

Disc and spinal injuries produce the longest treatment timelines and the most predictable defense argument: that the imaging shows aging rather than a collision. Pennsylvania's benefit statutes were written with long, expensive treatment in mind.

## svc_spinal_and_disc_injury_claims_body

<h2>Why spine cases run long</h2>
<p>Spinal complaints rarely resolve on a fixed schedule. A course of care can move through primary care, imaging, physical therapy, pain management, injections, and in some cases surgical consultation, spread over many months. Each step generates records held by a different provider, and each provider documents a slightly different version of the same problem. That length is what makes these claims administratively difficult. It is also why the statutory provisions about the duration of medical coverage matter more here than in a case that closes after six weeks of therapy.</p>
<h2>Medical benefits without a time limit, and the eighteen-month condition</h2>
<p>Section 1712 of the Vehicle Code lists the first-party benefits an insurer must make available, and paragraph (1) is unusually generous in one respect and conditional in another. The medical benefit covers reasonable and necessary medical treatment and rehabilitative services, and the statute lists categories including hospital, surgical, chiropractic, licensed physical therapy, nursing services, prosthetic devices, medications, and vocational rehabilitation. Those benefits are available without limitation as to time, but only where it is ascertainable with reasonable medical probability, within eighteen months of the accident, that further expenses may be incurred because of the injury. The condition is a documentation requirement in disguise: the treating record inside that window has to say that more care is expected.</p>
<h2>Extraordinary medical benefits</h2>
<p>Paragraph (6) of the same section defines extraordinary medical benefits as medical benefits exceeding one hundred thousand dollars. That category exists because catastrophic spinal injuries can outrun ordinary coverage limits, and it is an optional coverage a purchaser may or may not have selected years before a crash. Whether it appears on a household's policy is a declarations page question. Nobody discovers the answer at a convenient moment, which is an argument for reading the declarations page before it is needed rather than after.</p>
<h2>The four-year clock on benefits themselves</h2>
<p>Claims for first-party benefits have their own limitation period, separate from the deadline for suing the driver at fault. Section 1721 requires that an action for first-party benefits be commenced within four years of the accident where no benefits have been paid, and within four years of the last payment where benefits have been paid. For an injured minor, the section measures four years from the date the minor turns eighteen. The section also defines further benefits as expenses incurred no earlier than four years before an action begins. In a long spinal treatment course, where payments stop and later resume, the date of the last payment is worth tracking as it happens.</p>
<h2>The degenerative change argument</h2>
<p>The most common defense in disc cases is not that the collision did not occur. It is that the findings on imaging predate it. Degenerative changes are visible in a large share of adult spines, including in people with no symptoms at all, so the presence of degeneration on a scan resolves nothing by itself. What addresses the argument is the comparison the record makes possible: documented function before the crash, symptom onset in relation to the collision, whether prior imaging exists, and clinical correlation between the level of the finding and the distribution of symptoms. Prior records that show an asymptomatic history can be as useful as the new imaging.</p>
<h2>Coverage disputes over specific treatment</h2>
<p>Insurers may challenge the reasonableness and necessity of treatment through the peer review procedure in section 1797, and injections, extended therapy, and surgical recommendations are frequent subjects. The statute suspends the payment obligation in defined circumstances while a review proceeds and prohibits billing the insured for treatment under review. It also provides interest where treatment is later determined to have been necessary. Because that mechanism turns on the clinical reasoning in the chart, referrals that state a purpose and re-evaluations that record measured change carry more weight than volume of visits.</p>
<h2>What the tort threshold asks of a spine file</h2>
<p>Where the limited tort election applies, damages for pain and other nonmonetary detriment require a serious injury as defined in section 1702, and Pennsylvania courts examine impairment of a body function rather than the diagnosis. For a spinal injury, that means the record should show what the spine can no longer do and for how long: lifting limits with numbers, sitting or standing tolerance, sleep disruption, and specific tasks abandoned at work or at home. Permanence is not required for an impairment to be serious, but duration is one of the factors considered.</p>

## svc_statute_of_limitations_pennsylvania_lede

There is no single deadline after a Pennsylvania crash. There are several, they run from different dates, and at least one of them can end a claim against a public entity six months in, long before anyone is thinking about court.

## svc_statute_of_limitations_pennsylvania_body

<h2>The two-year period for injury claims</h2>
<p>The general rule sits at 42 Pa.C.S. 5524. Paragraph (2) requires that an action to recover damages for injuries to the person, or for the death of an individual caused by the wrongful act, neglect, unlawful violence, or negligence of another, be commenced within two years. Paragraph (7) covers other actions founded on negligent, intentional, or otherwise tortious conduct within the same two years. The period is measured from accrual, which in an ordinary collision is the date of the crash. Filing an insurance claim, exchanging correspondence with an adjuster, or receiving payments toward treatment does not extend it.</p>
<h2>The four-year period for benefits under your own policy</h2>
<p>A separate clock governs claims for first-party benefits. Section 1721 of the Vehicle Code requires an action for first-party benefits to be commenced within four years of the accident where no benefits have been paid, and within four years of the last payment where benefits have been paid. For a minor entitled to those benefits, the four years run from the date the minor turns eighteen. The section also limits further benefits to expenses incurred no earlier than four years before the action begins. The result is that the deadline for suing an insurer over unpaid benefits and the deadline for suing a driver are different lengths, running from different events.</p>
<h2>Six months, when a government unit is involved</h2>
<p>The provision most likely to catch a claim unaware is 42 Pa.C.S. 5522. Within six months of the injury or accrual, a person about to bring a civil action against a government unit for damages must file a written statement in the office of that government unit, and also with the Attorney General where the defendant is a Commonwealth agency. The statement must set out the name and residence address of the person to whom the cause of action accrued and of the person injured, the date and hour of the accident, the approximate location, and the name and address of any attending physician. If the statement is not filed, the statute directs dismissal of an action commenced more than six months after the injury, subject to the excuse provisions in the same section. Crashes involving a municipal vehicle, a transit vehicle, or an alleged roadway defect can all raise this question.</p>
<h2>Minors and the tolling rule</h2>
<p>Section 5533 of the Judicial Code addresses infancy. Where an individual entitled to bring a civil action is an unemancipated minor when the cause of action accrues, the period of minority is not counted as part of the limitation period, and the person has the same time to commence an action after reaching majority as anyone else would have. The section defines a minor as a person who has not yet reached eighteen. That rule and the separate four-year benefits provision are frequently confused, and they answer different questions.</p>
<h2>The reporting deadline that arrives first</h2>
<p>Long before any limitation period matters, the Vehicle Code imposes a reporting duty. Section 3747 requires a driver involved in a reportable crash to submit a written report to the department within a period the statute specifies, where the accident was not investigated by police. The section also makes those driver reports confidential and restricts their use as evidence, allowing a certificate stating that a report was filed. The reporting obligation exists independently of any claim, and satisfying it early avoids a separate problem later.</p>
<h2>Deadlines that no statute prints</h2>
<p>Evidence has its own calendar. Camera footage is overwritten on retention cycles frequently measured in days. Vehicles move to salvage. Roadway conditions are repaired. Witness memory degrades and phone numbers change. Insurance policies contain their own notice and cooperation conditions, and for uninsured claims involving an unidentified vehicle the Vehicle Code adds a notice requirement measured in days rather than years. None of those appear in the limitations statutes, and all of them can decide a claim before a statutory deadline is anywhere close.</p>
<h2>Reading this page</h2>
<p>Limitation analysis depends on when a cause of action accrued, who the defendants are, and which statutory provisions apply to each of them. This page summarizes Pennsylvania statutes in general terms for readers in the Harrisburg area and is not advice about any particular deadline. Anyone concerned about a specific date should raise it promptly with the attorney whose office advertises on this site, or with another licensed Pennsylvania attorney.</p>

## svc_underinsured_motorist_claims_lede

The driver at fault carried insurance, and it was not enough. Pennsylvania's underinsured motorist rules decide which policy pays first, how high the coverage can go, and what a signed request years ago may have done to the limits.

## svc_underinsured_motorist_claims_body

<h2>What underinsured coverage is for</h2>
<p>Section 1702 of the Vehicle Code defines an underinsured motor vehicle as one for which liability coverage exists but the limits are insufficient to pay the losses and damages. That definition marks the gap this coverage fills. It is not a claim against the other driver's insurer for more than the policy provides. It is a first-party claim under the injured person's own policy, or another policy in the priority chain, for the shortfall between what the liability coverage pays and what the loss is. Because it is a claim against an insurer that also collected the premium, the relationship is contractual, and the documents that define it are the policy and its declarations page.</p>
<h2>The order in which policies pay</h2>
<p>Where several policies could apply, 75 Pa.C.S. 1733 sets the sequence. First in priority is a policy covering the motor vehicle the injured person occupied at the time of the accident. Second is a policy covering a vehicle not involved in the accident under which the injured person is an insured. The statute then handles the practical problem of equal priority: the insurer that receives the claim first has to process and pay it as though it were wholly responsible, and afterward may recover a proportional contribution from any other insurer for the benefits paid and the cost of processing the claim. That structure exists so an injured passenger is not caught between two carriers pointing at each other.</p>
<h2>The written request that lowers limits</h2>
<p>One provision explains a surprising number of thin coverage situations. Section 1734 allows a named insured to request in writing that these coverages be issued in amounts equal to or less than the bodily injury liability limits on the policy. A request under that section is a document with a signature, and its effect can persist across renewals long after the person who signed it has forgotten the conversation. When a household discovers that its underinsured limits sit far below its liability limits, the explanation is often a written request in the underwriting file rather than an error.</p>
<h2>The ceiling on how high coverage goes</h2>
<p>Section 1736 sets the other boundary. Insurers may offer these coverages in amounts higher than the statutory minimums, but not greater than the bodily injury liability limits in the insured's own policy. In other words, a policy cannot carry more underinsured protection than it carries liability protection for others. That relationship is useful to know while reviewing coverage before anything happens, because raising one number is a precondition to raising the other.</p>
<h2>Stacking, household vehicles, and why the policy language matters</h2>
<p>Multiple-vehicle households raise questions about whether limits combine and whether every vehicle in the driveway is treated the same way. Those questions are answered by policy language and by Pennsylvania appellate decisions that have moved over time, and the answers differ between carriers and between policy forms. What is stable is the method: read the definitions section of the actual policy in force on the date of the crash, identify every policy in the household, and get each declarations page in writing. Summaries provided over the phone are not the contract.</p>
<h2>The sequence that protects the claim</h2>
<p>Underinsured claims usually cannot be evaluated until the liability side is quantified, and settling with the at-fault driver's insurer can affect the underinsured claim depending on policy conditions and notice requirements. Because of that, the order of operations matters as much as the substance. Notifying the underinsured carrier in writing that a claim may follow, before resolving anything with the liability insurer, keeps the sequence clean. Waiting until the liability release is signed inverts it.</p>
<h2>Documents that decide the outcome</h2>
<p>A complete underinsured file has the liability policy limits confirmed in writing, every household declarations page for the date of loss, any section 1734 written request in the underwriting file, the medical and wage documentation supporting the shortfall, and the correspondence showing when each insurer was notified. The dispute is usually about the size of the gap. The paperwork above is what establishes that a gap exists at all.</p>

<p>Uninsured motorist claims, where no liability coverage exists at all, follow a different statutory path and are covered separately on this site. Everything here is general information about Pennsylvania statutes for readers in the Harrisburg area, not advice about any individual policy or claim. Coverage questions turn on the specific contract language, and they belong with the attorney whose office advertises on this site or another licensed Pennsylvania attorney.</p>

## svc_whiplash_and_soft_tissue_injuries_lede

Soft tissue injuries are the most commonly disputed category in Pennsylvania auto claims, because they are real, they are hard to photograph, and the statute gives insurers a formal mechanism for challenging the treatment that documents them.

## svc_whiplash_and_soft_tissue_injuries_body

<h2>Why these injuries generate arguments</h2>
<p>A fracture appears on an image and settles the debate about whether something happened. Strains, sprains, disc irritation, and the cluster of neck and upper back complaints usually grouped as whiplash frequently do not appear that way. The proof is functional and clinical: range of motion measurements, palpation findings, the pattern of symptoms over weeks, and the response to treatment. Insurers know this category is difficult to verify, and the resulting disputes are less about whether the collision occurred than about how much of the treatment it required.</p>
<h2>The peer review process the Vehicle Code created</h2>
<p>Section 1797 of the Vehicle Code gives insurers a defined route for challenging treatment rather than simply refusing to pay. Subsection (b) allows an insurer to contest the reasonableness and necessity of treatment by referring the matter to a peer review organization. The timing is regulated: a challenge to a bill for treatment already rendered must be made within ninety days of the insurer's receipt of the bill, while ongoing treatment may be referred at any time. A provider dissatisfied with the determination may request reconsideration within thirty days. A referral made within the first thirty days after a bill is received suspends the payment obligation while the review proceeds.</p>
<h2>What peer review means for the patient</h2>
<p>Two protections in the same subsection matter to the injured person directly. While a claim is being challenged through peer review, the insured may not be billed for the treatment under review. And if a peer review organization or a court later determines that the treatment was medically necessary, the insurer owes the bill with interest at twelve percent per year. The statute goes further where an insurer's conduct is found to be wanton, allowing treble damages on the amount of benefits withheld, and it permits an award of costs and attorney fees to a claimant who prevails in court on that issue.</p>
<h2>The cap on what a provider may charge</h2>
<p>Subsection (a) limits provider charges rather than patient obligations. A provider may not require payment above the lower of the provider's usual and customary charge or a percentage tied to Medicare's applicable payment mechanism, set at one hundred ten percent, with a separate approach where no Medicare measure applies and an exception for trauma center and burn facility treatment of severely injured patients. The subsection also states that a provider subject to those limits may not bill or otherwise attempt to collect the difference from the insured. That is the answer to a common worry after treatment ends and a balance appears in the mail.</p>
<h2>Documentation that survives a challenge</h2>
<p>Because the dispute is about necessity rather than existence, the useful records are the ones that show reasoning. Referrals with a stated clinical purpose, objective measurements repeated over time so improvement or plateau is visible, work restrictions with specific limits and dates, and notes that connect symptoms to the mechanism of the collision all address the question a reviewer asks. Long unexplained gaps in treatment do the opposite, and where a gap has a reason, such as a work schedule, a waiting list, or a lack of transport, that reason is worth recording when it happens rather than explaining afterward.</p>
<h2>Where soft tissue meets the tort threshold</h2>
<p>A driver bound by the limited tort election faces a second question beyond payment of bills. Under section 1705(d), damages for pain and other nonmonetary detriment require a serious injury as defined in section 1702, and the middle branch of that definition is serious impairment of body function. Soft tissue cases therefore turn on documented functional loss over time rather than on the diagnosis label, which is a different evidentiary target from the one peer review addresses. Both records get built during the same course of treatment.</p>
<h2>The first-party benefits side</h2>
<p>Treatment in the early months is often paid through the medical benefit that every Pennsylvania auto policy must carry, and the coordination between that benefit, health insurance, and any later recovery follows separate statutory rules addressed elsewhere on this site. What matters here is sequencing: bills routed correctly from the start are far easier to reconcile than bills reassigned a year later.</p>

It contains no medical guidance and no assessment of any injury, and it does not predict how any claim will be handled. Treatment decisions belong with a licensed clinician, and questions about a claim belong with the attorney whose office advertises on this site or another licensed Pennsylvania attorney.</p>

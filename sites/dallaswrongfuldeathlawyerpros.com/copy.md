## symptom_1_title

Someone in the family was killed in a Dallas crash

## symptom_1

Most fatal collisions in Dallas County do not happen on the interstates people assume. In 2024 the county recorded 305 fatal crashes and 331 traffic deaths, and 133 of those fatal crashes were on city streets, against 81 on interstate highways. That is a Ross Avenue, a Buckner Boulevard, a wide arterial through Pleasant Grove — surface roads with signals, turn lanes and pedestrians, where liability turns on signal timing, sight lines and who had the right of way rather than on lane changes at speed.

Chapter 71 of the Civil Practice and Remedies Code makes a person liable for damages arising from an injury that causes a death when the injury was caused by that person's wrongful act, neglect, carelessness, unskillfulness or default. Those are the statute's own words, and they are broader than the word "accident" suggests. The driver is the obvious defendant. A trucking company, a vehicle owner who lent the keys, a commercial employer whose driver was working at the time, or a road contractor can also fall inside that language.

Two practical things. First, the police report and the medical examiner's file are the records everyone else will work from, and they are not always right at first; investigators amend them. Second, an insurance representative may call the household within days, sometimes asking for a recorded account of a death nobody has processed yet. You are not obliged to give one, and declining costs you nothing. The claim itself is measured from the date of death, not from when the paperwork settles down.

## symptom_2_title

A death at work, and workers' compensation is the only thing anyone has mentioned

## symptom_2

When a worker dies and the employer carried workers' compensation coverage, the Texas Labor Code makes those benefits the exclusive remedy against that employer. Families hear this as the end of the discussion. It is not, and there are three reasons why.

The first is that the exclusive remedy rule protects the employer, not everyone at the site. Dallas work deaths often involve a general contractor, a subcontractor whose crew was not the deceased worker's crew, an equipment owner, a property owner, or a driver from another company. A claim against any of those parties is an ordinary Chapter 71 wrongful death claim and is unaffected by the compensation carrier.

The second is the exception written into the Labor Code itself. Section 408.001 does not bar the surviving spouse or the heirs of the body of a deceased employee from recovering exemplary damages where the death was caused by an intentional act or omission of the employer, or by the employer's gross negligence. That is a high standard and a narrow door, but it is a real one.

The third is that Texas does not require private employers to carry compensation coverage at all. A non-subscribing employer has no exclusive remedy protection, and a death at such a company is litigated as a negligence case.

The records matter more here than in almost any other kind of claim, because they are held by the parties with the least reason to volunteer them: the site safety file, the training records, the equipment maintenance log, the daily report, and any federal safety inspection opened after the death.

## symptom_3_title

A death that followed hospital, clinic or nursing care

## symptom_3

A death arising out of medical treatment is not handled under Chapter 71 alone. It becomes a health care liability claim, and Chapter 74 adds requirements that nothing else in Texas civil practice matches for severity.

Written notice must go to each physician or provider at least 60 days before suit is filed, sent by certified mail with a release for the protected health information. Then, no later than the 120th day after each defendant files an original answer, the claimant must serve an expert report on that defendant, from a qualified expert, addressing the standard of care, how it was departed from, and how that departure caused the death. If the report does not arrive in time, the court must dismiss the claim against that provider with prejudice and award that provider its fees and costs. A timely report found deficient can be cured once, with a single 30-day extension. A late one cannot be cured at all.

Chapter 74 also caps what it calls noneconomic damages — the loss of the relationship itself. Against a physician or a provider that is not an institution, the limit is $250,000 for each claimant, no matter how many defendants are named. Against one health care institution the limit is another $250,000 per claimant, and where more than one institution is held liable the total against institutions is capped at $500,000 per claimant. Economic losses, such as the income the household depended on, are not capped by that section.

None of this makes such a claim impossible. It makes the medical records review the first real step rather than a later one.

## symptom_4_title

Nobody has explained who is even allowed to bring the claim

## symptom_4

This is the question families ask last and should ask first, because Texas answers it narrowly. Section 71.004 says the action is for the exclusive benefit of the surviving spouse, the children and the parents of the person who died. Those three categories are the whole list.

Children means children of any age; an adult daughter in Lake Highlands has the same standing as a minor child. Parents means the mother and father of the person who died, whatever that person's age at death. Adopted children and adoptive parents are treated as blood relatives for this purpose. Siblings are not on the list, and a brother or sister of the person who died cannot bring a Texas wrongful death claim in their own right, however close they were. Grandparents are not on the list either.

One member of that group may file the action for the benefit of all of them, so relatives who are not speaking to each other do not each need a separate suit. If none of the eligible relatives has begun an action within three calendar months of the death, the statute directs the executor or administrator of the estate to bring it, unless all of those relatives ask that it not be brought. Where damages are awarded, the jury divides them in the shares it finds, among the eligible people who are alive at that time.

There is also a second, separate claim. Under Section 71.021 the injured person's own cause of action does not die with them; it survives to the heirs, the legal representatives and the estate. That survival claim covers what was done to the person before death. The two claims are usually filed together and answer to different rules.

## qa_1_question

Can a brother or sister file a wrongful death claim in Texas?

## qa_1_answer

No. Section 71.004 limits the action to the exclusive benefit of the surviving spouse, the children and the parents of the person who died, and a sibling is not in that group. It does not matter whether the brother or sister was the closest person in the family's life, or the one handling every arrangement. A sibling who is an heir may still have a role in the separate survival claim brought on behalf of the estate under Section 71.021, and a sibling serving as executor or administrator may end up bringing the wrongful death action itself if no eligible relative has started one within three calendar months of the death. Those are different routes into the case, not an exception to the list.

## qa_2_question

Where would a Dallas County case like this be filed?

## qa_2_answer

Civil suits in Dallas County are handled downtown at the George L. Allen, Sr. Courts Building on Commerce Street. The county maintains thirteen civil district courts there — the 14th, 44th, 68th, 95th, 101st, 116th, 134th, 160th, 162nd, 191st, 192nd, 193rd and 298th — alongside five county courts at law. The county clerk's own guidance states that a Dallas County court at law has concurrent jurisdiction with the district courts in civil cases where the amount in controversy exceeds $500, with no ceiling above that, and that the choice between a district court and a county court at law belongs to the party filing. Where a suit belongs depends on where the death occurred and where the defendants are, so a collision on a Collin County road does not automatically become a Dallas County case.

## qa_3_question

Is there a limit on what a Dallas family can recover?

## qa_3_answer

It depends entirely on who the claim is against. In an ordinary claim against a private driver or a private company, Texas sets no cap on compensatory damages for a death. Two situations change that. A health care liability claim runs into the Chapter 74 caps on noneconomic damages, which are $250,000 per claimant against a physician or non-institutional provider, a further $250,000 per claimant against a single health care institution, and $500,000 per claimant where more than one institution is liable. A claim against a governmental body runs into the Texas Tort Claims Act, which limits a municipality to $250,000 per person and $500,000 per occurrence for death, and other units of local government to less than that. Section 71.009 separately allows exemplary damages where a death was caused by a wilful act or omission or by gross negligence.

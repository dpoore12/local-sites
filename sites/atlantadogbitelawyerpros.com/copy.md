## symptom_1_title
You were bitten, and the owner says the dog never acted this way before

## symptom_1
A first-time explanation is where Georgia cases become very different from the strict-liability rule people may have heard about elsewhere. Under Georgia Code section 51-2-7, a claim can involve proof that the dog was vicious or dangerous, careless management or allowing it to go at liberty, an injury, and no provocation by the person hurt. That means a bite does not automatically settle responsibility merely because the bite happened. The earlier history of the dog, what its owner knew, and how it was being handled become central questions.

That is the practical meaning of the so-called first-bite problem. It is not a rule that a person must accept one free attack. It means an incident needs evidence that connects the injury to a known dangerous tendency or to another legal route for establishing that issue. A prior bite is one possible clue, but it is not the only fact worth preserving. Earlier lunging, attacks on other animals, warnings to visitors, texts about keeping the dog away from people, fence failures, animal-control reports, or a property rule can all be worth investigating.

Georgia’s statute also contains an important leash provision. When a local ordinance required the dog to be at heel or on a leash and the dog was not restrained that way at the time, that can be sufficient proof of vicious propensity under the statute. The ordinance’s location and wording matter. Atlanta’s dog-control provisions are not identical across every county portion of the city, so the address should be pinned down rather than guessed.

Do not argue the point with the owner at the scene. Get medical attention first, take clear photos if you can do so without delaying care, identify witnesses, and save messages or posts that could disappear. A legal review can then determine what evidence actually supports the claim.

## symptom_3_title

## symptom_3
A dog bite at a rental does not automatically make every person connected with the property responsible. It creates a map of separate questions: who owned or kept the dog, who controlled the yard, hallway, gate, or common area, who had notice of a problem, and what written rules or complaints existed before the incident. The dog owner may be obvious. The harder part is often finding records that show what a property manager, landlord, homeowner, or association knew and could control.

Start with the place itself. Take photographs of the doorway, fence, broken latch, common walkway, posted rules, unit number, or gate exactly as it looked. If there was a camera, ask in writing that video be preserved quickly; many systems overwrite footage. Save the lease if you have it, messages about the dog, maintenance requests, prior complaint emails, and any animal registration or pet addendum. Neighbors may know about previous escapes or warnings, but their actual names and contact details are more valuable than a secondhand summary.

Georgia’s vicious-propensity rule makes notice a serious issue, and a property situation can add another layer because knowledge and control may sit with different people. A “beware of dog” sign, repeated leash complaints, a warning from a building manager, or a prior report can be relevant, but none should be treated as a guaranteed outcome. The documents must be read against the specific circumstances.

For attacks in the City of Atlanta, even the county portion of the address can matter for the applicable local dog-control provisions. ## qa_1_question
Does Georgia have automatic liability whenever a dog bites someone?

## qa_1_answer
No. Georgia Code section 51-2-7 is not California-style automatic liability. It addresses a person who owns or keeps a vicious or dangerous animal and, through careless management or allowing it to go at liberty, causes an unprovoked injury. Evidence about the dog’s dangerous tendency and what the owner or keeper knew can matter. The statute also says it can be sufficient to prove vicious propensity when a local ordinance required the dog at heel or on a leash and the dog was not restrained that way at the time. That is fact-specific, so preserve the address, restraint evidence, witnesses, and any earlier history.

## qa_2_question
What does Atlanta’s leash rule have to do with a dog-bite claim?

## qa_2_answer
The exact rule depends on the location. In the portion of Atlanta within DeKalb County, the city code says an owner or person in control may not permit a dog to be out of control and unattended off the owner’s premises, or on someone else’s property without permission. It defines control to include a leash, being at heel, or being beside and obedient to a competent person. Georgia’s dog-injury statute specifically recognizes a local leash or at-heel requirement as potentially sufficient proof of vicious propensity when the animal was not restrained. The location and the evidence from that moment are therefore important.

## qa_3_question
Should I report a dog bite to Fulton County Animal Services?

## qa_3_answer
Fulton County Animal Services lists dog bites as a reason residents can request field service and says animal bites are among the after-hours emergencies it handles. That can be useful for public-safety and animal-control purposes. It is not a substitute for medical care or for preserving your own claim information. Keep the report number if one is created, but also retain photographs, provider records, witness contact details, messages, and the exact place where the attack happened. A report does not decide a civil claim by itself, and no one should assume that every relevant fact will be captured in an agency record.

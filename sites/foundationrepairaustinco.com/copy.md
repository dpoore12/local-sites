## symptom_1_title

Hairline cracks, or cracks that are still changing?

## symptom_1

Concrete, drywall, plaster, and brick do not all age the same way. A thin drywall line that stays the same length for years, a small gap at one piece of trim, or a crack that appeared after ordinary settling can be cosmetic. That does not make it invisible, but it does mean “a crack exists” is not a repair design.

The more useful question is whether several changes make the same map. A diagonal crack running from a window or door corner, brick mortar opening in a step pattern, a separation that widens from one season to the next, or damage concentrated along one side of the structure deserves a measured assessment. Put a pencil date beside the end of a crack or photograph it with a ruler. Recheck it after a few months. Change over time is evidence; a single alarming photo is not.

Austin makes the timing especially important. A home over clay can respond differently through a dry spell and after the soil takes on moisture again. A home on shallow limestone may show a different pattern altogether, including issues tied to an uneven bearing surface or fill at a lot edge. Neither condition can be confirmed from a crack alone. The location, direction, width, matching exterior signs, and floor elevations all matter.

Do not seal a moving crack merely to make it disappear before someone evaluates it. Cosmetic patching can be appropriate after the structure is stable, but it should not erase the information needed to decide whether the crack is seasonal, old, or active. An on-site visit should distinguish a monitoring situation from a pattern that needs structural work, then state the scope and cost before work starts.

## symptom_2_title

Doors drag, cabinets pull apart, and the floor no longer feels level

## symptom_2

A sticking interior door does not automatically mean a foundation problem. Wood moves with indoor humidity, hinges loosen, and a door can rub after a minor adjustment to the jamb. One door that catches in wet weather is a maintenance item until other evidence says otherwise. The concern rises when doors, cabinet reveals, baseboards, and floors all change in related parts of the house.

Look for a cluster. A door that used to latch but now leaves a tapered gap, a cabinet that separates from the wall, a floor that consistently feels lower toward one room, and new cracks near the same corner create a pattern worth measuring. A level placed on one small tile is not enough; a meaningful evaluation uses a systematic elevation survey across the accessible floor and compares high and low areas with the visual evidence outside.

On Austin’s eastern and southeastern clay ground, movement may be seasonal without being harmless. The practical distinction is repeatability and trend. If a door binds every late summer and returns to normal without the surrounding cracks changing, keep records and ask about moisture management around the foundation. If the door is progressively harder to close, the gap above it grows, and nearby finishes are separating, it is time for an on-site assessment rather than another hinge adjustment.

On the western limestone side, a technician still should not assume the answer is “no movement.” Shallow rock, a sloping lot, and variable material beneath additions can produce localized support differences. The repair should follow measured conditions, not the neighborhood’s reputation. Expect the findings to name the affected area, explain what was observed, and separate necessary structural work from optional cosmetic repairs.

## symptom_3_title

Brick steps, exterior gaps, or a slab edge that looks different

## symptom_3

Exterior clues often tell more of the story than an indoor wall crack. Brick veneer can open in a stair-step pattern when part of the supporting structure changes elevation. Trim may separate at a window corner, a gap can appear where a porch meets the house, or caulk lines may pull open on one wall. These signs do not prove a specific failure by themselves; brick, trim, and concrete each respond to movement differently. Together, though, they help locate where a house is behaving differently from the rest of the structure.

Start by looking for direction and consistency. A short, old mortar crack that has been painted over and never changed is not the same as multiple fresh openings that taper toward one corner. A gap at an addition can be an ordinary construction joint, while a gap that grows alongside sloping floors and interior cracking should be mapped. Photograph the outside elevation as a whole, then the individual openings with a ruler or coin for scale. Avoid filling the gaps until the source is understood.

Austin’s geology is why a blanket answer is risky. The USGS description of the Austin area identifies a higher western district associated with the Edwards Plateau and a lower eastern district at the Coastal Plain margin. Clay-rich ground east of that divide and rockier ground west of it do not call for the same assumptions. Site grading, fill, and the shape of the structure can matter as much as the address.

An evaluation should examine the exterior all the way around, compare it with interior observations and floor elevations, and say whether the issue appears localized or part of a larger pattern. If repair is recommended, ask where support is being added, what evidence supports that placement, and how the work will be documented. A line of piers is not a diagnosis. It is a repair method that needs a site-specific reason.

## symptom_4_title

You were told you need piers before anyone measured the house

## symptom_4

A no-charge visit from a company that installs piers can be useful for learning what that company proposes. It is not the same thing as an independent engineering evaluation. The difference is not the word “inspection.” It is the purpose, the measurements, and who is responsible for the conclusion.

A thorough engineer’s report normally identifies the property and observed conditions, records relevant crack locations and directions, includes floor-elevation data or another documented measurement method, notes accessible exterior and structural observations, and explains the opinion about probable movement or distress. It should state the recommended action if one is warranted, including when monitoring or further investigation is more appropriate than immediate underpinning. The report should also define limits: concealed conditions, inaccessible areas, and items outside the engineer’s scope.

A repair-company assessment may also include useful measurements and photographs, but the company earns its work by selling a repair. Read its proposal as a repair scope, not as a substitute for independent design when the evidence is mixed, the house has a complicated addition, or the recommendation is extensive. Ask what was measured, what ruled out other causes, which elevations support the proposed pier locations, and whether a post-repair level survey is included.

This matters in Austin because a generic “expansive soil” explanation can skip the real question: what is happening at this particular house on this particular lot. Houston Black and Taylor clay, shallow limestone, fill, slope, and drainage conditions can produce different patterns. A responsible plan links the remedy to documented movement. It does not promise that every crack will vanish or sell a uniform pier layout before the building has been measured.

## qa_1_question

How can I tell seasonal movement from structural failure?

## qa_1_answer

Start with change, pattern, and measurement. One stable hairline crack or one humidity-sensitive door may be cosmetic. Concern rises when several symptoms appear in the same area: widening diagonal or stair-step cracks, doors that increasingly bind, persistent floor slope, exterior gaps, or a slab edge that has changed. Date and photograph the evidence from the same location. An on-site evaluation should compare those observations with a systematic floor-elevation survey and exterior conditions. In Austin, a seasonal clay response can be real, but a repeated or worsening pattern still deserves a plan rather than a guess.

## qa_2_question

What should a real engineer’s report contain that a free inspection may not?

## qa_2_answer

An engineering report should document what was observed and how the conclusion was reached. Look for property identification, accessible-condition observations, crack locations and directions, elevation data or another stated measurement method, photographs or diagrams, an explanation of the opinion, recommended action if justified, and limits on what could not be observed. A repair-company visit can be useful, but it often ends in that company’s repair proposal. For a complex or costly recommendation, an independent engineer’s report gives you a separate basis for deciding whether to repair, monitor, investigate further, or seek competing scopes.

Austin crosses a major geologic boundary. The Austin quadrangle includes higher Edwards Plateau ground to the west and lower Coastal Plain ground to the east, divided by the Balcones escarpment. ## pricing_body

### A pier price is not a job price

The most common way a homeowner misreads this market is by anchoring on the per-pier number. Austin companies publish per-pier figures between roughly $900 for a pressed concrete piling and $4,000 for a helical steel pier driven to load-bearing capacity. Both are real, and neither answers the question, because the total is pier price times pier count, plus mobilization, access, and whatever gets put back. Eight to twelve piers along one settled side is a different project from twenty-four around a full perimeter, and the published totals show it.

Pier count is not really negotiable either. It comes out of an elevation survey and a load path, so a lower count at the same coverage is a weaker repair. Compare pier type, count, depth criteria, and lift target first; the dollar figure only means something once those match.

### Method drives cost more than brand does

Pressed concrete pilings are cylinders driven hydraulically in segments until refusal, and they suit a typical slab in stable material at the lower end of the band. Steel push piers go deeper and carry more. Helical piers are turned in to a measured torque where soil is inconsistent or a lift must be precise, and they top the per-pier band. Injection is a separate category: it fills voids and lifts flatwork rather than supporting the structure, so it is no substitute for underpinning.

Access decides the rest. Exterior piers along a clear side yard go quickly. Interior piers mean cutting the slab inside or tunneling from outside, and tunneling is priced by the foot. Add a mature pecan, a patio to demolish, or a plumbing line under the slab, and the same pier count costs more.

### The Austin soil question, stated precisely

The USDA official series description for the Austin soil series puts clay at 35 to 55 percent of the particle-size control section. That single fraction is the mechanism behind nearly every slab complaint in this city. High-clay material expands when wet and contracts when dry, and a slab on it rides that movement seasonally. What makes the metro unusual is how sharply the ground changes across it. West of the Balcones escarpment the material is thin over limestone, so failures there tend to be localized. East and southeast the clay is deep, and movement is broad and cyclical.

So two houses ten minutes apart get different diagnoses and different prices, and a contractor quoting a standard package without an elevation survey has skipped the part that decides both. Drought followed by heavy rain is the worst case, because a slab dropping along a dry perimeter gets pushed back unevenly when the clay rehydrates.

### What the engineering standard actually says

The Texas Section ASCE guidelines for the evaluation and repair of residential foundations, Version 2, state that the evaluation and repair design shall be performed by a professional engineer licensed in the State of Texas, and that the engineer who seals the documents is the engineer of record with approval authority over changes. The same guidelines adopt the residential code deflection limit of L over 360 as the analogous limit for judging a foundation profile, and note that a floor slope greater than 1 percent is usually noticeable.

Read that against how repairs get sold. Drywall cracks and a sticking door are symptoms, not measurements. The engineer's question is whether the measured profile exceeds the deflection limit and whether symptoms support that conclusion. That is why the evaluation row earns its cost on any job past a few piers: a sealed report sets an independent lift target and a document to hold the work against. Austin lists foundation repair among the residential minor repairs handled through its Residential Express Building Permit, so paperwork is light but an inspection still attaches.

### Reading a fair foundation proposal

It includes the elevation readings in a diagram with low points marked. It states pier type, count, and locations, plus the refusal or torque criteria used to stop driving. It states the intended lift in inches and admits what will crack during it. It names who runs a plumbing test before and after, which matters on clay where a leaking sewer line under a slab is often the cause rather than the consequence. It separates patching, landscaping, and cosmetic repair into their own lines, and puts warranty terms and transferability in writing.

### What these numbers exclude

The plumbing repair if a hydrostatic test fails. Drainage correction, root barriers, and grading, which are what keep a fix stable. Drywall, tile, and paint after a lift. Fence sections removed for access. Engineering fees bought independently. And the second phase some houses need after a season of watching.

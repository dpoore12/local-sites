## symptom_1_title
A parenting schedule is proposed, but it does not reflect how the child actually lives

## symptom_1
Florida does not use “custody” as a complete answer to the questions parents have after separation. A court-approved parenting plan must address the daily work of raising the child, a time-sharing schedule, health care, school matters, activities, communication, and exchange arrangements. The issue is not simply which parent gets more days. The plan has to work with the child’s school week, medical needs, transportation, activities, routines, and the adults who can reliably carry those responsibilities.

Florida law begins with the child’s best interests. It also creates a rebuttable presumption that equal time-sharing is in the child’s best interests unless the evidence shows otherwise. That is not an automatic calendar split, and it is not a reward or penalty for either parent. The statute directs the court to examine the particular family: each parent’s ability to encourage the child’s relationship with the other parent; the child’s stability and continuity; the practical travel demands of the plan; each parent’s knowledge of school, health care, and daily life; the past division of parenting tasks; communication; routine; and developmental needs, among other factors.

Start with specifics rather than broad claims. Write down the existing school and care schedule, who handles pick-up and appointments, commute times, activity commitments, medical or educational needs, and the proposed exchange details. A parent who plans to move, work a changing shift, or rely on third-party care should identify that reality early. In Hillsborough County cases with minor children, the circuit’s standing order also calls for both parents to complete an approved parent-education course within 60 days after filing. A parenting plan needs legal review before it is signed or submitted because a workable-looking schedule can carry long-term decision-making and support consequences.

## symptom_2_title
A support payment no longer matches the household’s current circumstances

## symptom_2
Child support is not a number chosen from one parent’s monthly bill list. Florida uses guidelines, but the calculation depends on information that must be accurate: income, allowable deductions, health-insurance cost, child-care expense, the number of overnights in the time-sharing schedule, and other circumstances recognized by law. A proposed parenting plan and a support figure should be read together. A change in the actual schedule, insurance, care cost, work arrangement, or income can affect the analysis, even when the conversation began as a disagreement about one payment.

The first practical job is to separate a short-term strain from facts that can be documented. Gather recent pay records, tax returns, insurance information, child-care statements, the current order, and a calendar that shows the real parenting schedule. If income is irregular, records from more than one month may be needed to make the pattern understandable. Do not rely only on a text-message summary, an online balance, or a verbal understanding about who has been paying for a child’s needs.

Changing an existing order is different from informally agreeing to handle a month differently. The legal question can include what order is in force, whether the facts have materially changed, the effective date of any requested relief, and whether a revised plan or worksheet is needed. A parent should continue to read the existing order carefully rather than assuming a private arrangement has replaced it. If a petition or request for modification has been served, preserve the paperwork and note every response or hearing date. In the Thirteenth Judicial Circuit, family-law procedure can also include local standing requirements and case-management steps that are not apparent from a generic online form.

## symptom_3_title
Parentage has not been established, or the existing legal relationship is disputed

## symptom_3
A child’s daily relationship with an adult and that adult’s legal parentage are not always the same question. When parents were not married at the time of birth, or when the legal status is uncertain, a family-law matter may need to establish parentage before the court can enter enforceable terms about parental responsibility, time-sharing, child support, health insurance, or a parenting plan. This is why a disagreement that begins with access to a child can quickly require attention to documents, genetic-testing issues, an acknowledgment, a prior judgment, or a state-record question.

The right paperwork depends on what has already happened. Keep the child’s birth records, any acknowledgment or prior court order, notices from the Department of Revenue, copies of support payments, and communications about the existing schedule. Also make a factual timeline: the child’s date of birth, the dates the parents lived together or apart, what parenting tasks each person has performed, and whether another court or agency has already made a decision. A court filing is not an occasion to edit history or make records disappear. Accurate information lets an attorney identify what is established, what is alleged, and which court documents need a response.

Once parentage is resolved or addressed in the case, the court still needs terms that serve the child’s best interests. Florida’s parenting-plan framework does not turn on a generic title. It looks to the child’s needs and to each parent’s demonstrated capacity to meet them. That can include routines, school participation, medical care, communication, transportation, and the ability to protect the child from adult litigation. A family law attorney can assess the actual documents and procedural posture. This page cannot determine parentage, authorize a schedule, or tell someone which filing is appropriate for their particular family.

## symptom_4_title
Spousal-support terms are being discussed under Florida’s changed alimony law

## symptom_4
Many people still approach Florida alimony with assumptions that no longer match the statute. For initial dissolution and support cases pending or filed on or after July 1, 2023, Florida’s current law lists temporary, bridge-the-gap, rehabilitative, and durational alimony. Permanent alimony is not one of the listed forms. Before any type is considered, the court must make factual findings about the requesting party’s actual need and the other party’s ability to pay. A request or a draft agreement is therefore not a result; it is the beginning of a financial analysis.

Marriage length matters, but it is not the only issue. The statute treats a marriage under 10 years as short-term, 10 to 20 years as moderate-term, and 20 years or longer as long-term. Durational alimony is designed for a set period. In ordinary circumstances, its duration may not exceed 50 percent of a short-term marriage, 60 percent of a moderate-term marriage, or 75 percent of a long-term marriage, although the statute provides a route for a court to depart based on exceptional circumstances. The amount and duration depend on the statutory requirements and the evidence in the particular case, not on a rule of thumb about who earned more during the marriage.

That is why financial disclosure deserves early attention. Florida’s mandatory-disclosure materials include a financial affidavit and can require tax returns, income proof, account statements, debt information, real-estate documents, retirement-plan records, business records, insurance information, and agreements between the parties. The records should be collected in their original form where possible. A home, a retirement plan, a business interest, stock compensation, a recurring bonus, or a credit balance can affect more than one proposed term. Before accepting a settlement or offering a support figure, obtain advice on the full agreement and the actual financial record.

## qa_1_question
How long does a Florida divorce take before it can be finalized?

## qa_1_answer
Florida law sets a minimum, not a typical completion date. Under section 61.19, a final judgment of dissolution generally cannot be entered until 20 days have passed from the filing of the original petition. The court may enter a final judgment earlier when delay would cause injustice. That 20-day rule does not mean every Tampa case finishes in three weeks. A matter can take longer because service has not occurred, financial information has not been exchanged, a parenting plan or support terms remain unresolved, a standing order requires steps to be completed, mediation is needed, or the court calendar controls the available hearing date. The useful question is which required piece is still open in the actual case. Keep the filing date, service information, and any judicial notice together, and do not treat a statutory minimum as a promise about the final date.

## qa_2_question
What financial information must be disclosed in a Florida family case?

## qa_2_answer
Florida Family Law Rule of Procedure 12.285 requires mandatory disclosure in most original and supplemental family cases, with stated exceptions including simplified dissolutions and certain constructive-service cases where the respondent does not answer. The official compliance form says the documents are served on the other party within 45 days after service of the initial or supplemental petition on the respondent. They can include a financial affidavit, three years of tax returns, six months of income records, financial statements, real-estate documents, bank and brokerage statements, retirement records, debt information, insurance information, and records supporting claims about property or debt. Most of those source documents are exchanged rather than filed in the court file. Because the required list and any local order can matter, do not withhold, move, destroy, or selectively edit financial records. A complete, organized record is safer than attempting to reconstruct details after a dispute develops.

## qa_3_question
What does Hillsborough County require when a family case involves minor children?

## qa_3_answer
The Thirteenth Judicial Circuit’s standing temporary order for Hillsborough County family-law cases with minor children requires both parents to complete an approved parent-education course, whether the case is contested or uncontested. The order says the course must be completed within 60 days after the action is filed. If certificates for both parents are not in the court file, the court may not sign a final judgment. The same local order requires financial affidavits and mandatory disclosure under Florida Rule 12.285, and it states that mediation is required before temporary-relief and final hearings can be scheduled. Those are local procedural rules, not a substitute for advice about the merits of a specific case. Read the standing order that accompanies the petition and any later division-specific instructions; a Tampa case may have requirements that do not appear on a statewide checklist.

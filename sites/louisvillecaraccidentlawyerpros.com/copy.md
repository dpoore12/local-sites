## symptom_1_title

A rear-end crash left you hurt even though the damage looks small

## symptom_1

A rear-end impact can be straightforward on fault and still complicated on injury. The photographs may show only a compressed bumper while the person inside felt a hard snap forward and back. Do not let the appearance of the car become the whole story. The useful record is the sequence: where traffic was slowing, whether the other driver was following too closely, where each vehicle stopped, what you felt at the scene, and what changed over the next few days.

Start with the report number and take clear photos before repairs erase the condition of either vehicle. Keep the repair estimate, tow receipt, and any photographs of the cabin, child seats, or items thrown forward. If a nearby store, house, or intersection may have video, preservation matters because many systems overwrite footage quickly. Witness names and contact details belong in the same file, not in a loose text thread that disappears later.

Get evaluated when pain, dizziness, numbness, headache, or restricted motion makes that sensible. Then keep the treatment record complete. Missed appointments, gaps, and casual statements to an insurer can be used to argue that the symptoms were unrelated or resolved. Kentucky’s no-fault system adds another issue: ordinary drivers are generally subject to tort limits unless they made a valid written rejection before the collision. Medical-expense benefits over the statutory threshold, or certain serious injuries, can permit a pain-and-suffering claim even when the limits apply. A proper review matches the medical record, crash evidence, and policy election instead of assuming that a rear-end collision automatically settles itself.

## symptom_2_title

The other driver left, and the report does not identify them

## symptom_2

A hit-and-run collision creates two parallel problems. One is finding the responsible vehicle. The other is determining which coverage may respond if it is never found. Move quickly on both. Save the police report number, photographs of the vehicle and scene, treatment records, and every detail you can remember before repeated retelling changes it: direction of travel, lane, vehicle color, body damage, partial plate, stickers, cargo, and where the driver exited.

Look for witnesses early. A person who stopped may have a dash camera, and nearby businesses, homes, or transit locations may have exterior video. Ask that potentially relevant footage be preserved; a camera that recorded the crash may not still have it a week later. Do not post a request that names or accuses a particular person without proof. The evidence needs to be gathered carefully enough to be useful, not merely plausible.

Your own policy deserves an immediate review. Uninsured-motorist coverage can be relevant when the at-fault driver cannot be found, but the policy language and notice requirements matter. Kentucky basic reparation benefits, often called PIP, are a separate question from a claim for pain and suffering or other damages. A driver who did not reject Kentucky’s tort limitations in writing before the wreck may still have a claim when the medical-expense threshold or a listed serious-injury condition is met. A rejection filed before the crash changes that analysis and preserves full tort rights, but it can also affect PIP eligibility. Keep the original policy documents and correspondence rather than relying on a memory of what was chosen when the policy began.

## symptom_3_title

The at-fault driver has no insurance or too little coverage

## symptom_3

When the person who caused the wreck has no liability coverage, or a policy limit that cannot cover the loss, the claim does not simply disappear. It becomes a coverage-and-evidence problem. Collect the crash report, the other driver’s insurance information if any, your declarations page, medical bills and records, wage-loss documentation, repair records, and all insurer letters. The order matters: a settlement, release, or missed notice requirement can affect other coverage that might have applied.

Start by separating three questions. First, who caused the collision? Second, what losses are documented? Third, what coverage may respond? Kentucky basic reparation benefits can pay qualifying economic loss up to the statutory limit, while uninsured- or underinsured-motorist coverage is governed by the particular policy. Those are not interchangeable buckets. A claim against an uninsured driver may involve your own carrier, but that does not make the other driver’s conduct irrelevant. The same evidence that shows fault also supports the coverage claim.

Do not assume “no insurance” means the driver truly had none. The report may list incomplete or expired information, a different owner may be involved, or another policy may apply. Conversely, do not sign an early release because an adjuster says there is only one possible source of payment. A Louisville review should read the actual policy language, identify notice dates, and check whether a Kentucky no-fault rejection was on file before the collision. Without a pre-crash written rejection, Kentucky’s tort limits may affect a claim for noneconomic loss unless the medical threshold or an enumerated serious injury is satisfied. That is why the declarations page belongs with the medical and crash records from day one.

## symptom_4_title

The insurer made an offer before your treatment or losses are clear

## symptom_4

An early offer can be tempting when the car is damaged and bills are arriving. It is also usually the moment to slow down and read every word. The key question is not whether the amount helps today. It is what rights the release ends tomorrow. A broad release can close a bodily-injury claim before the medical picture, missed work, future treatment, and available coverage have been sorted out.

Build a single file before responding. It should include the crash report, scene and vehicle photographs, all treatment notes and bills, proof of missed income, medication and travel costs, repair or total-loss documents, and every email or letter from each carrier. Ask which coverage the offer represents. Property damage, basic reparation benefits, bodily injury, and uninsured- or underinsured-motorist benefits are different subjects. A payment under one is not necessarily a complete answer to another.

Kentucky drivers also need to know their no-fault election. By default, the Motor Vehicle Reparations Act limits certain tort claims. A written or electronic rejection filed with the Department of Insurance before the crash retains full tort rights. If no rejection was filed, a claim for pain, suffering, mental anguish, or inconvenience can still be available when medical expenses exceed the statutory threshold or the injury falls within a listed category such as a fracture, permanent injury, or death. That determination depends on records and law, not an adjuster’s summary. Do not make a statement about your health that goes beyond what you know, do not estimate future care, and do not sign away a claim without understanding what the document covers. A careful review puts the offer beside the evidence and the policy election before any final decision is made.

## qa_1_question

Does Kentucky no-fault coverage stop me from suing after a Louisville crash?

## qa_1_answer

Not always. Kentucky drivers ordinarily accept the Motor Vehicle Reparations Act’s tort limitations unless they reject them in writing or electronically and file that rejection with the Department of Insurance before the crash. If you accepted the limits, basic reparation benefits can cover economic loss up to the statutory limit, and an injury claim for pain and suffering may still be possible when medical expenses exceed the statutory threshold or when the injury fits a listed serious category, including a fracture, permanent injury, or death. If you rejected the limits before the collision, Kentucky law says you retain full tort rights, but that election can affect basic-reparation-benefit eligibility. The declarations page and any rejection form are worth locating before anyone makes a sweeping statement about whether you can sue.

## pricing_lede

Kentucky writes an exact fee schedule for work injuries and nothing at all for car wrecks, and this page separates the two: what SCR 3.130(1.5) demands of a written agreement, what the Jefferson Circuit Court clerk collects, and the two dollar figures in the no-fault statute that shape a Louisville claim before any fee question arises.

## pricing_body

### One state, two very different rules

Start with the rule the Supreme Court of Kentucky adopted. SCR 3.130(1.5)(a) forbids
agreeing to, charging, or collecting a fee that is unreasonable, and applies that same word
to the amount charged for expenses. Eight factors set the measure: labor and hours against
the novelty and difficulty of the questions; work turned away to take the case; the going
local charge for similar service; how the stakes compare with the outcome; deadlines in
play; the history between lawyer and client; reputation, skill and standing; and whether
payment is fixed or rides on the result. No percentage appears. For a crash on
the Watterson Expressway or a rear-end collision in the Highlands, reasonableness is the
entire ceiling.

Now the contrast. KRS 342.320 governs a workers' compensation claim with arithmetic rather
than judgment: twenty percent of the first $25,000 of the award, fifteen percent of the next
$25,000, ten percent of the remainder, and a maximum fee of $18,000 for contracts signed on
or after July 14, 2018. No fee is paid until an administrative law judge approves it, the
approval motion is due within thirty days after the claim becomes final, and the fee comes
out of the employee's award or settlement proceeds. Kentucky knew how to write a cap. It
chose not to write one for motor vehicle claims.

### What the written agreement has to carry

Under SCR 3.130(1.5)(c) a contingent fee agreement must be in a writing signed by the
client, and must state how the amount is determined, the percentages accruing on
settlement, at trial and on appeal, what litigation outlays get charged against the sum
collected, and whether that subtraction happens ahead of the fee calculation or after. Two
requirements in the Kentucky version deserve special attention. The agreement must clearly
notify the client of any expenses the client will owe whether or not the client prevails.
And at the conclusion of the matter, the lawyer must provide a written statement of the
outcome showing the remittance and the method by which it was determined.

Paragraph (d) closes two doors: no fee tied to the result for a criminal defendant, and no
fee in a domestic relations matter contingent on securing a divorce or on the amount of
alimony, maintenance, support or property settlement, apart from liquidated sums already in
arrears.

### The two figures in the no-fault statute

Kentucky is a choice no-fault state, and two numbers drive the early stage of a Louisville
claim. Basic reparation benefits are capped at $10,000 for all economic loss to one injured
person from one accident under KRS 304.39-020, regardless of how many carriers might owe
them. And KRS 304.39-060 allows recovery for pain, suffering, mental anguish and
inconvenience only where medical expense benefits exceed $1,000, or where the injury
involves permanent disfigurement, a bone fracture, loss of a body member, permanent injury
within reasonable medical probability, permanent loss of bodily function, or death.

Those figures explain a pattern people misread as delay. Treatment gets paid from the
reparation benefit, the $10,000 runs out faster than anyone expects on an emergency room
visit plus imaging plus therapy, and the liability claim then has to carry both the
remaining bills and the human loss.

### Court costs, and what else leaves the recovery

Filing suit in Jefferson Circuit Court costs $188 under CR 3.02(1) as amended for 2026,
plus a $20 court technology fee and other required charges, including the court facility
fee and library fee, collected by the circuit clerk. Those are costs of the case, not a
fee. Depositions, medical records, records custodians, a mediator, an accident
reconstruction opinion in a disputed intersection case, and trial exhibits are all case
expenses too, and the written agreement decides who advances them and when.

### Questions worth asking out loud

What percentage applies if the claim resolves before suit, and what changes it after suit
is filed. Whether expenses come off the top or after the fee is figured. Whether the
expense obligation survives a defense verdict, since the rule requires that warning
anyway. Whether a compensation claim is running in parallel, because the $18,000 statutory
ceiling governs that half and nothing governs the other half. And whether the closing
written statement will arrive with the settlement check, because the rule already entitles
the client to it.

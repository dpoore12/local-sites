## symptom_1_title

A sharp bang, then a door that feels impossibly heavy

## symptom_1

That report usually points to the counterbalance system, not a dead opener. A torsion spring can break with a crack that carries through the house. Once it does, the opener is trying to lift the actual weight of the door without the spring taking most of the load. It may hum, strain, or move the door only a few inches before stopping. On a torsion setup, a safe visual check from the floor may show a clean gap in one of the coils above the opening. On an extension-spring setup, one long spring along the track may be hanging loose.

Do not use the opener repeatedly to test the theory. It can damage the motor and rail, and a door that is no longer balanced can come down fast. Leave the door closed if it is already down. If it is open or partly open, stay out from beneath it, keep vehicles clear, and do not pull the emergency release. A door that appears stuck may be held by a remaining cable or spring in a way that is not obvious from the driveway.

The repair is not just fitting a new coil. The technician measures the existing spring specification against the door’s weight and height, checks cables, drums, bearing plates, and the shaft, then balances the door by hand after the work. On a two-spring system, both springs have worked the same number of cycles, so the second is inspected and the options are explained before either is replaced. The final check is practical: with the opener disconnected, the door should stay near the halfway point rather than diving to the floor or shooting upward. That balance protects the new spring and keeps the opener from doing a job it was never built to do.

## symptom_2_title

The door reaches the floor, then immediately opens again

## symptom_2

A door that reverses on the way down is usually responding to the safety system. Two photo-eye sensors sit low at either side of the opening. They must face each other continuously before the opener will close the door. A bicycle tire, leaf pile, stored box, or a sensor bracket bumped by a trash bin can interrupt that invisible line. Dust on a lens or a loose wire can produce the same result. First, look for a physical obstruction and wipe the lenses gently. Most sensor pairs have indicator lights; an unlit or flickering one is a useful detail for the repair visit.

Bright late-afternoon light can also confuse a sensor aimed toward an open garage. That does not mean the safety feature should be bypassed. Holding the wall button or changing force settings may close the door once, but it defeats the protection meant to stop the door when something is in its path. If the photo eyes are clean and square yet the door still reverses, the resistance may be coming from the door itself. Dry rollers, a tight hinge, a bent section of track, or a weak spring can make the opener think it has struck an obstruction.

A technician checks sensor alignment and wiring, but also runs the door by hand to find where it begins to bind. That distinction matters. Replacing a photo eye will not fix a door that is dragging through a misaligned track, and turning up the close force can hide the problem until a roller breaks or a cable slips. The right repair ends with the reversal system tested, the travel limit reset if needed, and the door moving smoothly enough that the opener is not forcing it shut. For a garage facing open space, intact bottom and side weather seals deserve a look too: CAL FIRE notes that gaps around garage doors can admit wind-blown embers.

## symptom_3_title

One side is low, a roller has left the track, or the panel is bent

## symptom_3

A crooked garage door is not a cosmetic problem. When one lower corner is down, a cable may have come off its drum or a roller may have climbed out of the track. The remaining hardware can be carrying the door unevenly. A dented panel can be only surface damage, but a strike from a vehicle, a branch, or a hard close can also bend the track, bow a reinforcement strut, or twist a hinge. Turning the opener on again can pull the damage farther through the opening and turn a repairable derailment into damaged sections.

Stop using the door. Do not try to pry a roller back into the rail, pull a cable tight, or remove a bottom bracket. Bottom brackets connect to the lift cable and can be under significant tension. If the door is partly raised, keep the garage clear and do not park beneath it. If it is closed but visibly racked, leave it closed until it is inspected. A technician secures the door before touching the tracks, then determines whether the cause began with a failed cable, seized roller, broken hinge, shifted track, or an impact.

The repair often includes more than placing a wheel back in its channel. The technician checks track spacing and mounting, replaces cracked rollers or hinges, resets the cable on the drum with matched tension, and looks at the top and bottom sections for damage that will keep returning the door to the same bad path. A hand-balance test follows before the opener is reconnected. ## symptom_4_title

The opener motor is running, but the door is not moving

## symptom_4

Motor noise without door movement is often an opener connection problem, but the sound matters. If the chain or belt is moving along the rail while the door stays put, the trolley may have disengaged from the carriage. That can happen after the red emergency-release cord was pulled or snagged. With the door fully closed and the area clear, some systems can be re-engaged according to the opener manufacturer’s instructions. If the door is open, crooked, or unusually heavy, do not attempt that step; the release can leave a compromised door unsupported.

A grinding or rattling sound inside the opener head points somewhere else. On many chain-drive units, a worn internal drive gear lets the motor turn without transferring motion to the rail. A brief hum followed by silence can point to a capacitor, motor, or control problem. A unit that works intermittently may have a failing wall control, remote receiver, logic board, or power issue. None of those faults should be assumed from sound alone, especially when a dragging door can make a healthy opener appear defective.

The service visit starts by disconnecting the opener and checking whether the door itself can move and balance correctly. If it cannot, the spring, rollers, cables, or track have to be addressed before the motor is blamed. If the door moves freely by hand, the technician checks the trolley, gear system, limits, safety sensors, wiring, and control components. The useful result is a specific repair decision: reconnect a release, replace an individual part, adjust travel, repair the door’s mechanical drag, or replace an opener only when its condition makes repair unsound. A replacement opener is not a substitute for a door with bad balance. After any opener work, the safety reversal and photo eyes are tested before the door is returned to regular use.

### The bang arrives before the symptom

People describe this one accurately, almost without exception. There is a single hard report inside the garage, loud enough to be mistaken for something structural, and afterward the door will not lift. Press the wall control and the motor labors against a slab of steel that no longer has anything offsetting it. Walk in, stay clear of the opening, and look up at the shaft above the header. A torsion coil that has failed shows a clean interruption, the two halves relaxed and slightly unwound where they were tight the day before. Older setups instead use long springs stretched back beside the horizontal rails, and a failed one of those droops visibly, sometimes held only by its safety cable.

### Which counterbalance a garage here is likely to carry

Terrain decides more of this than fashion does. Town planning material puts close to half of the community on hillsides, including the Las Trampas Ridge area and the slopes flanking Sycamore Valley, and a hillside lot tends to produce a hillside garage. Split-level framing, a tuck-under bay, and a header set tight against a floor above all reduce the straight run available for a torsion shaft, which is where low-clearance hardware and repositioned bearing plates come in. Flat-pad streets such as the Greenbrook blocks off Sycamore Valley Road are usually a straightforward torsion assembly. Out along Camino Tassajara, the Tassajara Ranch and Shadow Creek areas were already under construction when the Town Council recorded them as neighboring projects in February 1990, so those garages are wide, purpose-built double openings. A technician who assumes one configuration and arrives without hardware for the other burns a second appointment.

### Winding bars, or nothing

A broken torsion spring is not a spent spring. The surviving portion still holds stored energy, and the cones at each end are the point where that energy is released in a controlled way or not at all. Winding bars sized to the cone are the only correct tool. Screwdrivers, bolts and lengths of rebar slip under load and leave the cone as projectiles. If a spring has gone, the useful sequence is short: pull the opener plug so nobody presses the button out of habit, leave the door resting on the slab, and keep vehicles and people out of the opening until it is inspected. California treats this as licensed work, and the Contractors State License Board classification that covers overhead door assemblies and their activating devices is D-28.

### One spring on a two-spring shaft is a return visit

When a pair shares a shaft, both have absorbed the same number of openings under the same conditions. Fitting one and leaving the other loads the shaft unevenly and puts the second failure on a short clock. The steel is the cheap part of that decision. Three questions sort a careful technician from a careless one: what does this door weigh, what are the replacements rated for, and what becomes of the spring that did not break.

### What quietly shortens the life of the part

DASMA's technical sheet on spring cycle life is unusually blunt about how much of this happens before installation. A spring dropped on concrete can crack a cast fitting. A minor nick or notch becomes the weak point the coil eventually fails at. Rust reduces the effective area of the wire and leaves corrosion pits from which fatigue cracks accelerate. A spring torch-cut to length can pick up brittle spots from molten splatter if the adjacent coils are not shielded. The same sheet warns that a spring's cycle rating and a door's cycle rating are not the same figure. One local reality sits on top of all that. The Town's adopted design criteria place this community in Title 24 climate zone 12. A long dry season pulls grease off bearings and packs fine grit into what remains. An unlubricated shaft then makes the spring work harder than it was sized for.

### How the visit runs

Wire diameter, inside diameter, overall length and wind direction come off the failed spring, or off the stamped cone if the numbers survived. Those are matched against the actual door, not against a guess. Both springs come down, drums are reset, new springs go up and are wound to the turn count that door height calls for. With the tension out of the system, both cables get read end to end, since a collapsing door commonly abrades one on its way to the slab. The last step is a hand test with the opener disconnected. Released near the halfway point, the door should hold there rather than sinking or climbing. Watch that test happen.

### What moves the total

This is a parts-and-labor job with a comparatively narrow spread. Spring count, wire gauge, and whether cables, bearings or drums also need replacing account for most of the movement. Access does the rest: a tuck-under bay at the top of a graded driveway is not the same afternoon as a level double opening with room to stage tools. Whatever the figure is, it belongs in your hand before the first bar goes into a cone.

### A foot from the floor, then back up

That reversal is the safety circuit doing its job, correctly or otherwise. Two photo eyes sit low on either jamb and have to see each other for travel to continue downward. A bin, a bag of soil, a bicycle wheel, a bracket nudged out of square, or a film of dust on a lens all break that line. Check the indicator on each eye before anything else, since a dark or flickering light says that eye has lost its partner. Low sun into a west-facing opening can do it as well. What should never happen is a force setting cranked up to overpower the fault, because that removes the protection and hides a mechanical problem in the door.

### What the federal rule actually requires

Residential operators are built to 16 CFR part 1211, and the detail is worth knowing. For a downward moving door, the operator has to begin reversal within two seconds of meeting an obstruction, then take the door back to the full upmost position. That obligation is relieved only for the first foot of travel from fully open. The compliance test is deliberately unglamorous: a one-inch object placed on the floor beneath the door edge, in line with the driving point, with the operator exerting at least a twenty-five pound pull. The rule also allows a design where a control has to be held down continuously for the door to close. If your unit behaves that way, it is not broken.

### The disconnect handle and the six-foot rule

The same regulation says the manual detachment means must be capable of adjustment to six feet above the garage floor when the operator is installed per its instructions. That number exists so an adult can reach the release without a ladder in the dark. It is worth finding yours now rather than during an event. Pull it only with the door fully closed. Pulling a release while a door is mid-travel removes the operator from a load the springs may not be balancing, and the door can drop.

### Losing power in this county is a planned event

PG&E runs public safety power shutoffs during dangerous fire weather, which means an electric opener in Contra Costa County has a foreseeable number of days each autumn when it does nothing at all. State law caught up with that. California requires a residential automatic garage door opener sold or installed here on or after July 1, 2019 to include a battery backup function. That function has to operate during an electrical outage and keep the opener running without interruption while the power is off. The follow-on clause surprises people: a replacement residential door may not be connected to an existing opener that fails that requirement, whatever year the opener was built.

### Motor turns, door stays put

Watch the rail while somebody presses the button. Chain or belt traveling with a motionless door means the carriage has parted from the trolley, usually because the release cord was pulled or snagged and never relatched. On many units that is a two-minute fix with no parts. A hum with a dead chain is different, and so is a grinding rattle inside the head, which on aging chain drives typically means the nylon drive gear has rounded off its teeth. That is a component, not grounds for a new unit. After any manual operation, the first powered cycle should be watched to confirm the reversal still works.

### Dust, heat and offshore wind

Openers here fail in a pattern set by the local season rather than by cold. Months without meaningful rain leave a fine mineral dust on everything, and offshore wind events drive it under the bottom seal and onto sensor lenses and lens housings. Weather Service records from the October 2019 offshore event show a peak gust of sixty-three miles per hour at Las Trampas, which is the ridge on this town's western edge. Grit at the threshold also props the bottom seal open, which matters twice: the eyes stop reading cleanly, and the gap becomes the ember path CAL FIRE specifically flags on garage doors.

### Repair, replace, and where a permit enters

A head with no photo eyes at all belongs in a dumpster rather than on a bench, and so does one with a cracked housing or a control board nobody stocks. Most other faults are component work. Where a replacement adds or alters a circuit, the Town charges an electrical sub-permit as a percentage of the building permit fee, so anyone quoting new wiring should be able to say who files it. The answer you want at the end of a visit is a named fault, a part, and an honest read on whether spending it makes sense for a unit of that age.

### Do not run it a second time

Whatever hardware is still engaged is the only thing holding a derailed door where it is, and none of that geometry is stable once a section has left the rail. Pressing the button to gauge the damage is how a partial derailment finishes as a door lying across a hood. Pull the plug, clear the opening, move cars back, and put the pry bar down. This failure is genuinely urgent and gets pulled forward in the day.

### Three things put a section out of the channel

**A cable came off its drum.** Each lift cable ties into the bracket at a lower corner and spools into grooves on a drum carried at the end of the torsion shaft. When it jumps those grooves, commonly after a spring failure drops the door hard, that half of the door loses its lift and racks in the opening. Loose cable coiled near the jamb with one low corner is the tell.

**A roller failed.** Rollers are the wheels at the edge of each section. Nylon turns brittle and cracks; steel seizes once the bearing runs dry and dust packs in. A seized roller skids rather than rolls and eventually climbs out, nearly always at the radius where the vertical rail curves toward horizontal.

**The rail moved.** Reversing into it is the classic cause. Wind and a shifted jamb do it more quietly, and a rail pushed out of plumb destroys rollers on every cycle afterward.

### The wind that does this here has a name

Offshore flow off the Coast Ranges is the local hazard, and it arrives dry and hard rather than as a thunderstorm. During the October 2019 offshore event the Weather Service logged a peak gust of sixty-three miles per hour at Las Trampas and seventy miles per hour at the Los Vaqueros station, both in this county, on the afternoon of the twenty-seventh. Wind at that strength across a wide double opening flexes sections, works fasteners loose at the jamb, and drives oak litter into the track. DASMA publishes a checklist for occupants to use after a high wind event, and its instructions are conservative on purpose: keep the door closed, inspect visually first, and stop the moment anything looks deformed rather than testing it under power.

### What collects at a threshold on a sloped lot

Nearly half of this town sits on hillsides, and hillside drainage ends somewhere. On a driveway that pitches toward the door, that somewhere is the threshold. Grit, oak leaves and small gravel build a ridge the bottom seal cannot close over, which wears the seal, holds moisture against the bottom section, and blocks the photo eyes at exactly the height they are mounted. None of that puts a door off its track by itself. It does shorten the life of every part at the bottom of the door, and it leaves the perimeter gap that CAL FIRE identifies as an ember route into an attached garage.

### The one fitting to leave alone

The bottom bracket at each lower corner is fastened to a lift cable. On a door with any tension left in the system, unbolting it releases that cable under load. People are injured doing this every year, usually while trying to free a jammed roller. Cable, bracket and drum are a single system and are worked on with the door secured and the tension controlled, which is why the first thing a technician does is clamp the door rather than touch the track.

### What the reset involves

The door is clamped below the lowest roller so it cannot travel. Each section is guided back into the channel individually. Rollers are reset or swapped, both drums are respooled so that tension matches side to side, and rail that has bent is trued where that is sound and replaced where it is not. Hinges get checked in the same pass, because a section that came out of the channel usually took a hinge with it. Track spacing and the fasteners into the jamb are verified before anything is powered up. Then the hand-balance test: off the opener, the door should hold near the middle of its travel on its own. Drift in either direction means the counterbalance is off, and fresh rollers will not survive that for long.

### What moves the total

Rollers and cables sit at the cheap end. Rail sections are moderate. The variables that actually decide the figure are how far the door came out, whether a section bent on the way, and how much room there is to work. A garage reached at the top of a graded drive with nowhere to park a truck costs more in time than the same fault on a level pad. If a section is damaged beyond sensible repair, you should hear that plainly instead of paying twice over.

Several conditions add real time to identical parts. Tuck-under garages with short headroom. Driveways that pitch down to the door. Split-level framing with no straight run for a torsion shaft. Openings cut into a slope so one jamb sits against retained earth. A double-door spring change on a flat pad in Greenbrook and the same change on a tuck-under garage with a low-clearance track kit are not the same afternoon.

Fire hardening is the second variable, and it is specific to this valley. The Town identifies its open-space edges, grassland and oak woodland among them, as Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zone areas, and CAL FIRE points to gaps around garage doors and missing gasketing as a route for ember intrusion. A door being reset or replaced on a lot near that edge is worth doing with a bottom seal that actually contacts the slab across its full width, side and top weatherstripping without daylight, and a solid connection where the track meets the jamb. That work is inexpensive when a door is already apart and awkward to retrofit later, so it belongs in the scope from the start.

Third, appearance can become a review item. Danville's Design Review Board evaluates site design and architecture against Town standards, and its submittal requirements call for exterior doors, materials and colors to be identified on plans. Replacing hardware behind a door raises no such question. Choosing a new street-facing door in a subdivision with an architectural standard can, and finding that out after ordering a custom panel is an expensive sequence.

### What a defensible quote contains

Spring work should be specified, not described. Wire size, inside diameter, overall length, wind direction, and the drum series the door is set up for. A technician who has weighed or calculated the door can produce those numbers; one who has not will talk about heavy doors instead. Cables should be listed by diameter and length, rollers by stem length and bearing type, and hinges counted by position.

Labor should appear separately from parts, with the trip or diagnostic charge broken out and a clear statement of whether it applies against the repair. On a replacement, expect the manufacturer and model, steel gauge, insulation value, whether the existing opener and rail are reused, track configuration for the available headroom, new weatherseal, and removal of the old door. If a permit applies because framing or a circuit is involved, the fee and the inspection should be listed rather than folded silently into a lump sum.

### The trap

The expensive mistake in this trade is treating a balance problem as an electronics problem. Openers get replaced constantly for symptoms that springs caused. When a spring has lost tension, the motor lifts weight it was never sized for, draws harder, overheats, wears its gear, and eventually gives up. Swap the opener and leave the springs, and a new unit inherits the same overload. Reverse the order and the new opener rarely turns out to be necessary at all.

The second trap is quieter: a single spring left on a two-car door because it is cheaper today. It shortens the life of that spring, loads the shaft unevenly, and returns as a service call sooner than anyone plans. Ask what the door weighs, ask what the spring system is rated for in cycles, and ask what happens to the other spring if only one is replaced.

### What is missing from the number people find online

National cost pages price a door and a labor allowance. They do not include the Town's minimum permit and inspection charge, the electrical sub-permit on a new opener circuit, or plan review where framing changes. Also missing: the low-headroom conversion a tuck-under garage needs, ember-resistant sealing near an open-space edge, design review on a street-facing panel, and hauling old sections off a steep driveway. On repairs they leave out the second failed part that emerges once the first one is off. They also ignore the difference between reaching a door on a flat driveway and reaching one at the top of a grade with no room to park a truck.

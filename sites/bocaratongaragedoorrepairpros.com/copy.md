<h2>How you know the counterbalance is what failed</h2>
<p>The story barely changes from one driveway to the next. Something in the garage bangs like a dropped sheet of plywood, and after that the door will not rise. Press the wall control and the motor strains against a slab of steel that suddenly weighs what it has always weighed. Walk in and look at the shaft above the header. A failed torsion spring shows a clean gap of an inch or two in the coil, both halves sitting relaxed and slightly unwound. Older side-mounted systems fail differently. There the spring droops slack along the horizontal rail, and its safety cable is all that keeps it from whipping loose.</p>
<h2>What shoreline air does to spring steel</h2>
<p>Salt adds a second failure path that dry inland markets rarely deal with. DASMA's technical data sheet on spring cycle life is direct about it: springs should be kept dry to prevent surface rust, because rust reduces the effective area of the spring wire and lowers its overall strength. The sheet goes further and warns that rust creates corrosion pits, and that fatigue cracks accelerate out of those pits and cut cycle life. That is the mechanism behind a coil that lets go years ahead of schedule within sight of the Atlantic. Brown staining on coils, drums and bearing plates is a measurement, not a cosmetic complaint.</p>
<h2>Cycle life, and the arithmetic for your own house</h2>
<p>ANSI/DASMA 102 sets the floor at a minimum of 10,000 cycles for a door system that is properly selected, installed, operated and maintained. The same standard defines one cycle as travel from closed to fully open and back to closed again. Do that division against your own routine. A garage that serves as the household's real front entrance can run six or eight cycles a day, which consumes 10,000 of them in roughly four years. A door opened twice a day stretches the same rating across more than a decade. When the door is the entrance everyone uses, a higher cycle rating is worth ordering.</p>
<h2>Winding bars, and why no household tool substitutes</h2>
<p>Even a broken torsion spring is dangerous, because the surviving half is still partly loaded and the shaft can turn. Winding bars sized to the cone are the only correct tool. Screwdrivers, rebar and long bolts slip out under load and leave the garage at speed. If a coil has parted, pull the operator plug so nobody presses the button from habit, leave the panels resting on the slab, and move cars and people well away from the opening. The stored energy in the second spring is the part homeowners consistently underestimate.</p>
<h2>Why a wind-rated door changes the spring math</h2>
<p>Weight is where the building code reaches into the counterbalance. A door approved for an opening designed at the 170 mph velocity the city applies to single-family homes carries heavier gauge steel, additional horizontal struts and reinforced end stiles. Every pound of that has to be balanced by the spring. DASMA 102 also constrains the geometry, ruling out torsion springs on a sectional door with an index ratio under six to one, meaning a mean coil diameter of at least six times the wire diameter. Reusing springs from the lighter door that used to hang in the same opening is the error that follows a replacement around for years.</p>
<h2>What happens during the visit</h2>

<h2>Telling a safety reversal apart from a dead operator</h2>
<p>Start by watching, from the floor and well clear of the opening, while another adult runs the control. Four patterns cover almost everything. The chain or belt moves and the door does not, which means the trolley has let go of the carriage. The head hums with nothing turning, which points inside the housing. The door starts down and climbs back up, which is the safety circuit firing. Or the door moves in fits and grinds, which usually means resistance somewhere along the rail. Each of those leads somewhere different, and only one of them ends in a new operator.</p>
<h2>What the federal rule actually requires</h2>
<p>Every residential operator manufactured on or after January 1, 1993 for sale in the United States falls under the Consumer Product Safety Commission standard in 16 CFR part 1211. The rule requires inherent primary entrapment protection, plus one of three secondary arrangements: constant-pressure control, a connection for an external device such as photo eyes, or an inherent secondary device. The performance test is specific. Past the first foot of travel down from the upmost position, the operator has to begin reversing within two seconds of contact with an obstruction, then carry the door back to the full upmost position. The floor test object is one inch high, with the operator exerting a 25 pound pull or its rated pull, whichever is greater.</p>
<h2>Inside the housing</h2>
<p>A coarse grinding noise with the motor running on a chain-drive unit generally means the nylon drive gear has been chewed. That is a part swap, not a replacement. A short hum that stops can be a capacitor, a motor winding, a logic board, or a door that has simply grown too heavy for the unit. Humidity matters at this stage. Wiring, terminals and the receptacle deserve a look in a garage that stays damp, though moisture is a suspect rather than a diagnosis. The relays inside an entrapment protection circuit are built to survive 100,000 cycles of operation, so they are rarely the first thing to quit.</p>
<h2>The trap that produces a second visit</h2>
<p>A door that reverses before it closes is the most frequently over-repaired complaint in Palm Beach County garages. New unit, same behavior, because the fault was a clouded lens, a loose bracket, drifted travel limits or a binding roller. There is a second version of the trap. A new operator hung under a heavier wind-rated replacement door still needs travel and force set for that door, and if the counterbalance was never rematched to the new weight, the motor takes the abuse. Ask what was measured, not just what was installed.</p>
<h2>What happens during the visit, and what it costs</h2>
<p>The technician disconnects the operator when it is safe to do so, moves the door by hand to feel for drag, and checks balance before touching the head. Costs split into three bands. Sensor and bracket work sits lowest. An internal part such as a gear kit or a board sits in the middle. Look for the fastener schedule into masonry, the number of horizontal struts, the track gauge, and whether new jamb brackets are included or the old ones are being reused. On a repair, the quote should name the reading that condemned the part: the measured gap in a broken spring, the strand condition of a cable, the amp draw or force setting on an opener that reverses. It should separate the trip and diagnostic charge from repair labor and state whether one applies to the other. It should list whether the old door and track leave with the crew.

### The trap in this market

Two of them, and they both cost real money. The first is a storm-damage repair sold as a retrofit, or the reverse. Bracing a door that was never approved to be braced does not make it a rated door, and an owner who believes otherwise finds out during a claim rather than during an inspection. Ask directly whether the finished assembly is approved as installed, and ask to see the number.

The second is opener replacement offered as the answer to a door that reverses before it closes. On a coastal door the usual causes are a misaligned photo eye bracket that corrosion has loosened, a bottom seal packed with sand and salt crust, or binding rollers adding resistance the opener reads as an obstruction. Those are inexpensive faults. A new opener installed over them still reverses, and the second visit is the one that finds the actual problem.

### What the online average leaves out

A national cost page prices a door, not a coastal opening. It leaves out the permit, the recorded notice on larger jobs, and the reinspection fee when a fastening pattern fails its first look. It also leaves out the association review, rated track and struts, coated hardware, and removal of the old assembly. On repairs it leaves out the corroded second part that comes off with the first, which here is closer to the rule than the exception.

## svc_carriage_house_garage_doors_body

<h2>Overlay, integrated design or a genuine swing-out</h2>
<p>An overlay carriage door is a standard insulated sectional door with cladding and hardware applied to the face to imitate two hinged leaves. An integrated design builds the look into the stamped panel instead, which weighs less and costs less but reads as an imitation at close range. <h2>Approval comes before the order in Old Floresta</h2>
<p>Two districts change the sequence. <p>Decoration is load. <p>Windows are usually the point of a carriage door, and the requirement is conditional. Typical garage door layouts fail at least one of those, most often the pane size, so ordinary garage doors generally fall outside the requirement. That is a separate question from impact resistance, which is a wind and debris matter rather than a glazing-hazard matter.</p>
<h2>The rated door underneath the appearance</h2>
<p>Style does not exempt the opening. ## svc_commercial_garage_door_repair_lede

Commercial openings fail on a different schedule and against different rules than residential ones. Rolling steel curtains, high-cycle sectional doors and fire-rated assemblies each carry inspection obligations that a house door does not.

## svc_commercial_garage_door_repair_body

<h2>Rolling steel is not a large residential door</h2>
<p>A rolling steel door coils a curtain of interlocking slats onto a barrel above the opening, counterbalanced by springs inside that barrel, guided by wall angles rather than tracks. Nothing about servicing it resembles a sectional door. The tension is adjusted at a charge wheel that must remain stationary while the door runs, and DASMA's maintenance guidance for building supervisors says plainly not to adjust it and to call for service instead. Guides must be fastened in every hole or slot, the guide gap uniform top to bottom, and the hood secured so it cannot fall. The <a href="../services/">service list</a> covers the sectional equivalents.</p>
<h2>The force numbers that reveal a counterbalance problem</h2>
<p>Commercial doors give a measurable warning before they fail. Manual operating force should not exceed 35 pounds on a hand chain or 25 pounds on a crank, and a significant deviation from either indicates a counterbalance problem for a trained technician. On a powered door, the check is to close it with the operator, engage manual operation, and try to raise the door one to two feet by hand. Electric door performance deserves a quarterly look, and periodic inspection and maintenance at least annually. A high wind, seismic or flooding event calls for an immediate evaluation of every door in the building regardless of when the last inspection happened.</p>
<h2>Fire doors and the annual drop test</h2>
<p>A rolling steel fire door is life-safety equipment with a separate obligation. Its automatic closing has to be tested at installation and then inspected and tested not less than annually as required by NFPA 80, and that applies even to a door normally kept closed. The drop test is performed twice: once to confirm the door closes fully under its own weight, and once to confirm the release resets correctly. Average closing speed must be not more than 24 inches per second and not less than 6 inches per second. A door that fails either half of that test is a documented deficiency, not a maintenance note.</p>
<h2>Boca Raton's flex and industrial stock, and downtime arithmetic</h2>
<p>The city's commercial inventory is not a strip of warehouses. The former IBM campus, now a 123-acre business campus where the first personal computer was developed in 1981, sits alongside the Park of Commerce corridor and a range of light industrial and service buildings. What those buildings share is a dock or drive-in opening on the critical path: a door stuck down stops receiving, and a door stuck open leaves conditioned space and inventory exposed. That is why a temporary secure closure and a return visit with the correct part usually beats an improvised repair on the first trip.</p>
<h2>Corrosion, coatings and coastal exposure on a curtain</h2>
<p>Finish wear on a rolling door is mechanical before it is chemical, because the curtain abrades against itself every time it coils and uncoils, showing horizontal marks on the slat faces. Factory roll-coated and powder-coated curtains resist it far better than field-painted ones, and the door manufacturer should be consulted before any finish is applied so it does not react with the original coating. Once the coating is broken on an opening near the water, chloride reaches the galvanized layer and pitting follows at the guides and the bottom bar. Sectional commercial hardware failures are covered under <a href="../garage-door-hinge-repair/">hinge and bracket repair</a>, and documentation of safety devices under <a href="../garage-door-safety-inspection/">safety inspection</a>.</p>

## svc_emergency_garage_door_repair_lede

Two situations cannot wait for a scheduled visit: a car sealed behind a door that will not lift, and a door stuck open with the house exposed. Both have a correct sequence, and in storm season the sequence matters more than the speed.

## svc_emergency_garage_door_repair_body

<h2>Getting a car out when the door will not move</h2>
<p>Every residential operator has a manual disconnect, and DASMA's guidance on power outages says that red handle belongs about 6 feet above the floor so an adult can reach it without a ladder. Pulling it releases the trolley and the door becomes a hand-operated door. What the handle does not do is make an unbalanced door safe to lift, so a door with a snapped spring stays where it is. The same guidance notes that an operator's backup battery needs replacement every 2 to 5 years, which is why a unit that worked last September may do nothing this September. Other failures on the <a href="../services/">repair list</a> are urgent for different reasons.</p>
<h2>The four gripping points a door is supposed to have</h2>
<p>Manual operation is designed in, not improvised. ANSI/DASMA 116 requires four handles or gripping points on a door built for hand operation: two inside and two outside, within 8 inches of the bottom edge of the bottom section and again on the second or third section, positioned vertically in line. The joint between two sections is explicitly not a suitable gripping point, because fingers in that gap are what the design is trying to prevent. Many doors in service have lost their exterior handles to corrosion, and lifting by the section joint is how a routine power failure produces an injury.</p>
<h2>Before a named storm, lock it and unplug it</h2>
<p>The wind guidance is counterintuitive and worth following exactly. A door provided with a manual locking device should be closed, mechanically locked, and the operator disconnected while it stays locked, then unlocked before normal operation resumes. Operating an opener against an engaged lock damages both. The reason is geometry: the vertical rail tilts back toward the header and the top rollers sit in the curve, so wind pressure on the panel converts into upward force that tries to drive the door open. That mechanism is why doors failed in Hurricane Andrew in 1992 and Hurricane Katrina in 2005, and it is why a locked door resists what an unlocked one does not.</p>
<h2>What the season and the fee schedule imply about timing</h2>
<p>The Atlantic season runs from June 1 to November 30, averaging 14 named storms, 7 hurricanes and 3 major hurricanes across the 1991 to 2020 record, and peaking in the second week of September. On October 24, 2005, Boca Raton stayed in Hurricane Wilma's eyewall longer than surrounding communities. The city's fee schedule carries a dedicated garage door permit line for storm damage repair within one year of a named storm event, with retrofitting excluded and a reinspection charged separately. The practical reading is that post-storm repairs are a documented category, and that a door which kept operating after a wind event still needs its struts, brackets and jamb anchors looked at.</p>
<h2>A door stuck open overnight</h2>
<p>An opening that cannot be closed is a security problem before it is a mechanical one. If the door will hold position, it can be lowered by hand and secured with a clamp or a locking pliers on the vertical rail below the bottom roller, above the roller so the door cannot rise. If the spring has failed the door will not hold, and the safe interim is the door left down with the operator unplugged so nobody presses a button. Cars parked in the driveway rather than the garage remove the reason to force it. Off-track and cable failures are covered under <a href="../off-track-garage-door-repair/">off-track repair</a>, and the locking hardware itself under <a href="../garage-door-lock-repair/">locks and handles</a>.</p>

## svc_garage_door_hinge_repair_lede

Hinges, end stiles and bottom brackets are the parts that hold a door together while it changes shape twice a cycle. When one cracks, the load moves to its neighbors, and the failure spreads along the section rather than staying put.

## svc_garage_door_hinge_repair_body

<h2>Numbered hinges, and why the number matters</h2>
<p>Hinges on a sectional door are numbered by position, and the numbers are not decorative. A number one hinge sits at the intermediate joint nearest the bottom, and the numbers rise as the joint moves up the door, because each position needs a different offset to keep the panels tracking as the door bends into the horizontal rail. A number three hinge fitted where a number one belongs pulls the roller slightly out of alignment for every cycle afterward, and the wear shows up as a rasping roller and a cracked hinge leaf months later. End hinges also carry the roller stem, so a cracked end hinge is a track problem waiting to happen. Related failures are grouped on the <a href="../services/">repair list</a>.</p>
<h2>Torn stiles, and the fastener holes that caused it</h2>
<p>A hinge rarely fails alone. The end stile is the vertical member at the edge of each section that the hinge and roller bracket fasten into, and on a steel door it is a thin formed channel. Once a screw hole elongates, the hinge moves under load, the movement works the surrounding metal, and the stile tears. Refastening into the same torn hole lasts weeks. The repair is a stile reinforcement or a section replacement, chosen by how far the tear has run and whether the section is still straight. On a wind-rated door the stile is part of the rated assembly, which limits what can be substituted.</p>
<h2>Bottom brackets, and the one part to leave alone</h2>
<p>The bottom bracket at each side of the door anchors the lift cable, so it holds the full counterbalance load whenever the door is down. Removing or loosening one with the spring wound is how people are seriously injured. A bottom bracket that has pulled its fasteners out of the bottom section is a common find after a vehicle nudge or a cable failure, and it is not a bracket-only repair: the section it pulled out of has to be assessed, and the cable it holds has to be inspected at the drum wrap where corrosion concentrates. Cutting power to the operator is the extent of what should happen before a technician arrives.</p>
<h2>Block jambs, stucco and the anchors that actually hold</h2>
<p>South Florida construction changes the fastening problem. Where a wood-framed garage takes lag screws into a jamb, a concrete block jamb finished in stucco needs masonry anchors, and DASMA publishes fastener schedules for exactly this: quarter-inch by three-inch self-tapping anchors, three-eighths-inch sleeve anchors, three-eighths by three-and-a-half-inch expansion anchors, and larger L-bolts among them. Spacing is not a habit either. It comes from a calculation in which the maximum anchor spacing falls as the design wind pressure and the door width rise, which is why a wider door on the same wall needs more anchors, not the same anchors further apart.</p>
<h2>The pressure the fasteners are being asked to resist</h2>
<p>The load side of that calculation is local. The city applies a 170 mph wind velocity to a single-family residence and treats buildings as Exposure C unless Exposure D applies, and the code's own tables require the tabulated door pressure to be multiplied by an adjustment factor, 1.21 for Exposure C at a fifteen-foot mean roof height and 1.47 for Exposure D. An anchor pattern adequate at one exposure is not automatically adequate at the other. Hardware found loose during a track or roller repair is covered under <a href="../off-track-garage-door-repair/">off-track repair</a>, and the formal check of pads, brackets and anchors under <a href="../garage-door-safety-inspection/">safety inspection</a>.</p>

## svc_garage_door_insulation_lede

Insulating a garage door in a hot, humid climate is a heat-gain and moisture decision rather than a heating one. Two different numbers get quoted for it, they measure different things, and only one of them describes the door that will hang in the opening.

## svc_garage_door_insulation_body

<h2>R-value and U-factor are not interchangeable</h2>
<p>DASMA is explicit that the two are obtained by entirely different methods and are not reciprocals of each other. An R-value is calculated for a door section only, adding the resistance of the insulation core to the surface and air film contributions, with metal facings treated as negligible and the air films totaling 0.85 based on published data. A U-factor is measured on a complete installed door assembly, track and hardware and joints included, under the ANSI/DASMA 105 test standard. Because section joints, stiles and end caps create thermal shortcuts around the core, the tested assembly figure is the one that describes real performance, and it is the form building codes use. The rest of the <a href="../services/">service list</a> covers the mechanical side of the same door.</p>
<h2>How the core is actually rated</h2>
<p>The insulation number itself comes from a k-factor, a measured thermal conductivity traced to testing under ASTM C177 or ASTM C518 at an average material temperature of 40 degrees Fahrenheit, divided into the insulation thickness. Doors with varying core thickness across the section are estimated by examining the value at each thickness. That is why two doors advertised at the same figure can behave differently: one may be polystyrene board loosely fitted between ribs, the other polyurethane foamed in place and bonded to both skins. The bonded construction also stiffens the section, which matters for a rated door.</p>
<h2>Heat gain, glazing and the direction the door faces</h2>
<p>Insulation is only one of the flows. Solar heat gain depends on how much glazing the door carries, the glazing type, the direction the door faces, and the local climate, and it is quantified as a solar heat gain coefficient using NFRC calculation methods, with testing available for doors that fit a seven-by-seven-foot opening. Air infiltration through the assembly is measured under the same DASMA 105 standard as the U-factor. On a west-facing door with a row of windows, glazing choice will move afternoon temperatures in the garage more than a step up in core thickness will.</p>
<h2>Condensation in a humid garage, and what it is telling you</h2>
<p>Adding insulation changes where moisture collects. Condensation forms when humid air meets a cooler surface, and DASMA notes it is characteristic of a warm humid garage against a cooler door face rather than a product defect, with glazing the most likely place to see it. Even double or triple pane units can show it in a humid garage. Fogging is different from condensation and can indicate a failed seal in a double pane unit, which is a service item rather than a ventilation item. Ventilation to control humidity, tight seals around the glazing perimeter and at the section joints, and manufacturer-approved cleaning chemicals are the controls that work.</p>
<h2>The trade-off nobody mentions until the door arrives</h2>
<p>An insulated door weighs more than the door it replaces, sometimes substantially more, and the counterbalance has to match. Springs sized for the old door will leave the new one heavy at the floor and light at the top, and an operator asked to make up the difference wears out early. In a city where 65.8 percent of housing units are owner-occupied and the garage is often a workshop as well as a parking space, the upgrade usually makes sense, but it is a door-and-spring job rather than a panel swap. Seal work that has to happen alongside it is covered under <a href="../garage-door-weather-seal-replacement/">weather seal replacement</a>, and full assemblies under <a href="../garage-door-replacement/">door replacement</a>.</p>

## svc_garage_door_lock_repair_lede

The lock on a garage door has two jobs that pull against each other: keeping the door shut when the power is out or a storm is coming, and staying entirely out of the way when an operator is driving the door. Most lock calls come from that second job being neglected.

## svc_garage_door_lock_repair_body

<h2>What the hardware consists of</h2>
<p>A T-handle turns a spindle that drives two flat bars sideways into the vertical rails, and a slide bolt is a simple manual latch on one rail, thrown by hand from inside. Some doors carry a cylinder lock that turns a cam behind the face of the bottom section, and some carry only the exterior handle with no locking function at all. Interior slide bolts on both sides are the arrangement seen most often on older doors here. Knowing which of these is fitted determines whether the door can be locked at all, which is a question worth answering before it is needed rather than during an outage. Other hardware failures sit on the <a href="../services/">repair list</a>.</p>
<h2>The lock that jammed the opener</h2>
<p>An operator has no way to know a bolt is thrown. It applies force, the bar binds against the rail, and something gives: a stripped drive gear, a sheared trolley, a bent bar, or a torn end stile where the lock body is mounted. The DASMA guidance on high wind events makes the sequence explicit, saying the operator should be disconnected while the door is mechanically locked, and unlocked before normal operation resumes, because using an operator on a locked door damages both. Where a door was fitted with an operator years after it was built, the correct step is to disable the manual locks rather than to trust that nobody will use them.</p>
<h2>Why the lock matters more on a rated door</h2>
<p>The reason the sequence exists is structural. Wind pressure on a closed panel does not push the door straight back. The vertical rail tilts toward the header and the top rollers sit in the curve, so pressure converts into upward force that tries to drive the door open along its own track. A manual locking device provided with a higher wind-resistant door is part of how that door achieves its rating, and a door lifted a few inches off its stops in a gust has already lost the geometry it was rated with. That mechanism was seen in Hurricane Andrew in 1992 and again in Hurricane Katrina in 2005.</p>
<h2>Handles, corrosion and the ability to move the door by hand</h2>
<p>Locking hardware and manual handling hardware are usually on the same section, and they corrode together. A door built for hand operation should carry four gripping points, two inside and two outside, within 8 inches of the bottom edge of the bottom section and on the second or third section, in line vertically, and the joint between sections is specifically not one of them. On an opening within a mile or two of the water, exterior handles are frequently the first fastenings to fail, leaving the door with nothing to grip in a power failure. Replacing them is a small job that becomes urgent at the worst possible time.</p>
<h2>Deciding the lock question before June</h2>
<p>The season begins June 1 and peaks in the second week of September, and a door that cannot be locked is a decision that has to be made in advance, not during a watch. A door with no manual locking device should not be improvised with a bolt through a track hole, because the load path that produced the rating did not include it. Whether the correct answer is new hardware, disabling the existing locks permanently, or a rated replacement depends on the door. The overnight and storm-season procedures are set out under <a href="../emergency-garage-door-repair/">emergency repair</a>, and gear damage caused by a lock left engaged is covered under <a href="../garage-door-opener-repair/">opener repair</a>.</p>

## svc_garage_door_opener_installation_lede

Choosing an operator is mostly about the door it will move and the garage it will live in. Drive type, rail length, and where the power and low-voltage wiring already run decide the install more than horsepower claims do.

## svc_garage_door_opener_installation_body

<h2>Drive types, judged by what they do differently</h2>
<p>A chain drive turns a sprocket and pulls a chain along the rail. It is loud, mechanically simple, and unbothered by weight. A belt drive substitutes a reinforced belt for the chain and is markedly quieter, which is the deciding factor when living space shares a wall or a floor with the garage. A jackshaft unit mounts on the wall beside the torsion shaft and drives the shaft directly, freeing the ceiling entirely, which suits a garage with a low header or storage overhead. Rail length is a separate decision: a door taller than seven feet needs an extension kit, and ordering the door and the rail together avoids a second visit. The <a href="../services/">service list</a> covers repairs to units already installed.</p>
<h2>The wiring the operator expects to find</h2>
<p>DASMA's pre-wiring guidance, developed with the International Door Association, is specific. Three lengths of two-conductor Class 2 bell wire are needed, of a type such as CL2, and low-voltage runs must not share holes or conduit with the 120-volt line, because that violates the National Electrical Code and can interfere with safe operation. Sensor wires are positioned about 6 inches off the floor on each side of the opening with 24 inches of slack left. The wall button goes at least 5 feet above the floor, away from moving parts, out of a child's reach. For a rail-mounted unit the grounded 120-volt outlet sits roughly 10 feet 6 inches from the opening, centered, and moves out another foot for every foot of door height above seven feet.</p>
<h2>What the federal rule requires, and what Florida adds</h2>
<p>Any residential operator manufactured on or after January 1, 1993 has to carry inherent primary entrapment protection that reverses within 2 seconds of contacting an obstruction, an inherent system that either tracks door position in increments of an inch or less or reverses the door after a 30-second timer, and a secondary system such as photo eyes or a sensing edge. DASMA's summary of state legislation lists added installation requirements in California, Minnesota, New York and Indiana. Florida is not among them, so the federal standard is the operative rule here, along with whatever the city requires. That is worth stating plainly rather than implying a state-specific approval exists.</p>
<h2>Older garages in the Boca Raton Riviera and the missing receptacle</h2>
<p>Riviera was platted in four stages in 1945, and homes there from the late forties and early fifties were not built around ceiling-mounted machinery. What the ceiling has is often a pull-chain lampholder rather than a grounded duplex receptacle in the right place, and the low-voltage path to the opening does not exist at all. Adding a properly located circuit is electrical work, and the city's exemption from an electrical permit is limited to a short list that covers items such as replacing a lighting fixture or a receptacle, not a new circuit. Scoping that before the operator is ordered keeps the install to one day.</p>
<h2>Weight, balance and the check that ends the visit</h2>
<p>An operator does not lift a door, it moves a balanced door. Fitting a new unit to a door whose springs are tired transfers the load to a motor and gearbox that were never rated for it, and the guidance is explicit that an operator should not be expected to compensate for a poorly maintained door. Before the head goes up, the door gets a hand test, the springs and cables get read, and the top section is checked for the reinforcement the operator bracket needs. Force settings are then trimmed to the minimum that operates the door reliably. Units that are failing rather than aging belong under <a href="../garage-door-opener-repair/">opener repair</a>, and phone control is covered under <a href="../smart-garage-door-opener/">smart opener installation</a>.</p>

## svc_garage_door_remote_programming_lede

Remotes and keypads fail in ways that look like an operator failure and almost never are. Dead cells, a learn sequence that never completed, and radio interference from inside the same garage account for most of the calls.

## svc_garage_door_remote_programming_body

<h2>Start with the two dull answers</h2>
<p>Most transmitter complaints trace to a battery that is old, installed backward, or a cell that was already flat on the shelf, and to physical damage from a visor clip that dropped the remote onto a driveway. Those two account for the majority of failures before radio theory enters the discussion. A remote that works at two feet and not at twenty is a weak transmitter. A remote that never works while the wall control does is a pairing problem, and the wall control staying reliable is the clue that the operator itself is fine. The rest of the <a href="../services/">repair list</a> is worth reading only after both have been ruled out.</p>
<h2>Rolling codes and what pairing actually does</h2>
<p>Modern systems do not store a fixed code. The transmitter and the receiver agree on a sequence and step through it together, so a captured press cannot be replayed. Pairing writes a new transmitter into the receiver's memory, and that memory holds a limited number of devices. Adding a fifth remote to a receiver that holds four silently drops the oldest one, which is how a working keypad stops working the week a new car arrives. Clearing the memory and re-adding every device in one pass is faster than chasing one unit at a time.</p>
<h2>The interference sources sitting in the same ceiling</h2>
<p>DASMA's troubleshooting guidance is specific about what shortens range. Certain LED and compact fluorescent bulbs emit radio noise close to the receiver, and the Federal Communications Commission regulates that emission because it is a known problem. Local wireless security systems sharing the band do the same. High-power radio or television transmitters within a few miles reduce range across a whole neighborhood, and a Department of Defense land mobile radio system operating since 2004 near military installations was expected to affect as many as 40 million homes. When the interference is external, the fix is a retrofit to a less susceptible frequency rather than a new operator, and the hard-wired wall control keeps the garage usable while parts are ordered.</p>
<h2>Why exterior keypads corrode first in Camino Gardens</h2>
<p>Camino Gardens rose on the site of the Africa U.S.A. theme park, which closed in 1961, with the new development underway by 1962 around houses designed by local architect Howard McCall. Those garages face the street with the keypad mounted beside the opening, and that position takes sun, humidity and salt-laden air all day. The membrane over the keys cracks, water reaches the contact traces, and pressed keys start registering twice or not at all. A keypad that needs a firm push has already begun this failure. Replacement is straightforward once the wiring is confirmed, because the same two conductors serve the new unit.</p>
<h2>What gets left behind after the visit</h2>
<p>A finished job leaves every device paired, the memory documented, and the owner able to add a car without help. Programming is also the moment to confirm that a lost remote has been erased rather than left in memory, which matters more than a new transmitter does. If the operator has begun forgetting devices on its own, the receiver board is at fault and belongs under <a href="../garage-door-opener-repair/">opener repair</a>. Households that would rather manage access from a phone than a visor clip should look at what is involved in a <a href="../smart-garage-door-opener/">smart opener installation</a>.</p>

## svc_garage_door_safety_inspection_lede

A safety inspection is a pass or fail against published requirements, not an opinion about a door's condition. Most of the items have a number attached, and a door either meets it or does not.

## svc_garage_door_safety_inspection_body

<h2>The federal baseline every operator is measured against</h2>
<p>Residential operators manufactured on or after January 1, 1993 have to provide inherent primary entrapment protection plus a second layer, either a constant-pressure control, an external device such as photo eyes or a sensing edge, or an inherent secondary system. The reversal has to begin within 2 seconds of contact. A switch or relay in an entrapment protection circuit has to withstand 100,000 cycles of operation, and if one malfunctions the operator must move the door to and remain within one foot of the fully open position. Those requirements are what an inspection tests, and they are why an operator that predates 1993 fails on age alone. The <a href="../services/">repair list</a> covers what the findings lead to.</p>
<h2>The reversal test, done the way the standard describes it</h2>
<p>The test object is a solid piece of lumber laid flat, one and a half inches high, centered under the door path, and the test is required both at installation and monthly thereafter. The door must contact it, reverse, and return. A door that stops without reversing fails, and so does one that reverses only after visibly pressing the object down. Force settings are then examined: they should be set to the minimum needed to operate the door reliably, because a unit dialed up to overcome a binding door has had its primary protection defeated by the adjustment. The photo eyes are checked separately, and they should never sit higher than six inches off the floor.</p>
<h2>The items an inspector checks that owners never think about</h2>
<p>The published code inspection guidance for sectional doors covers ground that has nothing to do with electronics. Torsion spring pads must be structurally sound material fastened to load-bearing members. Horizontal and vertical tracks must be connected to sound load-bearing material. The manual release on a residential operator has to be easily accessible and within six feet of the floor. A control station must be mounted at least five feet above the floor, in clear view, away from moving parts and out of the reach of children. Warning labels have to be present as the manufacturer's documentation requires. Any single no answer stops the inspection at that item.</p>
<h2>What corrosion does to a pass on this coast</h2>
<p>An inspection near the ocean has to read the metal, not just the function. Rust reduces the effective area of spring wire and lowers its strength, and corrosion pits become the origin of fatigue cracks that cut cycle life, which means a spring can pass a balance test in May and part in August. The same logic applies to lift cables at the drum wrap, to hinge and roller bearings, and to the fasteners holding brackets to a block jamb, where a screw can spin in a corroded hole while still looking seated. A useful report names the location and the progression, so the next inspection has something to compare against.</p>
<h2>Timing, records and the documents the city expects</h2>
<p>Two dates drive the schedule: the monthly reversal test the standard asks the owner to perform, and an annual professional check placed before the June 1 start of the season. Where work has been permitted, the city requires complete and legible product approval or Notice of Acceptance documents for installed products to be on site for inspection, with a reinspection fee if they are missing, so keeping that paperwork with the door records is part of passing rather than an afterthought. Preventive hardware work that follows an inspection is covered under <a href="../garage-door-tune-up/">tune-up and maintenance</a>, and photo-eye findings under <a href="../garage-door-sensor-repair/">sensor repair</a>.</p>

## svc_garage_door_sensor_repair_lede

A door that starts down, stops short of the slab and runs itself back up is rarely a spring or a motor fault. It is a beam that has lost its partner across the opening, and near the Atlantic the cause is usually a hazed lens or a bracket that has drifted out of line.

## svc_garage_door_sensor_repair_body

<h2>Where the beam is supposed to sit</h2>
<p>Photo eyes are not aimed by eye. DASMA's guidance on sensor placement puts the beam no higher than 6 inches above the garage floor, within 6 inches of the door path measured perpendicular to it, and allows a spacing of up to 20 feet apart on a wide opening. Those numbers come out of ANSI/CAN/UL 325, and the same guidance asks the owner to test the beam monthly. This is the least expensive item on the <a href="../services/">full list of repairs</a>, and it is also the one most often mistaken for something serious.</p>
<h2>What haze on a lens does to a signal</h2>
<p>The lens does not have to be dirty to fail. Air off the shoreline leaves a fine chloride film that dries into a translucent crust, and lawn irrigation adds mineral scale to the same glass. Neither blocks light completely. Both cut the received signal enough that the receiver drops the beam intermittently, which is why the door closes on the third try and reverses on the fourth. Intermittent is the signature. A sensor that never works points at wiring, and a sensor that works four times out of five points at the lens or at alignment.</p>
<h2>Why the Boca Raton Riviera openings drift out of line</h2>
<p>Riviera was platted in four separate stages in 1945, and a few homes there still date from the late forties and early fifties. Those garages were built for one car, with block jambs finished in stucco, and the sensor brackets on them are usually retrofits screwed into that block rather than into wood framing. A masonry anchor in a hole that has corroded and widened holds position until a bicycle or a trash can nudges it. After that the bracket rotates a few degrees, the beam crosses the opening high, and the door starts refusing the last foot of travel.</p>
<h2>How the fault gets isolated</h2>
<p>The indicator lights do most of the work. One steady light on the sending eye and a dark receiver means the beam is broken or misaimed. A flickering receiver with the opening clear means signal loss rather than obstruction. Swapping the two eyes tells whether a unit has failed or the wire feeding it has. Staples driven through the insulation years ago are a common find, and so is a splice that has greened inside the wire nut. An operator that detects a faulty sensor is not permitted to close on a remote press at all, which is the behavior owners describe as the door ignoring the button.</p>
<h2>Whether the work involves the building department</h2>
<p>Low-voltage sensor wiring is not part of the wind load protection on the opening, so it does not run into the limit that ends the city's small-repair exemption for doors and shutters. That matters for scope: if the same visit finds a bent track or a loose jamb anchor, the job has crossed from an electrical nuisance into structural territory. Sensors that reverse the door because the door itself binds are handled on the <a href="../garage-door-opener-repair/">opener repair page</a>, and the full decision order for a door that refuses the floor is set out under <a href="../garage-door-wont-close/">will not close</a>.</p>

## svc_garage_door_tune_up_lede

A tune-up is a measurement visit, not a lubrication visit. The point is to find the parts that are close to failing while the door still works, and the checks that do it are published and repeatable.

## svc_garage_door_tune_up_body

<h2>The published checklist and what it actually covers</h2>
<p>DASMA's residential checklist takes about 15 minutes and needs a tape measure, a flashlight, a remote, a ladder, and a solid object 1.5 inches high and at least six inches long. It divides into Part A for the door and Part B for the operator, and any answer of no stops the inspection until that item is resolved. Two findings end it immediately: extension springs without a containment cable running through them, and a door that will not hold position when left three to four feet off the floor. Neither is a maintenance item. Both belong to the counterbalance section of the <a href="../services/">repair list</a>.</p>
<h2>Counting cycles instead of counting years</h2>
<p>A cycle is one trip from closed to fully open and back. ANSI/DASMA 102 sets a floor of 10,000 cycles for a properly selected and maintained door system, so the interval before wear is arithmetic rather than a guess. A household that uses the garage as its main entrance runs six to eight cycles a day and reaches 10,000 in about four years. A door opened twice a day takes well over a decade. Cycle count, not the age on the label, is what predicts when rollers, hinges, bearings and cables need attention.</p>
<h2>Where the coastal inspection differs from an inland one</h2>
<p>Corrosion is the item that changes the schedule here. DASMA's guidance on spring cycle life states that springs should be kept dry to prevent surface rust, that rust reduces the effective area of the spring wire and lowers its strength, and that corrosion pits become the origin of fatigue cracks that shorten cycle life. On an opening near the ocean, brown staining on the coils, the drums, the bearing plates and the cable at the drum wrap is a measurement of remaining life. A checklist that records where the staining is, and how far it has progressed since the last visit, is more useful than a checklist that records only pass or fail.</p>
<h2>Doing it before the first of June</h2>
<p>The city manages more than 4.75 miles of shoreline, and the season that puts pressure on those openings opens on June 1. Late spring is therefore the sensible slot: a door that is balanced, whose lift cables have full cross section, whose bottom brackets and jamb anchors are tight, and whose manual disconnect and exterior handles work, is a door that can be locked down and hand-operated when it has to be. Finding a seized drum in May is a scheduled repair. Finding it in September is a different conversation.</p>
<h2>What a completed visit leaves in writing</h2>
<p>The record matters as much as the wrench work. A useful tune-up report lists the balance test result, the reversal test result with the 1.5 inch object, the photo eye height and alignment, the fastener torque at the bottom brackets and jamb, the condition of each cable at the drum, and any part now on watch with the reason. That gives the next visit a baseline instead of a fresh opinion. Springs found near end of life are handled under <a href="../garage-door-spring-repair/">spring repair</a>, and the formal pass or fail against the federal operator rules is documented under <a href="../garage-door-safety-inspection/">safety inspection</a>.</p>

## svc_garage_door_weather_seal_replacement_lede

Water on a garage floor after a squall is usually blamed on the slab. More often it entered at the bottom seal, at the side stop molding, or between two sections, and each of those is a different part with a different fix.

## svc_garage_door_weather_seal_replacement_body

<h2>What a sectional door can and cannot be expected to do</h2>
<p>The honest starting point is that these doors are not watertight, and DASMA states it directly: sectional garage doors are not leak proof, and United States building codes have traditionally set no water infiltration requirement for them. Three paths account for what gets in. Water dripping off the panels when the door is raised, water held in the joints between sections that wicks sideways past the perimeter seal, and wind driving rain through the sides, the bottom, or between sections until it becomes a continuous stream. The window test methods people cite, ASTM E331, ASTM E1105 and AAMA 501.1, are window tests and were not written for doors. The <a href="../services/">repair list</a> treats each intrusion path separately.</p>
<h2>The parts, named correctly</h2>
<p>The bottom seal is an astragal, a flexible strip in a retainer on the bottom section, and it is the part that fails first because it takes the abrasion. Side stop molding covers the gap at each jamb and should overlap the jamb by about an inch per side. Header stop molding does the same at the top. A threshold is a separate aluminum or rubber piece bonded to the slab that the astragal meets. Between the sections themselves there are joint seals that most owners never see. Replacing an astragal when the leak is at a jamb is the most common wasted repair on this list.</p>
<h2>Slab geometry, and the numbers that make a threshold work</h2>
<p>The details are dimensional. DASMA's guidance on infiltration under the bottom describes an inclined lip or sill sloped at 3 to 5 degrees so water runs away from the opening, or a notch in the slab of at least half an inch that accommodates a two-inch track and a two-inch thick bottom section. Where a driveway apron slopes toward the garage instead, no seal will hold against standing water, and the correct answer is a threshold plus a rain lip rather than a thicker astragal. Adjustable jamb brackets matter too, because they let the door be pulled tight to the stop molding instead of leaving a gap the seal has to bridge.</p>
<h2>Wind-driven rain against a wide double door</h2>
<p>Pressure is what turns a drip into a stream. A sixteen-foot door presents a large area, and during a season that runs from June through November the rain arrives horizontally rather than vertically. Under positive pressure the panels deflect inward slightly, the perimeter seals unload at the moment they are needed most, and water enters at the corners first. This is also why the city's own over-the-counter affidavit for windows, doors and shutters references water infiltration testing provisions in the building code rather than treating water as a cosmetic issue. Enhanced perimeter seals, section joint seals and a threshold together address it, and no single one of them does.</p>
<h2>Whether replacing a seal touches the permit question</h2>
<p>Seal replacement on its own is maintenance, and it is the kind of work that fits inside the city's exemption for small repairs to a one- or two-family dwelling. The exemption ends where work adversely affects building wind load protection, which includes doors, so a job that begins as a seal and turns into new jamb brackets, new fasteners into the jamb, or a bottom section replacement has changed category and needs to be treated that way. Rust starting at the bottom section behind a failed astragal often points to the <a href="../garage-door-replacement/">panel and door replacement page</a>, and a seal replaced as part of a scheduled visit is described under <a href="../garage-door-tune-up/">tune-up and maintenance</a>.</p>

## svc_garage_door_wont_close_lede

A door that opens normally and refuses to close is reporting something specific. The federal operator rules make the behavior readable, because they define exactly how a compliant unit must respond to an obstruction, a lost sensor, or a control fault.

## svc_garage_door_wont_close_body

<h2>Read the reversal before touching anything</h2>
<p>Federal rule 16 CFR 1211 governs residential operators manufactured on or after January 1, 1993, and it sets the timing. Outside the first foot of travel from the fully open position, the operator has to begin reversing within 2 seconds of contact with an obstruction, then return the door to the fully open position. The test object on the floor is 1 inch high, and the door cannot be driven back down until the operator has reversed it at least 2 inches. Where in the travel the door quits, and whether it reverses fully or stops, narrows the cause faster than anything else on the <a href="../services/">repair list</a> does.</p>
<h2>The order that separates five different faults</h2>
<p>A door that reverses immediately at the floor with a clear opening is a sensor or an alignment problem. A door that reverses partway up the travel is meeting resistance, which means a roller, a rail, or a section. A door that hums without moving has lost its drive connection. A door that closes only when the wall button is held down has a monitored sensor circuit reporting a fault, because a compliant operator may not close on a remote press with a faulty external sensor. A door that runs to the floor and instantly returns is set with too little down force, or the down limit is set below the slab. Guessing between those five is what turns one visit into three.</p>
<h2>Rainy season debris in the beam path and the track</h2>
<p>The Atlantic hurricane season runs June 1 through November 30, with a climatological peak around September 10, and it is when the failure changes character. Wind pushes leaf litter and sand across the opening into the beam path, standing water on a driveway apron carries grit into the bottom of the vertical rails, and a wet bottom seal freezes to nothing but grips the slab. The door then stalls in the last inch and reads as an obstruction. This is worth checking before a part is ordered, because a swept rail and a rinsed lens have ended more of these calls than a replacement board has.</p>
<h2>Older openings in Pearl City and the narrow approach</h2>
<p>Pearl City was established around 1915 between Dixie and Federal Highways and is the city's oldest community, designated as a historic neighborhood in 2002. Vehicle access there developed on narrow lots, and the garages and enclosed carports are frequently later additions with a short driveway and a tight turn into the opening. Bumper contact with a bottom bracket or a vertical rail is common on that geometry, and the resulting quarter inch of misalignment shows up as a door that refuses the last foot of closing rather than as visible damage. Looking at the rail before the electronics saves the diagnosis.</p>
<h2>What a monitored circuit fault means for a permit</h2>
<p>An operator that will not close without constant pressure is doing what the standard requires, not failing. The repair is the sensor circuit, and low-voltage work of that kind sits outside the wind load protection limit that ends the city's small-repair permit exemption. A bent rail or a loose jamb anchor found in the same visit does not. The photo-eye side of this is covered under <a href="../garage-door-sensor-repair/">sensor repair</a>, and the reversal and force checks that document a door as compliant are described under <a href="../garage-door-safety-inspection/">safety inspection</a>.</p>

## svc_noisy_garage_door_repair_lede

Noise is a diagnosis, not a nuisance. Each part in a door assembly fails with its own sound, and getting the part right from the driveway is the difference between a lubrication call and a bearing replacement.

## svc_noisy_garage_door_repair_body

<h2>Sorting the four sounds</h2>
<p>A grinding rasp that tracks with door movement is usually a roller whose bearing has lost its cage, dragging steel on steel inside the rail. A rhythmic squeal that speeds up as the door accelerates is a dry hinge pin. A sharp pop at one point in the travel, always the same point, is a section joint or a hinge leaf flexing where a fastener has backed out. A low rumble that seems to come from the wall above the opening is the torsion shaft turning in a dry end bearing, and that one gets worse quietly. The rest of the <a href="../services/">service list</a> covers the failures these sounds warn about.</p>
<h2>Why grease turns into a grinding paste here</h2>
<p>Lubricant on a coastal opening does not simply dry out. Humid air keeps a film of moisture on every bearing surface, chloride dust settles into the grease, and the mixture becomes an abrasive slurry that polishes races and pits shafts. That is the mechanical reason a door two miles from the water can sound worse at six years than an inland door at fifteen. It is also why heavy grease is the wrong answer: it holds more of the grit against the metal. A light lubricant applied to clean hardware, on the hinge pins, the bearings and the spring, does the job without building a paste.</p>
<h2>The houses where noise reaches a bedroom</h2>
<p>Most of the city's housing stock went up during a long expansion, from 6,961 residents in 1960 to roughly 28,500 by 1972. Those plans put the garage inside the building envelope with living space on the other side of a shared wall or directly above it, which is why a door that would be ignored in a detached garage becomes a household argument. Structure carries the sound. A bracket transmitting vibration into a framed wall is louder inside the house than it is standing in the driveway, and the loudest part is often not the part that is worn.</p>
<h2>Hardware mounted to drywall, and what it telegraphs</h2>
<p>A retrofit is often the source. DASMA's guidance on drywall surfaces is blunt about mounting track brackets, bearing plates and angles to drywall at all, and prefers cutting the board away or fastening a wood member over it, with fasteners a half inch longer to pass through half-inch board and reach the framing. A 2x6 or larger at each jamb and a 2x4 or larger at the header are the recommended backing. Where none of that was done, lag screws hold in gypsum and vibration, and every cycle drums the panel like a soundboard. Refastening into the framing usually removes more noise than any lubricant will.</p>
<h2>The balance test that comes before anything is oiled</h2>
<p>A noisy door that is also out of balance will come back. With the operator disconnected the door should hold still when left three to four feet off the floor, neither climbing nor sinking, and it should move by hand without dragging at any point in the travel. A door that fails that test is loading its rollers and hinges unevenly, and the noise is a symptom of the counterbalance rather than the hardware. Scheduled hardware work belongs under <a href="../garage-door-tune-up/">tune-up and maintenance</a>, and cracked leaves or torn stiles are handled on the <a href="../garage-door-hinge-repair/">hinge and bracket page</a>.</p>

## svc_smart_garage_door_opener_lede

Phone control changes what the door is allowed to do on its own, and the standards treat that as a safety question rather than a convenience feature. Knowing which requirements attach to unattended closing is the difference between a working setup and a door that refuses remote commands.

## svc_smart_garage_door_opener_body

<h2>Unattended operation, and the three conditions it carries</h2>
<p>Closing a door from a phone when nobody is standing there is unattended operation, and UL 325 was revised in 2009 to permit it under conditions. The door system needs secondary entrapment protection such as photo eyes, the door has to be a sectional door, and the system must give both an audible and a visual alert for 5 seconds before the door begins to move. That five-second warning is not a manufacturer flourish, and a retrofit module that bypasses it is not a compliant installation. The rest of the <a href="../services/">repair list</a> deals with doors that move when they are asked to.</p>
<h2>Retrofit module or new operator</h2>
<p>A retrofit module wires into the wall-control terminals of an existing operator and adds a door-position sensor and a Wi-Fi radio. It works well on a unit with a healthy board and a monitored sensor circuit, and it is the cheaper path by a wide margin. It is the wrong path on an operator old enough to predate the federal entrapment rules, because adding remote closing to a door with no secondary protection increases the risk instead of the convenience. Native units integrate the radio, the alert, and often a camera, and they report status the module has to infer. Deciding between them starts with the manufacture date on the operator label.</p>
<h2>Signal, heat and humidity in an attached garage ceiling</h2>
<p>A garage ceiling is a poor place for a radio. The router is usually two walls away, the door itself is a large steel reflector, and the module sits above the vehicle bay where afternoon temperatures and humidity both run high. Marginal signal shows up as a device that reports offline at random and rejects a close command intermittently, which reads as a broken module. Verifying signal strength at the mounting point before the install, and adding an access point rather than replacing hardware, resolves most of it. The same radio noise sources that shorten remote range apply: certain LED and compact fluorescent bulbs near the receiver emit interference the Federal Communications Commission regulates for that reason.</p>
<h2>Power loss, battery backup and what Florida does not require</h2>
<p>A smart operator with no power is a manual door. California requires battery backup on operators sold or installed there after July 1, 2019, and Florida has no equivalent statute, so backup here is a decision rather than a requirement. In a season that runs June 1 to November 30 and peaks around September 10, it is a decision worth making deliberately, and the backup battery in a unit that has one needs replacement every 2 to 5 years to be there when it is needed. A smart module that loses its radio during an outage while the operator keeps working is normal behavior, not a fault.</p>
<h2>Access that survives the household changing</h2>
<p>The advantage of a phone-controlled system is auditable access: a code that expires, a guest permission that can be withdrawn, a log that shows when the door moved. That is also its failure mode. Accounts tied to one household member's email address strand everyone else when that person changes phones, and a shared password defeats the log. Setting the operator up under an account the household controls, with each user added separately, is the part that gets skipped. Programming physical transmitters and keypads is covered under <a href="../garage-door-remote-programming/">remotes and keypads</a>, and choosing the operator itself under <a href="../garage-door-opener-installation/">opener installation</a>.</p>

## svc_torsion_spring_conversion_lede

Converting a door from extension springs to a torsion bar is worth doing for two reasons: the door becomes better behaved, and the failure mode becomes far less dangerous. It is not a universal upgrade, because the garage has to have room for it.

## svc_torsion_spring_conversion_body

<h2>The two systems, and how each one lets go</h2>
<p>Extension springs stretch along the horizontal rails on both sides and pull through a pulley and cable arrangement. Torsion springs mount on a shaft above the opening and store energy in twist, transferring it through cable drums at each end. The difference that matters is what happens at failure. A stretched extension spring that parts releases a length of spring steel across the garage unless a containment cable runs through it, and the published residential checklist treats a missing containment cable as a stop-work finding. A torsion spring that breaks stays on its shaft. The <a href="../services/">service list</a> covers repairs to both systems as they are.</p>
<h2>Why a torsion system holds balance better</h2>
<p>Torsion delivers its force through drums at a fixed radius, so the assist is even across the width of the door and both sides rise together. An extension system depends on two springs, two pulleys and two cables staying matched over years of unequal wear, and once they drift the door racks slightly in its frame every cycle. That racking is what wears rollers on one side, tears one end stile, and eventually walks the door out of its track. A converted door also tends to hold position better when tested three to four feet off the floor, which is the check that says the counterbalance matches the door.</p>
<h2>The measurements the conversion depends on</h2>
<p>Nothing about this is estimated from a photograph. The door has to be weighed or calculated, the shaft length and the drum size have to suit the door height, and the geometry has to satisfy the standard: ANSI/DASMA 102 rules out torsion springs on a sectional door with an index ratio under six to one, meaning a mean coil diameter of at least six times the wire diameter. Shaft deflection is limited to 1.5 degrees per foot on a tubular shaft and 2 degrees per foot on a solid one. Spring wire is color coded by diameter and extension springs by door weight, but the published codes carry a caution that colors give only partial identification and must be confirmed against measurements and door weight, particularly on older installations.</p>
<h2>Headroom, backroom and the older openings in the city</h2>
<p>Space decides feasibility. A torsion assembly needs clear headroom above the opening for the shaft, bearing plates and a mounting pad on structurally sound material, and the low-headroom kits that exist are a compromise rather than a preference. Housing from the earlier phases of the city's growth, when the population went from 992 in 1950 to 6,961 in 1960, was built with shallow garages and low headers, and extension springs were the normal fitting on that stock. Where the header cannot take a spring pad, or where ductwork occupies the space, the conversation moves to what the existing system needs instead.</p>
<h2>What a heavier door does to the arithmetic</h2>
<p>Conversion is often bundled with a heavier door, and the counterbalance has to be sized for the door that will actually hang there. A wind-rated assembly carries heavier gauge steel, additional horizontal struts and reinforced end stiles, and every pound of that is spring work. This is also the reason that adding weight to a door afterward is a mistake: door components from sections through struts, hinges, cables, drums and fasteners are designed as an assembly for a defined weight, wind load and cycle life, and hanging storage or bolting decorative trim onto the panels can unbalance the door or twist the horizontal track. Spring failures on an unconverted door are covered under <a href="../garage-door-spring-repair/">spring repair</a>, and the finished system is documented under <a href="../garage-door-safety-inspection/">safety inspection</a>.</p>

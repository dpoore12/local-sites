## qa_1_question

What does California Civil Code section 3342 actually cover?

## qa_1_answer

Read the statute as a sequence, rather than as a slogan. It concerns a person bitten by a dog; it makes the dog’s owner the named responsible party; and it requires the bite to have happened in a public place or a private place where the person was lawfully present. The owner’s property is expressly included when lawful presence exists. The text says the dog’s prior viciousness and the owner’s knowledge of it do not change that statutory rule. An uninvited entry can therefore matter because the statute requires lawful presence; a dog that merely knocks someone down raises a different analysis because the section addresses bites.

## qa_2_question

Who must report a San Diego dog bite, and what happens to the dog?

## qa_2_answer

County Code section 62.615 assigns notice duties broadly. It covers the bitten person, a parent or guardian when a minor is bitten, the treating physician, the animal’s owner or custodian, and any person who knows of a bite by an animal subject to rabies. A person with knowledge who does not report within 24 hours violates the section unless earlier reporting was impossible. Under section 62.617, a dog or cat may be ordered into approved confinement and observation for at least 10 days; the County may allow an earlier release only after the stated veterinary-observation process. The agency, not the dog owner, controls release approval.

## qa_3_question

How long is the ordinary California deadline after a dog bite?

## qa_3_answer

Code of Civil Procedure section 335.1 sets a two-year period for an action for injury to, or death of, a person. That general deadline is different from the Government Claims Act procedure for an injury connected to a public entity: Government Code section 911.2 says a claim for personal injury must be presented within six months after accrual. A government or police dog also has a particular rule in Civil Code section 3342. The statute limits the special dog-bite action in specified law-enforcement or military circumstances, preserves it for certain uninvolved people, and requires a written agency dog-use policy for that limitation to apply. These are reasons not to treat a public-agency incident like an ordinary private-owner inquiry.

### California regulates the fee without capping it

Rule 1.5(a) of the California Rules of Professional Conduct runs to one sentence: no lawyer may enter into an agreement for, charge, or collect a fee that is unconscionable or illegal. That is the whole of the limit for a bite claim in this state. There is no graduated schedule and no ceiling by stage of the case. So the figure written into a contingency fee agreement in San Diego is a proposal being made to a client, not a rate the state handed down, and a person reading one is entitled to push back on it.

### Thirteen things that decide whether a fee is unconscionable

Rule 1.5(b) judges the fee on the facts as they stood when the agreement was signed, except where both sides expected later events to change it. The listed considerations start with whether the lawyer engaged in fraud or overreaching while setting the fee, and whether material facts went undisclosed. They take in the proportion between the fee and the value of the work performed, the relative sophistication of lawyer and client, and the novelty and difficulty of the questions. They also cover the chance that taking the matter shuts out other employment, the sum at stake and what was achieved, and the ability of the people actually doing the work.

### What section 6147 forces onto the page

Business and Professions Code section 6147(a) requires the contingency contract to be in writing, signed by both the attorney and the client or the client's guardian, with a duplicate copy handed to the client when it is signed. Four things have to appear in it. The agreed contingency rate, at 6147(a)(1). How disbursements and costs will hit both the fee and the client's own recovery, at (a)(2). Whether the client could owe compensation for related matters the contract does not cover, at (a)(3). And, at (a)(4), a plain statement that the fee is not set by law and is negotiable between attorney and client. Subdivision (b) supplies the teeth: a contract that misses any of this is voidable at the client's option, and the attorney is then limited to a reasonable fee.

### The percentages that belong to a different case

California does cap contingency percentages in exactly one area, and a dog bite is not it. Section 6146(a) covers claims against a health care provider based on professional negligence. There the limits are 25 percent of the amount recovered when the case settles before a complaint or arbitration demand is filed, and 33 percent afterward. Section 6146(a)(3) lets the plaintiff's attorney move for more on evidence of good cause. Those figures get quoted loosely around injury work generally. They have no application to a claim against a dog owner, a landlord, or a homeowners insurer, and a contract citing them for this kind of claim is citing the wrong statute.

### When the person bitten is a child

Children take a large share of serious bites, and a child's money runs on a separate track. Rule 7.955(a)(1) requires the court to apply a reasonable fee standard when allowing attorney fees payable out of money paid for the benefit of a minor, unless it approved the fee agreement in advance. Subdivision (b) gives fourteen nonexclusive factors, including three specific to contingent arrangements at (b)(13): the risk of loss the attorney carried, the costs advanced, and the delay in being paid. Subdivision (c) requires a declaration from the attorney addressing whichever factors apply, and (d) voids local rules that try to set their own standard. Under rule 7.952(a), the person petitioning and the child both have to appear at the hearing unless the court excuses them for good cause. Where the court orders a guardianship investigation, Probate Code 1513.1 puts that at $800 on the San Diego schedule.

### What the courthouse actually charges

Litigation costs are published, not negotiated. San Diego's Fee Schedule 1, effective July 1, 2026, puts a first paper in an unlimited civil case at $435 under Government Code 70611, with the same $435 due from each other party filing an answer. A motion requiring a hearing is $60, summary judgment is $500 under 70617(d), and the nonrefundable advance jury fee is $150 under Code of Civil Procedure 631(b). Court reporting is $30 for a proceeding under an hour and $573 beyond that. A complex designation adds $1,000 for the plaintiffs and $1,000 per defendant, capped at $18,000. These are case costs, and the agreement should state who advances them and what becomes of them if nothing is recovered.

### The paperwork attached to the dog

San Diego Humane Society sells licenses for the City of San Diego at $20 for one year, $35 for two and $50 for three when the dog is altered, and $60, $100 and $150 when it is not, a spread Food and Agricultural Code 30804.5 requires. A late renewal adds $25, and a change of ownership or a transfer from another jurisdiction $15 each. None of that decides a claim, but a lapsed license, a prior impoundment, or a dangerous dog determination the owner paid $45 to appeal all become records that speak to what the owner knew.

## svc_beach_and_trail_dog_attacks_lede

San Diego's shoreline, canyon trails and bay parks each carry a different dog rule, and the rule in force at the exact spot shapes what evidence exists. A leash-optional stretch of Fiesta Island and a leashed canyon path two miles inland are governed by separate provisions, enforced by different uniforms.

## svc_beach_and_trail_dog_attacks_body

<h2>The default in a City park is a leash of eight feet or less</h2>
<p>The City's Parks and Recreation guidance states that dogs must be kept on a leash not exceeding eight feet, including on trails and in canyons in all parks, and that in parks with designated off-leash areas the dog stays leashed until it is inside the posted boundary. The provision behind the open-space rules is Municipal Code section 63.0102(b)(2), cited on the City's open space canyons and parklands page alongside section 44.0304.1 on cleaning up after an animal. A photograph of the trailhead sign and the posted boundary is therefore a record of which rule applied, taken at a place where signs get replaced and boundaries get re-striped.</p>
<h2>Shoreline access turns on the hour and the location</h2>
<p>City guidance keeps dogs off any public beach, adjacent park or adjacent sidewalk between 9 a.m. and 6 p.m., with licensed dogs permitted at any time, leashed or not, at Fiesta Island in Mission Bay Park and at Dog Beach at the west end of the San Diego River flood control channel. Elsewhere in Mission Bay Park the closure runs 9 a.m. to 4 p.m. from October 31 to March 31 and 9 a.m. to 6 p.m. from April 1 to October 31, and outside those hours a leash is required. Fiesta Island itself is open 4 a.m. to 10 p.m., excludes dogs from the youth aquatic camp, and closes two buffer zones around California least tern nesting sites from April 15 through September 15. An incident at 8:40 a.m. and one at 9:20 a.m. on the same sand are not governed by the same sentence.</p>
<h2>Who writes the report depends on who patrols</h2>
<p>The City states that violations of leash rules and restricted hours may draw citations from Park Rangers, San Diego Police, San Diego Humane Society or Lifeguards. That matters after an attack because the responding uniform determines where the paperwork lands, and a beach incident can generate a lifeguard log, a police event number and a humane law enforcement report describing the same minutes. The 2024-2025 County Grand Jury leash report describes the City's animal services contract requirement for a Park Officer Program with no fewer than four humane law enforcement officers assigned to park patrol and at least two vehicles in use during patrols.</p>
<h2>Off-leash by design does not mean unrestrained by law</h2>
<p>A leash-optional area removes the leash requirement, not the duty. County Code section 62.669(b) permits an unleashed dog only in defined circumstances and conditions its voice-control exception on control effective enough to keep the animal from violating any law, and section 62.669.1 requires ordinary care to prevent a dog from biting or otherwise injuring a person engaged in a lawful act or interfering with a person or animal legally using the space. Civil Code section 3342 does not soften at Dog Beach either: a public place is exactly where the section applies most plainly.</p>
<h2>Trail and shoreline evidence disappears faster than most</h2>
<p>Sand moves, tides come in, and a canyon path looks different once the light changes. Useful records are the GPS point or trail marker rather than a park name, photographs of the sign and the boundary line, the leash if one existed, the direction both parties were traveling, and contact details for people who stopped. Both restraint sections also carve out dogs assisting or training to assist a law enforcement officer in the course of official duties, so identifying the handler matters as much as identifying the dog.</p>

Posted signs also change by season and by project, and the City publishes hour restrictions that differ between Mission Bay Park generally and Fiesta Island specifically. Photographing the sign that was actually posted on the day, rather than relying on a page read weeks later, is the only way that detail survives.</p>

A remote trail does not suspend that. ## svc_child_bite_injuries_lede

Children are bitten differently than adults. The clinical literature reports that a child's proportionately larger head and shorter stature put the head, face and neck in the animal's reach, and the law treats the resulting claim differently too, because the calendar that applies to an adult does not apply in the same way to a minor.

## svc_child_bite_injuries_body

<h2>Why the wound pattern is different</h2>
<p>The StatPearls review of animal bites, published through the National Center for Biotechnology Information, records that children are most commonly bitten on the head, face and neck, attributing that pattern to a proportionately larger head and shorter stature that place those areas within reach. That anatomy also explains why pediatric bites concentrate specialists early: facial wounds bring in plastic or oral-maxillofacial teams, and wounds near the eye bring in ophthalmology. A review of dog bite management in children published in Canadian Family Physician notes that facial bites are among the categories for which antibiotic prophylaxis is indicated, alongside moderate to severe wounds, punctures, and bites to the hand, foot or genitals.</p>
<h2>Closure decisions on a child's face</h2>
<p>The same pediatric review describes a retrospective series of 87 children with facial dog bite wounds that were primarily closed with sutures and treated with antibiotics, in which no patient developed a wound infection and none required scar revision. That is a narrow finding about a specific series rather than a rule for every wound, and the broader literature reaches a different conclusion for other locations: a review of mammalian bite injuries to the hand in the Journal of Hand and Microsurgery describes primary closure as acceptable in superficial head and neck wounds where blood supply is plentiful and appearance matters, while treating it as relatively contraindicated in the hand. The decision belongs to the treating clinician, and the record of it belongs in the file.</p>
<h2>The minority tolling rule</h2>
<p>Code of Civil Procedure section 352 handles claimants who were minors when the claim accrued. Subdivision (a) takes the period of that disability out of the count, which is why a child's claim is not lost while the child grows up, and it works alongside the two-year period in section 335.1. Subdivision (b) is the exception that surprises families, because it withholds subdivision (a) entirely where a claim against a public entity must be presented under the Government Code first. Government Code section 911.2 measures six months from accrual for presenting an injury claim to a public entity. A bite by an animal connected to a public agency therefore runs on a short clock even where the injured person is a child.</p>
<h2>San Diego reporting duties reach parents directly</h2>
<p>County Code section 62.615 requires the parents or guardians of a bitten minor child to notify the Department or the Health Officer as soon as practicable, imposes the same duty on a treating physician and on any other person who knows of a bite by a species subject to rabies, and deems a person who fails to report within 24 hours to have violated the section unless earlier reporting was impossible. The County's rabies program states that all animal bites are reportable to the local animal control provider and that the post-bite observation period for the animal is coordinated through animal services, so a parent's report is what starts the record that later confirms which animal was involved.</p>
<h2>What a family can preserve in the first week</h2>
<p>Photographs taken daily as bruising and swelling change, the discharge instructions and every follow-up note, the name of the person supervising the child, the location and whether entry was by invitation, the animal's identity and vaccination record, and the animal-control incident number. Where a bite happened at a home the child was visiting, the invitation matters legally: Civil Code section 3342 conditions its rule on the injured person being in a public place or lawfully in a private one, and defines lawful presence on the owner's property to include presence by express or implied invitation.</p>
<h2>Bites by a familiar dog</h2>
<p>Many pediatric bites involve an animal the family knows, which changes the practical picture more than the legal one. Civil Code section 3342 identifies the dog's owner as the responsible party and does not ask about the relationship between the households, and County Code section 62.615 places a reporting duty on the parents or guardians of a bitten minor regardless of who owned the animal. Reporting is also what creates the vaccination and observation record that protects the child medically. Families frequently delay a report to avoid conflict with a relative or a neighbor, and the practical cost of that delay lands on the child's own medical file.</p>
<h2>The part that is not medical</h2>
<p>Children's claims also involve procedures adults' claims do not, including court oversight of settlements involving minors, and this site's pricing page describes the published fee rules that apply to them. Nothing here is a prediction about any child's recovery or any claim's value. This page is a summary of published clinical literature and California and San Diego rules, offered as general information rather than legal advice, and a licensed attorney selected by the family is the right person to apply it.</p>

## svc_condo_and_hoa_liability_lede

A bite in a condominium courtyard, a shared stairwell or a gated pool deck involves a set of documents most detached-home cases never touch: recorded restrictions, an association with enforcement power, and a management company that may have been receiving complaints about the dog for months.

## svc_condo_and_hoa_liability_body

<h2>Recorded restrictions are enforceable law between owners</h2>
<p>Civil Code section 5975 provides that the covenants and restrictions in a recorded declaration run with the land, bind all owners, and operate as equitable servitudes unless unreasonable. The same section states that unless the declaration says otherwise, those servitudes may be enforced by any owner of a separate interest or by the association, or by both, and that in an action to enforce them the prevailing party is awarded reasonable attorney fees and costs. That is why a pet provision inside a declaration is a different sort of document from a house rule printed on a flyer: it is recorded, it binds successive owners, and enforcement power is expressly allocated.</p>
<h2>Enforcement power is not the same as a duty to an injured guest</h2>
<p>An association's authority to demand compliance answers a governance question. Whether anyone owed a duty to a person injured in a common area is a separate inquiry, and California resolves duty questions involving a tenant's dog through knowledge and control rather than through titles. In Uccello v. Laudenslayer, 44 Cal.App.3d 504, the Court of Appeal held that a duty of care arises where there is actual knowledge of the presence of the dangerous animal together with the right to remove it by retaking possession, added that constructive knowledge is not enough, and declined to impose any obligation to inspect premises looking for a tenant's dangerous animal. Those two components — knowledge in fact, and a present power to end the arrangement — are what a common-interest development file either documents or does not.</p>
<h2>The documents that carry the knowledge question</h2>
<p>In practice the evidence lives in ordinary association records: the recorded declaration and any pet addendum, board meeting minutes, violation letters, fine notices, hearing records, incident reports to management, work orders on gates and latches, correspondence from other residents, and any prior animal-control involvement at the address. Because section 5975 makes the association an enforcement authority, records showing it received complaints and what it did next describe both the knowledge and the response. Requests for those records follow the association's own procedures and the Davis-Stirling provisions on record inspection, which is a reason to make the request in writing and date it.</p>
<h2>Restraint rules apply inside the development too</h2>
<p>County Code section 62.669(c) governs a dog lawfully on private property and requires that it be leashed or tethered as allowed under Health and Safety Code section 122335, kept under direct and effective control by voice or an electronic containment system, or kept in a building or enclosure adequate to physically confine it while meeting humane standards. The subsection adds that an animal is not considered leashed if the leash is not in the hand of a person capable of controlling it, or if the person is not actually controlling the animal. Section 62.669.1 requires a dog's owner, custodian or other person having control to use ordinary care to prevent the animal from injuring a person engaged in a lawful act, from interfering with a person or animal legally using public or private property, and from damaging personal property lawfully present. A shared corridor is private property that many people are lawfully using at once, which is exactly the situation those subsections describe.</p>
<h2>Where the common area line usually gets argued</h2>
<p>Common-interest developments split responsibility between separate interests and common areas, and the split is defined by the declaration and the maps recorded with it. A latch on a courtyard gate, a fence between a patio and a walkway, and a stairwell landing may fall on different sides of that line at the same address, which is why the recorded documents get read before anyone assigns responsibility. Nothing on this page establishes where a particular development draws it, and no general description substitutes for reading the declaration for the specific property.</p>
<h2>Shared gates, latches and the maintenance record</h2>
<p>Bites in common-interest developments often trace back to hardware rather than to a decision: a courtyard gate that no longer latches, a broken fence panel between a patio and a walkway, a propped door on a dog-run enclosure. Work orders, vendor invoices, inspection walk sheets and board minutes discussing repairs establish when a condition was reported and what followed. County Code section 62.669(c) requires that a dog on private property be confined by a leash held by someone capable of controlling it, by direct voice or electronic control, or by an enclosure adequate to physically confine the animal. A gate that does not close is evidence about that requirement, and it gets repaired quickly after an incident.</p>
<h2>Insurance in a condominium is layered</h2>
<p>An association usually carries its own liability coverage while individual owners and renters carry policies of their own, and the California Department of Insurance describes the personal liability section of a residential policy as coverage that responds when the insured or a household resident is legally responsible for injury to others, with exclusions such as intentional acts spelled out in the policy itself. The Department's guide notes that a renters policy is generally written with a personal liability minimum of $100,000 and medical payments to others generally subject to a minimum of $1,000. Which layer answers for a bite in a shared area depends on the policies actually in force. This page is published general information about California statutes, County ordinances and insurance guidance rather than advice about a specific building, and an attorney of the reader's own choosing is the person to review the documents.</p>

## svc_dog_attack_wrongful_death_lede

A fatal dog attack raises questions that are procedural before they are anything else: who is entitled to bring an action, what damages the law recognizes after a death, and how quickly a claim has to be presented when a public agency is involved. Each of those answers sits in a statute.

## svc_dog_attack_wrongful_death_body

<h2>Who may bring the action</h2>
<p>Standing comes from Code of Civil Procedure section 377.60. Its first category covers a surviving spouse or domestic partner, children, and the issue of any child who died earlier; where nobody in that category exists, the right passes to those who would take the estate under the intestacy rules. The section separately allows certain dependents to sue, including a putative spouse and that person's children, stepchildren, parents, and the legal guardians of the decedent if the parents are deceased, and it addresses a minor who resided in the decedent's household for at least 180 days and was dependent on the decedent for one-half or more of support. Standing is therefore a statutory question with a defined answer, not a matter of who felt closest.</p>
<h2>What a survival action can recover, and the window that closed</h2>
<p>Where a personal representative or successor in interest sues, section 377.34(a) confines the recovery to loss or damage the decedent had already sustained or incurred by the time of death, and rules out pain, suffering and disfigurement by name. Subdivision (b) then carves out two situations: a case that received trial preference before 2022, and an action filed within the four calendar years running from 2022 through 2025. For an action filed after that closing date, the general rule in subdivision (a) governs again. This is one of the few places where the filing date changes the categories of recoverable damage rather than merely the right to file at all.</p>
<h2>The deadlines</h2>
<p>Code of Civil Procedure section 335.1 sets two years, and death caused by another party's wrongful act or neglect is among the categories it names. Where a public entity is involved, Government Code section 911.2 allows six months from accrual to present a claim founded on death or injury to a person. Where responsibility is asserted against the United States, title 28 of the United States Code, section 2675(a), requires presentation of the claim to the appropriate federal agency and a written final denial before an action may be filed, and section 2401(b) bars a claim not presented within two years of accrual or an action begun more than six months after mailed notice of final denial. Section 352(b) withholds the minority tolling allowance for claims that must first be presented to a public entity.</p>
<h2>How the animal's history enters the case</h2>
<p>Civil Code section 3342 does not require proof of the animal's prior viciousness or of the owner's knowledge of it, but the surrounding rules do treat history as significant. Food and Agricultural Code section 31603 defines a vicious dog to include one that inflicts severe injury on or kills a human being when unprovoked and acting aggressively, and section 31604 defines severe injury by its consequences: muscle tears, disfiguring lacerations, multiple sutures, or corrective or cosmetic surgery. County Code section 62.669.5 makes it unlawful for a person owning or having care, custody or control of a guard dog, a dangerous dog or a potentially dangerous animal to fail to exercise ordinary care resulting in injury, where the person knew or should have known of the animal's vicious or dangerous propensities. Whether such a record exists is answered by the animal control file.</p>
<h2>The administrative track continues in parallel</h2>
<p>County Code section 62.674 lets the Department issue a notice of intent to declare a dog dangerous, requires the notice to state each incident relied on and the right to contest, requires a written hearing request within ten days, and provides that a declaration remains in effect for the animal's lifetime, with impoundment permitted before the declaration where the Department states the reasons. The County's dangerous dog brochure states that the Department may seek destruction of an animal in certain cases and describes the hearing as usually scheduled within 30 days before an uninvolved senior manager. The 2023-2024 County Grand Jury dog bite report describes 9 to 18 such hearings a year across three fiscal years, with 78 to 90 percent resulting in a declaration.</p>
<h2>Two proceedings, two record sets</h2>
<p>A fatal attack can generate a criminal investigation, an administrative dangerous dog matter and a civil claim from the same set of facts, each with its own timetable and its own access rules. Records held by a law enforcement agency during an open investigation are often withheld while it remains open, which is a reason to make a written request early and renew it rather than to assume nothing exists. The animal control file, the responding agency's report, veterinary or licensing records for the animal, and any prior complaints at the address are the documents most likely to describe the animal's history.</p>
<h2>What a family can reasonably do first</h2>
<p>Obtain the animal control incident number, identify the responding agency, request the complete investigation file in writing, preserve the location and any physical evidence, and collect the names of witnesses before they disperse. Medical and coroner records will follow their own process. County Code section 62.615 requires notification of the Department or the Health Officer as soon as practicable by those who know of a bite by a species subject to rabies. Nothing on this page is legal advice, and no page can tell a family which statute applies to their loss; a licensed attorney of the family's own choosing is the person to make that determination.</p>

## svc_dog_on_dog_attacks_and_vet_bills_lede

When a loose dog attacks another dog, the statute most people have heard of does not apply, because Civil Code section 3342 is written about a person who is bitten. San Diego County ordinance, by contrast, does speak about animals and about property, and that is where an analysis of this kind usually starts.

## svc_dog_on_dog_attacks_and_vet_bills_body

<h2>The ordinance that names animals</h2>
<p>County Code section 62.669.1 requires a dog's owner or custodian, or another person having control of a dog, to exercise ordinary care to prevent the animal, while under that person's care, custody or control, from three things: attacking, biting or otherwise causing injury to a person engaged in a lawful act; interfering with a person or animal legally using public or private property; and damaging personal property that is lawfully on public property, or that is on private property with the permission of the owner or another person entitled to possess or use it. The second and third clauses are the ones that describe an incident between two animals. The section exempts a dog assisting or training to assist a law enforcement officer executing official duties.</p>
<h2>Restraint and being at large</h2>
<p>Section 62.669(a) requires an owner, custodian or person in control to prevent a dog from being at large, subject to narrow exceptions in subsections (b) and (d) for a dog under direct and effective voice control while assisting with legal hunting, legal herding, or activity on public property with written agency permission, and for a dog assisting or training to assist a law enforcement officer. On private property section 62.669(c) requires a leash or tether permitted under Health and Safety Code section 122335, direct and effective control by voice or electronic containment, or an adequate humane enclosure, and states that an animal is not leashed if the leash is not in the hand of a person capable of controlling it. County Code section 62.601(c), quoted in the 2024-2025 County Grand Jury leash report, defines at large as being unrestrained by a leash on private property open to the public or on public property unless a law or regulation expressly allows otherwise.</p>
<h2>Enforcement outcomes and what they cost the other owner</h2>
<p>The 2023-2024 County Grand Jury dog bite report records typical animal control fines of $100 for a first offense, $200 for a second in the same year, and $500 for subsequent offenses, and separately notes a $175 initial dog bite fine in Chula Vista. County Code section 62.669.1(b) requires the owner of an unaltered dog that bites a person engaged in a lawful act to pay the Department an altering deposit in addition to other applicable fees, and section 62.679 sets out redemption of an impounded animal, an altering deposit for an unaltered dog found at large, refund on proof of altering by a licensed veterinarian within 30 days, or by five months of age for a younger animal, and forfeiture of the deposit to the County otherwise, with forfeited deposits offsetting animal control costs. Section 62.679(d) allows the Department to require payment of microchip implantation cost when an unidentified animal found at large is redeemed.</p>
<h2>Veterinary records are the damages record</h2>
<p>The invoice is the least of it. Useful documentation includes the emergency clinic's intake and discharge notes, imaging, surgical reports, drain and bandage change schedules, medication lists, follow-up appointments, any referral to a specialty surgeon, and the pre-incident record showing the animal's baseline health. Where a working, service or therapy animal is involved, records of training and of the tasks the animal performed describe a loss that a general invoice does not. Photographs of the injured animal, of the location, and of any failed gate or fence complete the file.</p>
<h2>Two systems, two timelines</h2>
<p>An animal control citation and a civil dispute over veterinary costs proceed separately, and a citation is not a finding about who pays. Code of Civil Procedure section 335.1 sets two years as the period for the personal injury and death categories it names; a claim framed as damage to property follows a different provision, and which one applies depends on how the claim is framed, so no period for that is stated here. This page does not reproduce court jurisdictional limits or filing fee amounts because they were not confirmed from a primary source for this page.</p>
<h2>Identification is the first practical problem</h2>
<p>An animal that leaves the scene is often unidentified, and County Code section 62.679(d) reflects how frequently that happens by allowing the Department to charge microchip implantation cost when an unidentified animal found at large is redeemed. Useful steps at the scene are photographing the animal and any collar or tag, noting the direction it went, asking bystanders for a name or an address, and requesting that responding officers record a description. Licensing records held by the animal services provider for that jurisdiction can connect a description to an owner, which is one more reason to identify the correct provider before making a request.</p>
<h2>Where a person was also hurt</h2>
<p>Owners are frequently bitten while separating animals, and at that point Civil Code section 3342 is back in play on its own terms: it makes the dog's owner liable for damages suffered by a person the dog bit while in a public place or lawfully in a private place, regardless of the animal's former viciousness or the owner's knowledge of it. County Code section 62.615 requires a bitten person to notify the Department or the Health Officer as soon as practicable, and treats failure to report within 24 hours by someone who knows of a bite as a violation absent impossibility. Reporting the human injury and the animal injury in the same call keeps one record instead of two partial ones. This page is general information about published ordinances and statutes rather than legal advice, and an attorney of the reader's own choosing is the person to apply it.</p>

## svc_hand_and_arm_bite_injuries_lede

Hands take the worst of a defensive reaction, and the literature treats them as their own category of bite injury. The reasons are anatomical: a puncture over a knuckle can seed a joint space, and the structures under thin skin there are tendons, sheaths and nerves rather than muscle.

## svc_hand_and_arm_bite_injuries_body

<h2>Why the hand is treated as high risk</h2>
<p>The StatPearls animal bites review identifies bites to the hands and feet as higher risk for infection because of the small compartments in those regions and the lack of protective soft tissue, and reports that nearly a third of hand bites become infected. The review of mammalian bite injuries to the hand published in the Journal of Hand and Microsurgery records that dog bites account for 80 to 90 percent of mammalian bites and most commonly affect the hand, and that human and animal bites account for 30 to 40 percent of all hand infections. In a retrospective analysis of 525 wounds cited in that review, 14.9 percent of dog bite wounds became infected.</p>
<h2>What grows in a hand bite</h2>
<p>The same review describes bite wound cultures as typically polymicrobial, with a median of five isolates per wound and 56 percent of cultures growing both aerobic and anaerobic organisms. That mixed picture is the reason a single narrow-spectrum antibiotic is not the standard answer, and it is also why depth matters more than diameter: a small puncture that reaches a tendon sheath introduces that mixture into a space with limited blood supply and no room for swelling.</p>
<h2>Irrigation and the closure question</h2>
<p>The hand review sets a minimum of one liter of saline irrigation for a bite wound and states that primary closure is relatively contraindicated in hand injuries, citing a 25 percent infection rate reported after primary suture. It contrasts that with superficial head and neck wounds, where plentiful blood supply and appearance justify closure. The StatPearls review adds that infectious disease guidance advises leaving bite wounds open unless there is a cosmetic concern such as the face, that deep puncture wounds and wounds involving a joint space should not be closed, and that closure of an uncomplicated wound may be considered after cleaning and debridement within eight hours. A wound left open on purpose is a treatment decision, and describing it that way in the record prevents it from being read later as neglect.</p>
<h2>Prophylaxis carries an unusually clear number</h2>
<p>The Cochrane review referenced in the hand literature found a significant reduction in infection after prophylactic antibiotics in bite wounds, with a number needed to treat of four. The pediatric review in Canadian Family Physician reports that prophylaxis lowered the infection rate in hand bites from 28 percent to 2 percent, with the same number needed to treat, and identifies amoxicillin-clavulanate as the first-line agent, generally for three to five days. The StatPearls review likewise treats prophylaxis as warranted for bites that penetrate the epidermis or deeper and names amoxicillin-clavulanate as the preferred choice. Whether it is appropriate for a particular wound is a clinical judgment about a particular patient.</p>
<h2>Function is what gets measured later</h2>
<p>A hand injury is documented in movement rather than in photographs alone: grip and pinch strength, range at each joint, sensation over each digit, and the tasks a person can no longer perform at the pace they used to. Occupational therapy notes, splinting orders, work restrictions, and a written account of what a shift now requires carry more weight than a general statement that the hand hurts. For trades, clinical work, cooking, cosmetology, instrument playing and keyboard-heavy jobs across San Diego, the loss is specific, and specificity is what a file can preserve.</p>
<h2>Why a small wound gets a specialist</h2>
<p>The hand literature describes bite wounds over joints as capable of reaching a tendon sheath or joint space through an opening that looks trivial on the surface, which is why an emergency clinician may refer a puncture no larger than a pencil tip to a hand surgeon. The StatPearls review states that wounds involving a joint space should not be closed. Imaging may be ordered to look for a retained tooth fragment or a fracture. None of that follows from wound size, so a record that describes only the size of the opening leaves out the part of the injury that determined the treatment.</p>
<h2>The local paperwork still applies</h2>
<p>County Code section 62.615 requires a bitten person to notify the Department or the Health Officer as soon as practicable, with the section deeming a failure to report within 24 hours a violation unless earlier reporting was impossible, and County Code section 62.669.1 requires a dog's owner, custodian or other person in control to exercise ordinary care to prevent the animal from attacking, biting or otherwise causing injury to a person engaged in a lawful act. Civil Code section 3342 turns on a bite to a person in a public place or lawfully in a private one, without regard to the animal's history or the owner's knowledge of it. This page summarizes published clinical and legal sources as general information, not medical or legal advice, and questions about a specific hand belong to the treating clinician and to an attorney the reader chooses.</p>

## svc_homeowners_insurance_and_policy_limits_lede

A dog bite claim is usually paid, if it is paid at all, out of a residential insurance policy, and the limit written into that policy is a fixed number decided long before the incident. Understanding which section of the policy is in play — and what the published minimums look like — explains most of what follows.

## svc_homeowners_insurance_and_policy_limits_body

<h2>Two sections of a residential policy do different jobs</h2>
<p>The California Department of Insurance describes Coverage E, personal liability, as the section that responds when the named insured or a resident of the household is legally responsible for injury to others, states that it normally provides a defense and pays damages as the insurer deems appropriate, and warns that it will not protect the insured in every situation, giving an intentional act as an example. Coverage F, medical payments to others, is described separately as paying reasonable medical expenses for people accidentally injured on the insured's property, with the Department noting that it does not apply to the insured's own injuries or to household residents and is not a substitute for health insurance. Business activities are excluded. The Department repeats on both that the exclusions and specific language are found in the policy itself.</p>
<h2>Published minimums, and what they are not</h2>
<p>The Department's residential guide lists the coverage generally provided under a renters policy, including personal liability generally subject to a minimum of $100,000 and medical payments to others generally subject to a minimum of $1,000. Those are stated as the low end of what such a policy generally carries, not as legal requirements, and not as caps. The guide does not describe excess or umbrella policies, so no figure for those appears on this page. Nor does it state that a homeowner must carry liability coverage at all; in California, requirements to carry coverage generally come from lenders and contracts rather than from insurance statutes.</p>
<h2>One San Diego rule does attach an insurance figure</h2>
<p>The 2023-2024 San Diego County Grand Jury report on dog bites records that the owner of a dog declared dangerous is required to carry liability insurance of at least $100,000. The County's dangerous dog brochure lists liability insurance among the conditions that can be imposed on a declared animal, alongside specified fencing, a defined residence for the dog, restraint or muzzling requirements, microchip and photo identification, altering and special registration, and states that noncompliance can bring a misdemeanor citation, financial penalties, or surrender of the animal. That obligation belongs to the administrative designation process described in County Code section 62.674, not to the civil claim, and it exists whether or not any claim is ever made.</p>
<h2>Where the limit becomes the practical ceiling</h2>
<p>When a hospital bill, a surgical revision and a period out of work exceed the policy limit, the limit does not expand. What happens next depends on facts and documents this page cannot see: whether more than one policy responds, whether more than one person or entity had a duty, whether a lease or association document shifts responsibility, and whether the insured has other resources. Those are questions for an attorney reviewing actual policies. No page can tell a reader what a claim is worth, and any site that offers a number for a described injury is guessing.</p>
<h2>Renters and landlords carry separate policies</h2>
<p>A tenant's renters policy and a property owner's policy are different contracts with different insureds, and Navy Housing's guidance for privatized family housing at NAVBASE San Diego states that renters insurance is strongly encouraged, describing it as protection for belongings and for financial responsibility if accidents happen in the home. In a rental or a condominium the useful early step is identifying every policy that might apply — resident, property owner, association, and any business policy if the dog was working — because the identification exercise is factual and can be done before anyone argues about coverage.</p>
<h2>Breed and animal exclusions live in the policy</h2>
<p>Whether a residential policy responds to a particular dog is a question about that contract's own terms. The California Department of Insurance repeats, for both the personal liability and the medical payments sections, that the exclusions and specific language are found in the policy itself, and warns that liability coverage does not protect an insured in every situation. Some carriers write animal-related restrictions and some do not, and none of that can be determined from a coverage summary or a general guide. The declarations page, the policy form and any endorsements are the documents that answer it, and they are worth obtaining before anyone characterizes a claim as covered or excluded.</p>
<h2>What to keep, in the order it matters</h2>
<p>The declarations page for any policy already known, the claim number and adjuster contact, every written communication from the insurer, the medical bills as issued rather than as summarized, proof of missed work, and the animal-control incident number. County Code section 62.615 requires the bitten person, a parent or guardian of a bitten minor, a treating physician, and the animal's owner or custodian to report a bite by a species subject to rabies as soon as practicable, with failure to report within 24 hours deemed a violation unless earlier reporting was impossible. That report is often the only neutral record of what happened. This page describes published insurance guidance and San Diego rules as general information; it is not legal advice, not a coverage opinion, and not a substitute for having a licensed attorney of the reader's choosing read the policy.</p>

## svc_landlord_knowledge_and_liability_lede

California does not hold a property owner responsible for a tenant's dog simply because they own the building. The rule turns on two narrow findings: knowledge in fact of the specific animal's danger, and a present legal power to get the animal off the property.

## svc_landlord_knowledge_and_liability_body

<h2>The holding, in its own terms</h2>
<p>In Uccello v. Laudenslayer, 44 Cal.App.3d 504, the Court of Appeal stated that a duty of care arises when the landlord has actual knowledge of the presence of the dangerous animal and when the landlord has the right to remove the animal by retaking possession of the premises. The opinion is explicit that proof of knowledge of the dog and its dangerous propensities is required before any duty is imposed, and that because keeping pets is an ordinary part of life and exclusive possession normally rests with the tenant, actual knowledge rather than constructive knowledge is what the rule demands. It goes further and holds that a landlord has no duty to inspect the premises for the purpose of discovering a tenant's dangerous animal.</p>
<h2>The statute the court applied</h2>
<p>The theory in that case ran through Civil Code section 1714, whose opening sentence makes a person answerable both for willful acts and for injury caused to another by a want of ordinary care or skill in managing that person's own property or self. The Uccello opinion, having assumed knowledge of the animal's propensities and a power to end the tenancy on two weeks' notice, concluded that a cognizable theory of liability had been stated under that section. This is ordinary negligence law, which is why a claim against a property owner looks structurally different from a claim against the dog's owner under Civil Code section 3342, where the animal's history and the owner's knowledge of it are expressly removed from the analysis.</p>
<h2>What actual knowledge looks like on paper</h2>
<p>Because the standard is knowledge in fact, the evidence is documentary and testimonial rather than inferential: written complaints from other residents, emails or texts to a property manager, maintenance tickets mentioning the dog, notices to the tenant, a lease or pet addendum identifying the animal, prior animal-control involvement at the address, and any record of a bite before this one. A property owner who received a complaint and did nothing is in a different evidentiary position from one who never heard about the animal, and the difference is usually visible in a management file.</p>
<h2>The power to remove is a separate question</h2>
<p>The second half of the rule asks whether the owner could have done something about the dog. That turns on the lease term, whether the tenancy was periodic or for a fixed term, any pet provision and its enforcement mechanism, and the notice periods that applied. In a condominium the analysis adds recorded documents: Civil Code section 5975 makes the covenants and restrictions in a recorded declaration enforceable equitable servitudes binding all owners, enforceable by any owner of a separate interest or by the association unless the declaration says otherwise, with reasonable attorney fees and costs awarded to the prevailing party in an enforcement action. Enforcement authority and a duty to an injured guest are not the same thing, but the documents that establish authority are the documents a reader can actually obtain.</p>
<h2>San Diego restraint duties apply to the person in control</h2>
<p>County Code section 62.669(c) requires a dog's owner or custodian, or a person having control of a dog lawfully on private property, to keep the animal leashed or tethered as allowed under Health and Safety Code section 122335, under direct and effective control by voice or electronic containment, or inside a building or enclosure adequate to confine it while meeting humane standards, and adds that a leash not held by a person capable of controlling the animal does not count. Section 62.669.1 obliges the owner, custodian or other person having control to use ordinary care to prevent the dog from attacking, biting or otherwise injuring a person engaged in a lawful act, from interfering with a person or animal legally using public or private property, and from damaging personal property lawfully present. Those sections describe control, which is why the person exercising day-to-day control of an animal can matter as much as the person on the deed.</p>
<h2>Property management companies and on-site staff</h2>
<p>Knowledge in fact can reach an owner through the people who work for the owner. Resident managers, leasing agents, maintenance technicians and contracted management companies generate the written record that shows what was reported and when, and their notes tend to be contemporaneous in a way later statements are not. In privatized military housing the relevant private entity is defined by statute: title 10 of the United States Code, section 2871, describes the landlord as the contracting eligible entity along with its agents and later lessors, and states that the term does not include a federal government entity. Identifying the right entity early avoids sending a claim to a party with no responsibility for the property.</p>
<h2>Records requests are time sensitive</h2>
<p>Property management files, association minutes, gate and fence work orders, and prior incident reports all have retention schedules, and tenants move. A dated written request preserves a position better than a phone call. Deadlines run in parallel: Code of Civil Procedure section 335.1 allows two years for a personal injury action, and Government Code section 911.2 requires a personal injury claim against a public entity to be presented within six months of accrual. Nothing on this page is advice about a particular building or a particular owner; it is a description of a published appellate holding, statutes and ordinances, and an attorney the reader selects is the person who can apply it.</p>

## svc_military_housing_dog_bites_lede

San Diego County holds one of the largest concentrations of military families in the country, and a bite inside privatized base housing raises a question civilians rarely face: who the landlord actually is. Federal statute answers part of it directly, and the answer is not the Navy.

## svc_military_housing_dog_bites_body

<h2>Privatized housing has a private landlord by definition</h2>
<p>Family housing at San Diego installations is run under the Department of Defense privatization authority, and title 10 of the United States Code, section 2871, defines the landlord in that scheme as the eligible entity that contracted with the Secretary concerned, including any agent or later lessor that owns, manages or is otherwise responsible for a unit. The same paragraph states that the term does not include an entity of the federal government. Navy Housing states that NAVBASE San Diego has partnered with Liberty Military Housing, that the local Housing Service Center refers personnel to the partner company, and that the property management company assigns units and handles maintenance and resident matters. So the entity holding the lease obligations for a housing area is a company, and the entity holding the dog is usually a neighbor.</p>
<h2>The lease and its addenda are the governing documents</h2>
<p>Section 2890 requires each military department to attach the housing documents to every lease and each contract with a landlord to incorporate them. Among the enumerated tenant rights are a written lease with clearly defined terms including addenda and other landlord regulations covering occupancy and use of common areas, a community with well-maintained common areas and amenity spaces, a plain-language briefing before signing and 30 days after move-in that covers the dispute resolution process and the identity of the military tenant advocate, and the right to report housing problems without reprisal. Pet rules for a neighborhood live in those addenda and in the partner company's resident guidelines, which Navy Housing points residents to directly.</p>
<h2>Why the paperwork trail differs from an off-base rental</h2>
<p>A tenant in North Park keeps a lease and a management email thread. A family in base housing may also have a work order history, a housing office briefing record, and a chain-of-command paper trail, and the statute's anti-reprisal provision exists because reporting has consequences people worry about. Navy Housing also states plainly that renters insurance is strongly encouraged to protect belongings and financial responsibility if accidents happen in the home — which is a reminder that a resident's own policy, not the installation, is often the coverage in play when a resident's dog injures a visitor.</p>
<h2>Where a federal claim procedure does apply</h2>
<p>If injury is caused by a federal employee acting within the scope of employment, the route is the Federal Tort Claims Act, not an ordinary complaint. Title 28, section 2675(a), bars filing an action until the claim has been presented to the appropriate federal agency and finally denied in writing, and treats agency silence for six months as a denial at the claimant's option. Under section 2401(b) the written claim has to reach the agency inside two years of accrual, and suit has to follow within six months of a mailed final denial. Section 1346(b)(1) measures the United States' liability by the law of the place where the act or omission occurred. Civil Code section 3342 separately limits its own action against a governmental agency using a dog in military or police work in defined situations, preserves it for a person who was not a party to or suspected of the conduct that prompted the dog's use, and applies only where the agency has adopted a written policy on such use.</p>
<h2>What this page will not tell a reader</h2>
<p>Which agency holds the bite report for an on-base address, whether a particular housing area's ground lease shifts a duty, and whether a specific claim belongs in state court or an administrative claim package are questions that turn on documents this page cannot see. The County's rabies program states that all animal bites are reportable to the local animal control provider and that the monitoring period after a bite is coordinated by animal services and control agencies, and the County Epidemiology Unit takes reporting and post-exposure questions by phone. Asking the installation housing office and the County unit where the report went is faster than guessing.</p>
<h2>The advocate and the dispute process</h2>
<p>Section 2890 lists among tenant rights access to a military tenant advocate as described in section 2894, along with a plain-language briefing that covers the dispute resolution process both before signing a lease and again within 30 days of moving in. It also protects a tenant who reports a problem from reprisal, and enumerates forms that reprisal can take. Those provisions matter after an incident because they describe an internal path for raising a housing-related complaint that runs alongside, and independently of, any injury claim. Using one does not substitute for the other, and neither one pauses a deadline set by state or federal law.</p>
<h2>Keep the two questions separate</h2>
<p>One question is medical and public health: care for the wound, and the observation record for the animal. The other is contractual and factual: who owned the dog, whose unit it lived in, what the lease addendum said, who had been told about the animal before, and which policy of insurance sits behind whom. Written accurately, both files survive a permanent change of station. This is general information about published federal statutes and Navy housing guidance rather than advice about any incident, and a licensed attorney chosen by the reader should be the one to apply it.</p>

## svc_off_leash_violations_and_citations_lede

Off-leash enforcement in San Diego runs through two code systems at once, and a County Grand Jury report found that the one actually used in City parks is not the one the City's own contract calls for. That gap explains why paperwork from the same incident can look inconsistent.

## svc_off_leash_violations_and_citations_body

<h2>The two provisions in play</h2>
<p>The City's Municipal Code, at section 63.0102(c)(2) as quoted in the 2024-2025 County Grand Jury leash law report, makes it unlawful to bring, leave, turn loose or allow to go free any animal, fowl or bird of any kind in or upon any park. Separately, County Code section 62.669 requires an owner, custodian or person in control to prevent a dog from being at large, and the same Grand Jury report states that the City adopted that County section by reference. Both therefore apply within City parks. The City's open space parklands rules cite Municipal Code section 63.0102(b)(2) for the requirement that dogs be leashed, and section 44.0304.1 for cleaning up after an animal.</p>
<h2>The finding about which section gets cited</h2>
<p>The Grand Jury reported that leash violations in City parks are cited under County Code section 62.669 rather than under Municipal Code section 63.0102(c)(2), even though the animal field services contract requirement directs citation under the Municipal Code provision. The same report describes the contract's Park Officer Program, under which no fewer than four humane law enforcement officers are assigned to park patrol with at least two vehicles in use during patrols, and notes that the contract requires enforcement of leash laws and of areas where dogs are not allowed. For anyone gathering records after an incident, the practical consequence is that a citation may reference a County section for conduct in a City park.</p>
<h2>What a violation can carry</h2>
<p>Municipal Code section 12.0201 makes a violation of the code a misdemeanor unless otherwise specified, allows the City Attorney to charge it as an infraction, and sets a misdemeanor at a fine of up to $1,000 or up to six months in county jail or both. For an infraction it sets up to $250 on a first conviction and up to $500 on a second or subsequent conviction within one year of the first, and provides that each day a violation continues is a separate offense. Section 12.0202 provides for a civil penalty of up to $2,500 for each violation for each day it continues. The City's dog guidance states that fine amounts appear in the Superior Court bail schedule and are imposed at the court's discretion; no specific leash citation amount is reproduced here because the current published schedule reviewed for this page did not list one.</p>
<h2>The rules a dog owner is measured against</h2>
<p>The City requires a leash of no more than eight feet in parks, including trails and canyons, and requires that a dog stay leashed until inside the posted boundary of a designated off-leash area. Dogs are kept off any public beach and adjacent park or sidewalk between 9 a.m. and 6 p.m., with licensed dogs allowed at any hour, leashed or unleashed, at Fiesta Island in Mission Bay Park and at Dog Beach at the west end of the San Diego River flood control channel. Elsewhere in Mission Bay Park dogs are excluded 9 a.m. to 4 p.m. from October 31 to March 31 and 9 a.m. to 6 p.m. from April 1 to October 31, and must be leashed outside those hours. Fiesta Island operates 4 a.m. to 10 p.m., with buffer zones around least tern nesting sites closed April 15 through September 15.</p>
<h2>Who writes the citation</h2>
<p>City guidance names Park Rangers, San Diego Police, the San Diego Humane Society and Lifeguards as the parties that may cite leash and restricted-hour violations, and the Grand Jury describes the Humane Society as the City's contracted animal services provider. County Code section 62.601(c), quoted in that report, defines at large to include a dog unrestrained by a leash on public property or on private property open to the public unless a law expressly allows otherwise, and section 62.669(c) treats a leash as ineffective if it is not in the hand of a person capable of controlling the animal.</p>
<h2>Records to request after a citation issues</h2>
<p>Two files usually exist. The enforcement file held by the citing agency contains the officer's narrative, witness information and the code section charged, while the court record shows the disposition. Municipal Code section 12.0201 provides that each day a violation continues counts as a separate offense, so a course of conduct can generate more than one count. The City states that fine amounts appear in the Superior Court bail schedule and are imposed at the court's discretion. Requesting both files, in writing and with the incident number, produces a clearer picture than relying on a citation copy alone, which frequently records only the section number and a hearing date.</p>
<h2>A citation is not the civil case</h2>
<p>An infraction resolves a public enforcement question and does not decide who pays for an injury. Civil Code section 3342 makes the dog's owner liable for damages suffered by a person the dog bit in a public place or while lawfully in a private one, without regard to the animal's prior history, and it applies whether or not any officer wrote anything. Conversely, a dismissed citation does not extinguish that claim. County Code section 62.669.1 supplies a parallel ordinary-care duty covering injury to a person engaged in a lawful act, interference with a person or animal legally using property, and damage to personal property lawfully present. This page describes published City and County provisions as general information; it is not legal advice, and an attorney selected by the reader is the person to apply any of it to an actual citation or injury.</p>

## svc_plastic_surgery_and_scar_revision_lede

Scarring is where a bite injury stops being an acute medical event and becomes a long one. The published literature ties closure decisions to location, and California's animal statutes tie the definition of a severe injury to the very treatments a scar case involves.

## svc_plastic_surgery_and_scar_revision_body

<h2>The statutory definition built out of treatment</h2>
<p>Food and Agricultural Code section 31604 builds its definition of severe injury out of results and treatments: torn muscle, lacerations that disfigure, a wound needing more than one suture, or a wound needing corrective or cosmetic surgery. That definition matters beyond the medical chart: section 31603 defines a vicious dog to include one that, when unprovoked, inflicts severe injury on or kills a human being, and the San Diego County dangerous dog brochure sets its own threshold at a dog that has attacked or bitten a person engaged in lawful activity and caused substantial injury or death, or has bitten two such people within a 48-month period. So the treatments a scar case involves are the same treatments the statute uses to describe severity.</p>
<h2>Closure is a location decision</h2>
<p>The review of mammalian bite injuries to the hand published in the Journal of Hand and Microsurgery describes primary closure as acceptable in superficial head and neck wounds, where blood supply is plentiful and appearance matters, and as relatively contraindicated in the hand, citing a 25 percent infection rate after primary suture there. The StatPearls animal bites review states that infectious disease guidance advises leaving bite wounds open unless there is a cosmetic concern such as the face, that deep punctures and wounds involving a joint space should not be closed, and that closure of an uncomplicated wound may be considered after cleaning and debridement within eight hours. A wound deliberately left open is a decision with a reason, and the reason belongs in the record.</p>
<h2>What the pediatric literature reports about facial wounds</h2>
<p>The review of dog bite management in children in Canadian Family Physician describes a retrospective series of 87 children whose facial dog bite wounds were primarily closed with sutures and treated with antibiotics, in which none developed a wound infection and none required scar revision. It also lists facial bites among the wound types for which antibiotic prophylaxis is indicated, along with moderate to severe wounds, punctures, and bites to the hand, foot or genitals, naming amoxicillin-clavulanate as the first-line agent. That is a report about one series, not a guarantee about any wound.</p>
<h2>Documenting a scar over time</h2>
<p>A scar changes for a long period, and a file assembled at the end cannot show what a file assembled along the way would have. Practical documentation includes dated photographs taken in consistent light and from consistent angles, the operative report for every procedure, referrals to plastic or oral-maxillofacial surgery, notes on pressure garments, silicone products, laser or steroid treatment where ordered, and any recommendation that further procedures wait for maturation. Statements about visible scarring in a person's own words, dated, record something a clinical note does not.</p>
<h2>What is deliberately absent from this page</h2>
<p>No dollar figures for revision surgery, laser treatment or anesthesia appear here, because none were confirmed from a primary published source for this page. No timeline for when revision becomes appropriate appears either, for the same reason: that judgment belongs to a treating surgeon and varies by wound, age and location. Any site that publishes a settlement range for a facial scar is describing something it cannot know.</p>
<h2>Function, sensation and the parts that are not visible</h2>
<p>A scar record that lists only appearance misses half the injury. Numbness or altered sensation across a graft or flap, tightness that limits how far a joint moves, cold sensitivity, itching, and the psychological weight of a facial injury on a child are all documented in clinical notes when someone asks about them. The Food and Agricultural Code definition of severe injury reaches muscle tears as well as disfiguring lacerations, which is a reminder that depth and appearance are separate questions. Occupational therapy notes, school reports and a dated personal account fill gaps that surgical records were never meant to cover.</p>
<h2>The local record that fixes the starting point</h2>
<p>County Code section 62.615 requires the bitten person, a parent or guardian of a bitten minor, a treating physician, the animal's owner or custodian, and any other person who knows of a bite by a species subject to rabies to notify the Department or the Health Officer as soon as practicable, and deems failure to report within 24 hours a violation absent impossibility. The County's rabies program states that all animal bites are reportable to the local animal control provider. That report, and the emergency department record, are what later establish the original wound against which every subsequent photograph is compared. This page summarizes published clinical literature and statutory definitions as general information; it is neither medical nor legal advice, and both kinds of question belong to professionals the reader selects.</p>

## svc_potentially_dangerous_dog_designation_lede

San Diego County runs an administrative process that can label an animal and impose conditions on its owner for the rest of its life. California also keeps a separate court route. The two are not the same procedure, and confusing them wastes the ten-day window the County notice creates.

## svc_potentially_dangerous_dog_designation_body

<h2>What the County treats as a dangerous dog</h2>
<p>County Animal Services publishes the threshold in its dangerous dog brochure: a dog that has attacked or bitten two people engaged in lawful activity within a 48-month period, or that has attacked or bitten one such person and caused substantial injury or death. The brochure defines a bite as teeth causing a break in the skin no matter how small, treats an attack as any action putting a person in reasonable fear for their safety, and states that a dog declared vicious or dangerous in another jurisdiction can be designated here on that basis.</p>
<h2>How a case reaches a hearing</h2>
<p>The brochure describes a complaint to County Animal Services, an investigation by an animal control officer that includes witness statements and evidence collection, review by an animal control lieutenant who decides whether to send the matter to the Dangerous Dog Task Force, and a second review before the owner is notified in writing of the Department's intent. Code section 62.674 sets out what that notice must contain: the Department's authority, each incident relied on, the right to contest, the consequences, and the requirement that a written hearing request reach the Department within ten days of the notice date. Missing that deadline satisfies the hearing obligation and results in the declaration issuing anyway.</p>
<h2>The hearing itself</h2>
<p>A hearing is usually scheduled within 30 days, the hearing officer is a senior manager with no involvement in the case, witnesses must appear in person, testimony is sworn and recorded, and the proceeding does not follow courtroom evidence rules. The owner may pursue administrative review and ultimately the courts. Section 62.674(a)(2)(G) ties the outcome to the definition in section 62.602(l), and a declaration made under it stays with the dog for life. Section 62.674(b) allows impoundment before the declaration issues when the Department finds that step necessary to protect public health and safety, in which case the notice must explain why.</p>
<h2>Conditions the County can attach</h2>
<p>The brochure lists liability insurance, specified fencing, a defined residence for the animal, particular restraint or muzzling, microchip and photo identification, altering, and special registration, and states that the Department may seek destruction in certain cases. The 2023-2024 County Grand Jury dog bite report records the insurance figure as at least $100,000 and notes that a designation is lifted where there are no further incidents or violations within 36 months of the hearing. Noncompliance, per the brochure, can bring a misdemeanor citation, financial penalties, or surrender of the animal for impoundment and euthanasia.</p>
<h2>The state court route is a different animal</h2>
<p>Food and Agricultural Code section 31621 lets an animal control or law enforcement officer who finds probable cause petition the superior court of the county where the dog is kept for a determination that it is potentially dangerous or vicious, expressly allows a city or county to run an administrative procedure instead, and sets the hearing no less than five and no more than 10 working days after service on the owner. That proceeding is a limited civil case, is open to the public, allows incident reports and affidavits into evidence, provides no jury, and turns on a preponderance. Section 31603 defines a vicious dog as one that inflicts severe injury on or kills a person when unprovoked and acting aggressively, or a listed potentially dangerous dog that keeps offending after notice. Section 31604 defines severe injury by result: muscle tears, disfiguring lacerations, multiple sutures, or corrective or cosmetic surgery.</p>
<h2>What a declaration does not decide</h2>
<p>A declaration answers a public safety question about an animal's future. It does not determine who pays for an injury, it does not measure damages, and its absence does not mean an animal had no history. The County brochure notes that a Vicious or Dangerous declaration made in another jurisdiction can be adopted here, which is why a dog that moved into the county may carry a record from elsewhere. The 2023-2024 County Grand Jury separately observed that information about where declared dangerous dogs live is not readily available to the public, so a neighbor's inquiry and a party's records request are not the same exercise.</p>
<h2>Why the file matters to an injured person</h2>
<p>The Grand Jury found these hearings rare against an estimated half a million dogs in the county — 9 to 18 a year across three fiscal years of San Diego Humane Society reporting, with 78 to 90 percent ending in a declaration — and also found that information about where declared dangerous dogs live is not readily available to the public. So the administrative file, when one exists, may be the only documented record of what an animal did before. It is public safety paperwork, not a civil finding, and it decides nothing about damages. This page describes published procedure and is not legal advice; a licensed attorney of the reader's own choosing is the right person to ask what the file means for a specific claim.</p>

## svc_san_diego_county_animal_services_reports_lede

The bite report is the one document in a San Diego dog bite matter that is created by a neutral party close to the event. Getting it depends on knowing which of several agencies covers the address where the bite happened, because this county divides that work among many providers.

## svc_san_diego_county_animal_services_reports_body

<h2>Who covers which address</h2>
<p>The 2023-2024 San Diego County Grand Jury report on dog bites describes each incorporated city as responsible for its own animal control and lists how that is arranged: the San Diego Humane Society contracts to provide services for Cardiff, Carlsbad, Del Mar, Encinitas, Oceanside, Solana Beach, Vista, San Marcos, Escondido, Poway, La Mesa, El Cajon and the City of San Diego; Chula Vista Animal Care Facility serves Chula Vista, Imperial Beach and Lemon Grove; and Coronado and National City police departments handle animal control themselves, with County Animal Services covering the unincorporated areas. An attack on a trail that crosses a jurisdictional line can therefore produce a report held by an agency other than the one a reader would guess.</p>
<h2>What triggers a report</h2>
<p>County Code section 62.615 places the duty to notify on the bitten person, on the parents or guardians of a bitten minor, on a treating physician, on the animal's owner or custodian, and on any other person who knows of a bite by a species subject to rabies, and requires notification of the Department or the Health Officer as soon as practicable. Subdivision (d) deems a person with knowledge who fails to report within 24 hours to have violated the section unless it was impossible to report sooner. The Grand Jury report states that reports come from emergency rooms, from the person bitten, or from the dog's owner, and that most originate with emergency rooms.</p>
<h2>The scale, as the county measures it</h2>
<p>That report records roughly 2,500 dog bites reported annually by the County to the California Department of Public Health, notes that the true number is larger because not every city reports, and estimates about half a million dogs in the county. It cites 60,000 emergency room visits in California in 2022 arising from an encounter with a dog, 87 percent of them due to bites, notes that dog bites rank as the thirteenth leading cause of injuries in the United States, and reports that San Diego ranks fifth among cities nationally for dog bites to postal carriers. Of roughly 2,500 bites over 2020 through 2022, the report attributes 40 percent to dogs that were unvaccinated or whose vaccination status was unknown.</p>
<h2>What the file usually contains</h2>
<p>A bite investigation file can include the reporting source, the date and location, a description of the animal and its licensing and vaccination status, owner information, the officer's narrative, witness contacts, the quarantine or observation arrangement, and any citation issued. The Grand Jury report describes a ten-day home quarantine for low-risk dogs. Where a matter escalates, the file may also show a referral into the dangerous dog process, which County Code section 62.674 governs through a written notice of intent, a ten-day written hearing request window, and a declaration that remains in effect for the dog's lifetime.</p>
<h2>Public health has a parallel record</h2>
<p>The County Health and Human Services Agency states that all animal bites are reportable to the local animal control provider, that the post-bite monitoring period is coordinated by animal services and control agencies, that animals that die or are euthanized during that period must be tested, and that testing occurs at the County Public Health Laboratory. Its rabies post-exposure algorithm defines exposure as a bite or scratch breaking the skin or contact of saliva, cerebrospinal fluid or neural tissue with a mucous membrane or open wound, and lists an Epidemiology Unit business-hours number of 619-692-8499 and an after-hours consultation line of 858-565-5255. The County also publishes a local rabies data page covering 2021 through the current year, updated monthly.</p>
<h2>When the report is wrong or incomplete</h2>
<p>Reports are written quickly, often from a hospital notification rather than from an interview, and errors in a breed description, an address, a time or an owner's name are common. A written supplemental statement submitted to the investigating agency becomes part of the file without altering the original, which is preferable to arguing about the first version later. The 2023-2024 County Grand Jury noted that most bite reports originate with emergency rooms, and that the County's reported total understates reality because not every city reports, both of which are reasons a bitten person's own account adds information the file otherwise lacks.</p>
<h2>Asking for the record</h2>
<p>A request is more likely to be answered when it identifies the date, the time, the exact location, the animals and people involved, and any case or incident number already given. Where the responding uniform was a park ranger, a police officer, a lifeguard or a humane law enforcement officer, more than one record may exist for the same minutes; the City's park guidance names all four as possible enforcers of leash and restricted-area rules. Reports about third parties are subject to redaction and to the public records rules the holding agency applies, and this page cannot promise what any agency will release. It is published general information about how San Diego County records bites, not legal advice, and an attorney the reader chooses is the person to ask about using a record in a claim.</p>

## svc_statute_of_limitations_california_lede

Deadlines in a dog bite matter are not one deadline. Which calendar applies depends on who is responsible, how old the injured person is, and whether a public agency or the federal government is involved, and the shortest of those calendars is measured in months rather than years.

## svc_statute_of_limitations_california_body

<h2>The general personal injury period</h2>
<p>Code of Civil Procedure section 335.1 sets a two-year period. The categories it names are assault and battery, plus injury to an individual, plus death, each where the cause is another party's wrongful act or neglect. That is the period ordinarily associated with a bite claim against a private dog owner, a tenant, or a property owner. It runs from accrual, and negotiations with an insurer do not extend it. Nothing about an open claim file, a pending medical course, or a cooperative adjuster changes the date by itself.</p>
<h2>Minors are treated differently, with one large exception</h2>
<p>Section 352(a) addresses a claimant who has not yet reached majority when the claim accrues: the span of that disability drops out of the calculation, so the ordinary period does not consume a childhood. Subdivision (b) withdraws that treatment where the target is a public entity or public employee and a Government Claims Act presentation is required first. So the same child injured by a neighbor's dog and by an animal connected to a public agency faces two different calendars, and only one of them waits.</p>
<h2>Public entities run on six months</h2>
<p>Government Code section 911.2 gives six months from accrual to present a claim where the underlying cause of action is death, injury to a person, or injury to personal property, and a year for other causes of action. Presentation is a prerequisite step, not a courtesy, and the six-month figure is the one that most often catches families who assumed a two-year window applied to everything.</p>
<h2>Federal claims add a two-step sequence</h2>
<p>Where responsibility is asserted against the United States, title 28 of the United States Code, section 2675(a), bars an action until the claim has been presented to the appropriate federal agency and the agency has finally denied it in writing, and permits a claimant to treat the agency's failure to make final disposition within six months as a denial. Section 2401(b) is the outer bar: written presentation to the agency has to happen inside two years of accrual, and once the agency mails a final denial the suit has six months to start. Section 1346(b)(1) then measures liability by the law of the place where the act or omission occurred. In San Diego County, with its large military-connected population, that sequence is not an abstraction.</p>
<h2>Death cases have their own structure and a closed window</h2>
<p>Code of Civil Procedure section 377.60 sets out standing for a death claim. A spouse or domestic partner, children and the issue of a predeceased child come first, with intestate heirs next where that category is empty, and a separate list reaches certain dependents such as a putative spouse, stepchildren, parents, and guardians standing in for deceased parents. Section 377.34 keeps a survival action to what the decedent lost before dying and rules out pain, suffering and disfigurement. Subdivision (b) opens a narrow exception, and its two gates are a grant of trial preference predating 2022 and a filing made during the four calendar years that began in 2022 and ended with 2025. An action filed after that closing date falls back to the general rule in subdivision (a). That is a statutory window, not a policy the reader can negotiate.</p>
<h2>Local reporting deadlines are shorter than any of them</h2>
<p>County Code section 62.615 requires notification of the Department or the Health Officer as soon as practicable after a bite by a species subject to rabies and deems a person who knows of such a bite and fails to report within 24 hours to have violated the section unless earlier reporting was impossible. The County's rabies program states that all animal bites are reportable to the local animal control provider and that the observation period for the animal is coordinated by animal services. A missed report is not a missed lawsuit deadline, but it destroys the record that later proves which animal was involved.</p>
<h2>Why accrual is not always the date of the bite</h2>
<p>Every period on this page runs from accrual, and accrual is a legal conclusion rather than a calendar entry. Most bite claims accrue when the injury occurs, but disputes arise where responsibility of a second party surfaces later, where the identity of an owner or property holder is unknown, or where a condition is diagnosed after the fact. The safest practical assumption is the earliest plausible date, because a claim filed early is a claim that exists. Preserving the animal control incident number, the emergency department record and dated photographs makes the accrual question answerable with documents instead of recollection.</p>
<h2>Why a page cannot calculate a date</h2>
<p>Accrual, tolling, the identity of every potential defendant, and whether a claims-presentation requirement applies are fact questions, and getting any of them wrong moves the date. This page reproduces what published statutes say and nothing more. It is general information rather than legal advice, and a licensed attorney chosen by the reader is the only person who should be relied on to fix a deadline for a specific matter.</p>

## svc_strict_liability_in_california_lede

Strict liability is a rule about proof, not about blame. Under Civil Code section 3342 the question is whether a dog bit someone who was allowed to be where the bite happened — not whether the animal had a history. San Diego adds a second layer, because the restraint rules enforced here sit in the County code.

## svc_strict_liability_in_california_body

<h2>Three elements, and nothing about reputation</h2>
<p>Section 3342(a) attaches responsibility to the owner of the dog for damages suffered by a person the dog bit, provided the bitten person was in a public place or lawfully in a private one. The subdivision names the owner's own property as a private place that counts. It then removes the two things an owner usually wants to talk about: the animal's former viciousness, and whether the owner knew about it. Neither changes the rule. That is what separates this state from jurisdictions that require proof of a prior incident, and it is why the sentence "he has never done that before" answers a question the statute never asked.</p>
<h2>Lawful presence is the element that gets fought over</h2>
<p>The statute defines when someone is lawfully on the owner's private property, and the definition is narrower than most people assume. It reaches a person performing a duty imposed by the laws of this state or by federal postal regulations, and it reaches a person there on an invitation that may be express or implied. So a letter carrier at a Clairemont Mesa door, a meter reader inside a side gate, and a guest who was waved in all sit inside the definition, while someone who climbed a fence does not. Proof of the invitation — a text message, a work order, a delivery scan, a witness — is therefore evidence about an element, not background color.</p>
<h2>The word bitten is doing real work</h2>
<p>Section 3342 governs a bite. A dog that lunges and knocks a jogger onto Sunset Cliffs Boulevard, or one that trips a person on the boardwalk, has caused an injury the section does not describe, so the analysis moves to ordinary negligence. Civil Code section 1714 states the general principle that a person answers for injury caused by want of ordinary care in managing property, and the Court of Appeal applied that section to an injury involving a dog in Uccello v. Laudenslayer, 44 Cal.App.3d 504. Locally there is a third route: County Code section 62.669.1 obliges anyone with care, custody or control of a dog to use ordinary care to stop it from attacking, biting or otherwise injuring a person engaged in a lawful act.</p>
<h2>San Diego's restraint rules come from the County code</h2>
<p>The City of San Diego did not write its own leash statute. County Code section 62.669 requires an owner, custodian or person in control of a dog to keep the animal from being at large, with narrow carve-outs for lawful hunting, herding, and written permission on public property, and the 2024-2025 San Diego County Grand Jury report on leash enforcement records that the City adopted that section by reference. County Code section 62.601(c) supplies the definition that matters in practice: a dog is at large when unrestrained on public property, and a leash nobody is holding or controlling does not count as restraint. On private property, section 62.669(c) accepts a leash or tether permitted by Health and Safety Code section 122335, direct voice or electronic containment, or an enclosure that actually confines the animal.</p>
<h2>A violated ordinance is evidence, not the claim</h2>
<p>An officer's finding that a dog was at large does not itself establish the Civil Code claim, and the absence of a citation does not defeat one. The two systems run on different tracks, which is why the animal-control file and the civil timeline should be gathered separately. Under County Code section 62.674 the Department may issue notice of intent to declare a dog dangerous, must tell the owner that a hearing request has to arrive in writing within ten days, and a resulting declaration lasts the dog's lifetime. That paperwork can describe the animal in a way no later reconstruction will.</p>
<h2>Provocation, trespass and the arguments that actually fit</h2>
<p>Because subdivision (a) removes the animal's history from the inquiry, the contested ground shifts to the elements that remain. Was the injury a bite, or something else the dog did? Was the person in a public place, or on private property with permission that can be shown? Comparative fault principles still operate in California injury cases generally, and an owner may argue that conduct toward the animal contributed to what happened. Those are factual disputes about a specific afternoon, resolved by witnesses, photographs, the animal control narrative and medical timing rather than by a rule. Anyone reading a demand letter that skips those elements is reading advocacy, not analysis.</p>
<h2>Deadlines run whether or not anyone is negotiating</h2>
<p>Code of Civil Procedure section 335.1 allows two years, and the categories it covers include injury to a person and death caused by another party's wrongful act or neglect. Section 352(a) provides that time spent under the age of majority is not counted against a person entitled to bring such an action, and subdivision (b) withholds that allowance where a claim has to be presented to a public entity first. Government Code section 911.2 puts personal-injury claims against a public entity on a six-month presentation schedule. Those calendars are not interchangeable, and no page can apply them to a particular address, dog, or defendant. This is published information about how California and San Diego rules are written; a licensed attorney chosen by the reader is the person who applies them to facts.</p>

## svc_wound_infection_and_antibiotics_lede

Most bite wounds do not become infected, but the ones that do declare themselves within days, and the published ranges are wide enough to explain why clinicians treat prophylaxis as a judgment call rather than a formality. A bite is also a rabies exposure question, and in San Diego County that question has its own algorithm.

## svc_wound_infection_and_antibiotics_body

<h2>The reported infection rates and why they vary</h2>
<p>The StatPearls review of animal bites reports an infection incidence of 2 to 25 percent for dog bites, and cites a 2016 prospective study of more than 5,000 cases in which 14 percent of dog bites became infected compared with 58 percent of cat bites. It identifies puncture wounds and wounds larger than three centimeters as carrying higher risk, along with bites to the hands and feet. The pediatric review published in Canadian Family Physician gives a range of 1 to 30 percent. Those spreads reflect differences in wound type, site, time to care and patient factors rather than disagreement about the biology.</p>
<h2>Organisms, including one that behaves unusually</h2>
<p>Bite wound cultures are typically mixed. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention describes Capnocytophaga as bacteria commonly living in the mouths of dogs and cats that rarely spread to people but can cause serious illness, most often after a bite, with higher risk for people whose immune systems are weakened, including people without a spleen, people with alcohol use disorder, and people receiving certain cancer treatments. The agency lists sepsis, heart attack, kidney failure, gangrene and amputation of fingers, toes or limbs among possible complications and states that symptoms usually begin within 14 days of the bite. It also names Pasteurella and Bartonella among other organisms carried in animal mouths and advises washing a bite with soap and water immediately and calling a health care provider.</p>
<h2>Prophylaxis and first-line agents</h2>
<p>The StatPearls review treats prophylactic antibiotics as warranted for bite wounds that penetrate the epidermis or deeper, and identifies amoxicillin-clavulanate as the preferred agent. The pediatric review lists the situations in which prophylaxis is indicated — wounds undergoing primary closure, moderate to severe wounds, puncture wounds, facial bites, bites to the hand, foot or genitals, and immunocompromised or asplenic patients — and reports the same first-line agent for a course of roughly three to five days, along with a number needed to treat of four drawn from the reduction in hand bite infection from 28 percent to 2 percent. The hand literature reports a Cochrane review of eight randomized trials reaching the same number needed to treat.</p>
<h2>Closure changes the infection calculation</h2>
<p>Infectious disease guidance summarized in StatPearls advises leaving bite wounds open unless there is a cosmetic concern such as the face, and states that deep punctures and wounds involving a joint space should not be closed, while an uncomplicated wound may be closed after adequate cleaning and debridement within eight hours. The hand review reports a 25 percent infection rate after primary suture of hand bites and treats primary closure there as relatively contraindicated. Irrigation is the step both sources treat as non-negotiable, with the hand review setting a one-liter saline minimum.</p>
<h2>The rabies question is answered by a published algorithm</h2>
<p>The County of San Diego Health and Human Services Agency publishes a rabies post-exposure prophylaxis algorithm that defines an exposure as a bite or scratch that breaks the skin, or contact of saliva, cerebrospinal fluid or neural tissue with a mucous membrane or open wound, and states that contact with blood, urine or feces is not an exposure. The document notes that laboratory examination of a rabies-suspect or biting animal that has died falls under local health department authority, with bat testing results generally available within 24 hours. The County's rabies page adds that in San Diego bats test positive more often than any other animal, that more than 20 bat species live in the county, that wounds should be washed for 15 minutes, and that the Epidemiology Unit can be reached at 619-692-8499 during business hours. The CDC states that about 100,000 people in the United States are vaccinated after a possible exposure each year, that more than four million Americans report animal bites annually, and that post-exposure prophylaxis consists of wound washing, human rabies immune globulin and vaccine.</p>
<h2>Tetanus and the questions a clinician asks</h2>
<p>Bite care includes a tetanus history, an immune status review and questions about the animal, and the answers change the plan. The CDC advises washing a bite with soap and water immediately and contacting a health care provider, and identifies people without a spleen, people with alcohol use disorder and people on certain cancer treatments as being at higher risk from Capnocytophaga. The County's algorithm distinguishes true rabies exposure from contact that is not exposure, which is why a clinician asks whether saliva reached a mucous membrane or an open wound. Recording those answers when they are fresh keeps a later reader from having to guess why a particular treatment was chosen.</p>
<h2>Why the medical record ends up in a legal file</h2>
<p>Dates matter more than adjectives. The time of the bite, the time of first cleaning, the time antibiotics were started, the culture results, the return visits, and any change in the plan build a timeline that later reading cannot invent. County Code section 62.615 places the reporting duty on the bitten person, a treating physician, and the animal's owner, and deems a failure to report within 24 hours a violation absent impossibility. None of this is medical advice or legal advice, and both categories of question belong to a professional the reader selects: a treating clinician for the wound, and a licensed attorney for the claim.</p>

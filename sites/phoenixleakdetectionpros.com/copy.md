## symptom_1_title

The City Services bill jumped and nothing obvious is running

## symptom_1

Start with the meter, not the bill alone. Turn off faucets, washing machines, ice makers, pool auto-fill, irrigation, and anything else that uses water. Then look at the leak indicator or the small sweep hand on the meter. If it continues moving after a short quiet period, water is passing somewhere on the customer side of the meter. That could be a toilet flapper, a softener, a pool fill line, irrigation, a service line in the yard, or a supply pipe under the slab. The meter proves water is moving; it does not identify the pipe.

Phoenix Water Services specifically tells customers that an unexplained month-to-month increase can signal a leak. Keep the bills and the repair paperwork. The city’s Sewer Fee Review can consider broken-plumbing and irrigation spikes for the January through March billing period, but it has a seasonal calculation and filing deadline. For the 2026 process, requests more than 60 days after the July bill date are not eligible. That is useful to know before a high bill is filed away and forgotten, but it is not a reason to postpone locating the leak.

At the visit, the technician compares meter movement, system pressure, fixture behavior, and the property’s plumbing layout. A shutoff test can separate the house plumbing from irrigation or pool equipment. Acoustic equipment can listen for a pressurized leak, while pressure testing can show whether a closed section is losing water. A credible finding ties the test result to a location and explains what still needs confirmation. Do not let a bill by itself turn into a promise that the leak is under the floor. In Phoenix, the bill is often the first clue, not the diagnosis.

## symptom_2_title

A warm or damp spot is showing through the slab floor

## symptom_2

A warm tile strip, a patch of floor that feels damp, unexplained buckling, or a persistent musty odor deserves quick attention in a Phoenix house. Many local homes are slab-on-grade. The supply pipes may run below or through concrete instead of under a crawlspace, so a pressurized hot-water leak may announce itself as warmth in a hallway or bedroom floor long before water is visible. A cold-water leak can leave a damp joint line, lift flooring, or quietly wet baseboards.

First rule out easier explanations. A refrigerator line, a washing-machine connection, a shower pan, a failed caulk joint, a roof or window intrusion after rain, and condensation from a cooling line can all put water where it does not belong. Do not drill through tile or chip the slab to investigate. Shut off the water if the area is actively spreading, move belongings away from the spot, and avoid outlets or cords in the wet area. If water is close to electrical equipment, leave the area and arrange urgent help.

Detection for a suspected slab leak is a sequence, not a magic wand. The technician may isolate the hot and cold sides, test pressure, listen across the slab, use thermal imaging where heat is escaping, and confirm the likely point against pipe routing and meter behavior. Copper beneath concrete can develop small failures over time, and Phoenix’s mineral-heavy water is a reason to look closely at older copper and scale history. It does not mean every warm spot is a pinhole. The useful outcome is a documented location range and a repair plan: access the pipe locally, reroute a section, or compare options after the actual line and damage are known.

## symptom_3_title

The water pressure is weaker than it used to be

## symptom_3

Weak pressure throughout the house is different from a slow showerhead. If only one fixture has changed, begin there: clean the aerator, check the shower cartridge, and make sure a nearby stop valve is fully open. When several fixtures lose pressure at the same time, especially with the water meter showing movement while everything is off, the cause may be a leak, a failing pressure regulator, a partially closed main valve, a filtration or softener restriction, or a problem at the service line. The testing has to separate those possibilities before repair work is proposed.

Phoenix’s current water-quality report lists total hardness at 10 to 17.6 grains per gallon. Minerals can leave scale on faucet aerators, valves, water-heater connections, and older plumbing. That makes a fixture-level restriction common enough to inspect, but scale and a hidden leak are not interchangeable explanations. A technician checks static and flowing pressure, uses shutoffs to isolate sections, looks at the meter, and compares hot against cold. That process is more useful than turning up a regulator blindly, which can hide a leak or put stress on aging parts.

Ground conditions can matter to buried pipe, too. The Arizona Geological Survey documents land subsidence and earth fissures associated with groundwater withdrawal in parts of Maricopa County. That does not mean a pressure loss at a Phoenix address is an earth fissure, and it is not something a leak technician can diagnose from a kitchen tap. It is a reason not to assume buried lines stay perfectly supported forever. Where testing points outside the house, the technician follows the service or irrigation route and checks for soft ground, isolated wet vegetation, and a pressure loss on the appropriate line. The repair decision follows the confirmed section, not a guess based on one low-flow faucet.

## symptom_4_title

The pool level keeps dropping faster than evaporation explains

## symptom_4

Phoenix heat can make pool water disappear quickly, but a pool that needs frequent make-up water should be tested before the deck, plumbing, or shell is blamed. Mark the waterline on the tile or skimmer and compare it with a bucket test: set a bucket of pool water on a step so its water level matches the pool’s, then compare both levels after a day with the pump conditions noted. If the pool falls more than the bucket, there may be a leak. Do not use that comparison as a final answer; wind, splash-out, a backwash setting, and an automatic fill valve can distort it.

The next clue is whether the loss changes with the equipment running. A bigger drop while the pump is on can point toward pressure-side plumbing after the pump. A loss that continues with equipment off can involve a suction line, return line, skimmer, light niche, shell fitting, or the vessel itself. Wet soil near equipment, air entering the pump basket, bubbles from returns, and a low filter-pressure pattern each narrow the search but do not prove the location.

A proper pool leak visit checks the equipment pad, auto-fill operation, fittings, and plumbing response before any deck is disturbed. Depending on the symptom, testing may include pressure isolation, dye testing at fittings, listening equipment, and line tracing. The goal is to distinguish a plumbing leak from a structural or operational issue and to locate the suspect path without opening a large area on speculation. If pool water has been disappearing alongside a higher household bill, tell the technician that both are happening; an auto-fill can turn a pool leak into a City Services bill problem. Shut off the auto-fill only if doing so will not leave equipment running dry, and do not run the pump without the required water level.

## qa_1_question

Can Phoenix adjust a water bill after a plumbing leak?

## qa_1_answer

Phoenix’s published program is a Sewer Fee Review, not a blanket promise to erase a water bill. The city says it may adjust the sewer-fee calculation when higher-than-usual water use is caused by broken plumbing or outdoor irrigation equipment. Its sewer calculation is based on January, February, and March use and takes effect each July. For the 2026 review, the city says a request received more than 60 days after the July bill date is ineligible, and it does not adjust prior years. Keep the bill, a repair invoice or receipt, dates, and any test documentation. Read the city’s current instructions before filing because the eligible period and paperwork matter. Locating and repairing the leak promptly is the practical first step; a review does not stop additional water loss.

## qa_2_question

Does a warm Phoenix floor always mean a slab leak?

## qa_2_answer

No. A warm floor is a strong reason to test, particularly when it follows hot-water use or appears in a narrow band across tile, but it is not proof by itself. Sun through glass, a heating source, a nearby appliance connection, moisture below flooring, and other building conditions can change how a floor feels. The technician needs to pair the temperature clue with pressure testing, meter behavior, fixture isolation, and listening or thermal tools. In a slab-on-grade home, that sequence matters because opening the wrong section of concrete creates an avoidable repair. If the area is getting wetter, flooring is lifting, or water is close to an outlet, treat it as urgent and keep people away from the affected electrical area.

## qa_3_question

Can a leak be found without breaking the slab or pool deck?

## qa_3_answer

Often, detection can narrow the location without opening the surface first. Meter checks, isolation tests, pressure tests, acoustic listening, thermal imaging, tracer methods, and pool-line testing are used to establish which system is losing water and where the evidence is strongest. Exact accuracy depends on pipe depth, material, access, noise, soil, and whether the line is actively pressurized. Sometimes a small access opening is still the most sensible way to confirm and repair the pipe, especially after testing has reduced the target area. The important distinction is between opening a selected point after evidence supports it and opening broad sections because nobody tested first. The technician should explain the confidence level, the next confirmation step, and the repair choices before destructive work begins.

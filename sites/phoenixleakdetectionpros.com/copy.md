## pricing_body

### Two invoices, not one

Finding a leak and fixing it are separate jobs with separate prices, and this table keeps them apart deliberately. A locate is an hour or two of listening, tracer gas, pressure isolation and thermal imaging, ending with a mark on the floor and a photograph. The repair is whatever the mark requires. A few valley companies credit the locate against the repair when they do both, and one serving Phoenix and Scottsdale says so in writing. Most flat rate locators credit nothing, and their published figures sit lower for that reason. Any repair number produced before the locate is the average of every possible repair, which is another way of saying it is not about your house.

### The city bills you for water you never used

This is the part that changes the math on waiting. Phoenix City Code section 37-27 makes each customer responsible for all leaks downstream of the point of service delivery, and for full payment of charges for any water lost to them. There is no leak forgiveness program to apply for. Water in the June through September window runs $6.13 per hundred cubic feet, which is 748 gallons, on top of the monthly meter charge. A line weeping under a slab in July compounds weekly rather than rounding to nothing.

The one adjustment the city publishes is the sewer fee review, and it runs on a fixed calendar. Sewer charges are recalculated each July from average January through March water use, and a request landing more than 60 days after that July bill is ineligible. A winter leak therefore shapes a full year of sewer billing, and the window to contest it closes fast.

### Copper, caliche and 1980s slabs

Valley water is hard, and hard water moving fast through soft copper produces pinholes from the inside out. Add a recirculating hot water loop, decades of thermal cycling, and soils that shift and fissure across Maricopa County, and failures cluster predictably: hot lines first, elbows and transitions next, the loop return after that. A leak found in a hot line raises a fair question about the rest of that line. A house on its second slab leak is also a different conversation from a house on its first, which is why the reroute and repipe rows exist above.

### Reading a leak estimate line by line

A usable estimate names the detection method, states whether the detection fee is credited, and separates the spot repair from the reroute so both can be compared. It includes the concrete cut and the patch, says who repairs the drywall and whether texture and paint are included, and states whether a permit is being pulled. The city's fee schedule sets a $195 minimum permit fee on residential work and charges $195 for every re-inspection after the first, so that line is real money on a small job. It also says what happens if opening the slab reveals a second failure in the same run, the most common reason a job lands at the top of its band.

### The reroute against repipe pitch

Both are legitimate. A reroute suits aging pipe, poor access, or a second failure in one line. A repipe suits a system failing along its length. What should raise an eyebrow is a whole house repipe proposed off one located leak with no evidence of wall thinning and no history of prior failures. Published valley repipe prices run from roughly $4,000 to $24,000 depending on square footage and whether the material is PEX or copper, so the gap between this line and the whole house is the largest number anyone will put in front of you. Ask for the evidence, and for the spot repair figure as a comparison.

### What the online calculator never includes

Cutting and patching concrete. Flooring that cannot be matched once it comes up. Drywall texture and paint. The pool equipment loop and the irrigation manifold, which are frequently the actual source. The permit and its re-inspection. And the water that already ran, which under section 37-27 is on the account holder no matter who finds the leak.

## svc_water_leak_detection_lede

Water is leaving the system somewhere between the meter box and the last fixture, and the only evidence so far is a number on a bill. What follows is how that loss gets narrowed before concrete, drywall or landscaping is opened.

## svc_water_leak_detection_body

### The meter is the instrument and the bill is only the alarm

Phoenix Water Services treats an unaccounted rise in consumption units, month to month or year to year, as a reason to start looking. Its outdoor-leak guidance says plainly that losses in a yard are the ones nobody notices. Run the quiet test properly. Close every fixture, shut the ice maker supply, switch off the irrigation controller, and disable any pool auto-fill. Read the meter, wait fifteen minutes, read it again. A change proves water is moving somewhere private. Which pipe is moving it is a separate question, and answering that question is the entire job.

### Where the utility's pipe ends and yours begins

Section 37-1 of the city code draws that boundary at one fitting. The point of service delivery is the terminal end of the service connection, and where a meter sits at that end, the boundary is the downstream side of the meter, meaning your side. Everything from there to the farthest bathroom is private plumbing. That includes the buried run crossing the front yard, the piece homeowners most often assume the utility maintains. It does not. A locate that begins at the meter box settles the ownership argument before it can start.

### Every gallon already gone is billed to the account

Section 37-27 is short and unsentimental. A customer is responsible for all leaks downstream of that point of delivery, and for full payment of charges for water lost to them. No forgiveness program exists to apply for, whatever a neighbor may have told you. The same section requires pipes, valves, sprinklers and fixtures to be kept in good repair, and permits the department to end service after ten days of written notice when they are not. The published rate schedule sharpens it: water inside the city costs $6.13 per hundred cubic feet from June through September, and a hundred cubic feet is 748 gallons.

### Isolation comes before listening

Competent work narrows the field mechanically before a microphone appears. Closing the house main shows whether the meter stops; if it keeps creeping, the loss lies outside, in the service run or an irrigation manifold. Separating hot from cold at the heater makes a warm-line failure declare itself. A gauge on a hose bib gives static pressure and shows whether that pressure holds with a section valved off. Acoustic listening, correlation between two sensor positions, tracer gas in a drained line and thermal imaging all earn their keep afterward, because by then they are searching a short list rather than a whole property.

### Two local conditions shorten a supply line

Mineral load is the first. The city's quality report puts total hardness between 172 and 302 parts per million, ten to roughly seventeen and a half grains per gallon, enough to crust valve seats, heater connections and aerators and to disguise a slow seep behind scale. Unstable ground is the second. USGS work in the West Salt River Valley documents aquifer compaction and land subsidence after decades of pumping, with as much as eighteen feet of settlement recorded east of the White Tank Mountains by 1995. Neither fact diagnoses a leak on its own. Together they explain why buried pipe that behaved for twenty years stops behaving.

### If you rent rather than own

Arizona statute puts this duty on the owner. Section 33-1324 requires a landlord to keep plumbing in good and safe working order and to supply running water at all times. Where the fix is small, and the statute's line is three hundred dollars or half of one month's rent, whichever is greater, Section 33-1363 lets a tenant give written notice and wait ten days. After that the tenant may have the work done by a licensed contractor, then deduct the actual reasonable cost once an itemized statement and a waiver of lien reach the landlord. Save the meter reading and the bill, because both become evidence.

### What the visit settles, and what moves the figure

A finished locate leaves you a marked position, a photograph, the method that found it, and a plain account of what remains uncertain. Access drives the number far more than pipe does. A fitting inside a laundry wall, a run beneath tile, a service line under a driveway apron and a valve manifold set in caliche are four unrelated amounts of labor. Permit scope belongs in the estimate as well, since the city requires a permit to move or add fixtures while a like-for-like swap needs none, and state law withdraws its small-job licensing exemption the moment a permit is required.

## svc_slab_leak_detection_lede

A slab leak is a pressurized line failing inside or beneath the concrete, with the floor as the only witness. What shapes every decision afterward is what that concrete happens to be holding.

## svc_slab_leak_detection_body

### Reading the floor correctly

The classic tell is a hot spot, a patch or narrow band of tile that stays warm while the heater idles, marking a hot-water line losing pressure below. Cold-side failures are quieter. A grout line darkens, a baseboard never dries, wood flooring cups in one direction, a closet turns musty. A meter dial that refuses to settle with the house shut down confirms an active loss. No single sign locates the pipe. Water spreading beneath concrete follows the path of least resistance and surfaces where it can, occasionally a full room from the break.

### The post-tensioned slab decides the whole job

The city wrote this into its building code. The 2024 Phoenix Building Construction Code, adopted by ordinance in June 2025 and effective that August, requires every post-tensioned slab on ground to be permanently stamped or marked in a conspicuous location. Entrance porches, the slab at a garage door and patio slabs are the examples the code itself offers. Its stated reasoning is direct. Many structures have been and continue to be built this way, and a tendon cut during the life of a building can seriously injure people nearby. The marking exists so a contractor knows to identify tendon positions before cutting or drilling. Go find that stamp before anyone quotes concrete work.

### Tensioned steel is nothing like rebar

An unbonded tendon is a greased strand stressed against anchors after the concrete cures, and it stays loaded for as long as the house stands. Sawing or coring one releases that stored energy at once. It also removes compression the slab was designed to rely on, which is a structural change and squarely outside a plumber's scope. Cutting one is an engineering decision, made by whoever will carry responsibility for the foundation, informed by radar mapping of tendon positions and by the original slab drawings. The order that works is simple: locate the leak precisely, then find the steel, then decide.

### The soil those slabs were designed around

Code section 1803.5.13 shows what the designers were worried about. A geotechnical investigation is required for structural post-tensioned slabs on ground, and the drawings must carry soil parameters, bearing value and depth, coefficient of subgrade friction, subgrade modulus, plus the expansive-soil values written as em and ym. Those last two exist because soils here change volume as moisture changes. Add caliche, which Arizona Cooperative Extension describes as soil particles cemented by calcium carbonate, appearing as loose lumps or as a solid layer from a few inches to several feet thick and restricting drainage. Copper bedded in subgrade that both swells and traps water, cycling hot several times a day, works against loads nobody designed it to carry.

### What not to do while you wait

Never chip an exploratory hole where the wet shows, because the wet is downstream of the break. Never rent a rotary hammer for a floor you have not proved is free of tendons. Avoid running hot water to watch whether the warm patch grows, since every gallon travels under the floor and into insulation and cabinet bases. Water approaching an outlet, a floor register or the panel is an urgent hazard, so stay clear of it. Close the main if you can reach it from dry ground, and stop before forcing a corroded stop valve hard enough to snap the stem.

### Reroute against penetration

Two honest options exist and they are not interchangeable. Penetration opens a window in the floor above the located point, replaces the failed section, tests it, then patches concrete and tries to match a floor finish that may no longer be manufactured. A reroute abandons the buried run and rebuilds it overhead or through walls, never touching tensioned steel, and lifts the pipe out of the ground that failed it. One failure in otherwise sound pipe can justify a spot repair. A second failure in the same line, a run under irreplaceable tile, or a tendon layout that leaves no clean window all argue for rerouting. Watch for the opposite pitch as well: a whole-house repipe proposed from a single located leak, with no evidence of thinning anywhere else and no record of earlier failures.

### The sequence, and the shape of the cost

Isolate hot from cold, pressure-test each side independently with a gauge and watch for decay, listen along the floor with ground microphones, correlate two sensor positions, then push tracer gas into the drained section when acoustics stall on carpet or thick tile. Thermal imaging confirms the picture under a workable temperature differential. The point gets marked with measured offsets from two walls and photographed. Radar mapping and slab drawings follow if the answer is a penetration. Four things move the total: whether the pipe can be reached without lifting finished floor, whether one fitting or a rebuilt run is the answer, whether an engineer and a scan are needed first, and who restores tile, texture and paint afterward.

## svc_underground_leak_detection_lede

Between the meter box and the house wall, and onward through valves and manifolds feeding the landscape, water can run for months into ground that shows nothing at the surface. The city itself calls yard losses the ones that go undetected.

## svc_underground_leak_detection_body

### What a buried line does leave behind

Evidence outdoors is indirect and easy to misread. A strip of unusually green plants in August, soil that stays soft after a week without watering, a sinking paver, water surfacing in a gutter near the curb, or a meter that creeps with the house main closed all point outside the building. Pressure that sags at every indoor fixture belongs on the list too, because a service line losing water bleeds pressure and usage together. Municipal guidance also warns that irrigation losses can damage plants, patios and building slabs, which is why a wet yard is not a cosmetic problem.

### Caliche sends the water sideways

Cemented soil is the reason surface evidence lies. Arizona Cooperative Extension describes caliche as soil particles bound together by calcium carbonate, forming at or below the surface as loose concretions or as a solid layer measured in inches or in feet, and it notes that these layers restrict drainage. Water escaping a buried pipe reaches such a layer, stops soaking downward, and travels along it until something lets it up. The green patch can therefore sit many feet from the split. Excavation cost follows the same geology, since a trench that opens in an hour in sandy fill can take a full day through cemented ground.

### Ground that moves under a pipe

Long-term change matters as much as the trench. USGS work on the West Salt River Valley basin-fill aquifer records water-level declines exceeding fifty feet across much of the area, and in places more than three hundred, followed by aquifer compaction and land subsidence. East of the White Tank Mountains and north of Interstate 10, the surface had dropped as much as eighteen feet by 1995. The same study maps thick silt and clay lenses, some more than eighty percent fine material, near Goodyear and Glendale. The Arizona Geological Survey separately links withdrawal to earth fissures in Maricopa County and notes they can damage utility lines. A rigid pipe crossing ground that settles unevenly is being asked to act as a beam.

### Irrigation and the monsoon calendar

Most buried failures in older neighborhoods start at the landscape system rather than the potable service. Valve bodies crack, solvent joints on thin-wall pipe fail, a rotor gets clipped by a mower, and a drip line splits where a root pushed it. The National Weather Service office here defines the season officially as June 15 through September 30, and a storm cell dumping an inch in twenty minutes both saturates ground around those fittings and washes soil out from beneath them. Arcadia's mature citrus and flood-irrigated lots, Encanto-Palmcroft's older lawn systems and the deep-rooted trees along Willo's side streets all put big roots next to small pipe. Mention it if the trouble began within a day of a storm.

### Locating through soil rather than concrete

Outdoor work uses the same instruments in a different order. The line is charged and its route traced electronically so the search follows actual pipe instead of a guess drawn from the meter to the wall. Ground microphones listen at intervals along that route, then a correlator compares two sensor positions to place the sound between them. Where soil damps the noise, as dry cemented ground reliably does, the section is drained and tracer gas introduced so a surface probe can find where it escapes. Static pressure tests on isolated sections tell you whether the potable service or the irrigation zone is the one bleeding down. Every conclusion should end with a mark, a depth estimate and a distance from a fixed feature.

### Whose pipe is about to be dug up

Two boundaries decide who pays. City code puts the point of service delivery at the downstream side of the meter, so the yard run belongs to the property. Anything wrong on the utility side of that fitting is the department's to answer for, and a leak visible in the street or at the meter itself should be reported before a shovel goes anywhere. The other boundary is legal: state licensing law exempts only casual or minor work under one thousand dollars, and that exemption disappears entirely once the job needs a local building permit. Ask which side of both lines the proposed work sits on.

### What it costs to reach the pipe

Reaching the failure is the expense, not the coupling. Depth, surface material, cemented layers, tree roots, and whether the route crosses a driveway apron or a decorative hardscape move an excavation figure far more than pipe or fittings do. Rerouting a short service line through softer ground occasionally beats repairing the original in place. A repeatedly patched irrigation main is usually cheaper replaced than chased. Insist on a written locate with a depth and a distance, plus a clear statement about who backfills, compacts and restores the surface, before authorizing anything with a trencher on it.

## svc_pool_leak_detection_lede

A pool wanting make-up water twice a week may be leaking, or may simply be sitting outdoors in July. Telling those two apart comes first. Deciding which fitting, line or surface is responsible comes second.

## svc_pool_leak_detection_body

### Prove the loss before the deck is opened

Start with a measurement rather than an opinion. Mark the waterline on tile with tape, set a bucket of pool water on a step so its inside level matches the pool, and compare both after twenty-four hours with the pump conditions written down. Evaporation acts on both, so a pool dropping faster than the bucket suggests a genuine loss. Wind, splash-out, a backwash valve left ajar, heavy swimming and a working auto-fill can all distort the result, so repeat it. Metering the refill water is the other test worth running, since a sharp rise in what the pool consumes says the same thing from the billing side.

### What the climate takes anyway

Expectations need calibrating before anyone spends money. Published Arizona water figures put the average annual evaporation rate around Phoenix and Tucson at roughly six feet, enough that a typical sixteen-thousand-gallon pool loses close to its whole volume across a year. Half an inch overnight in late June is not automatically a defect. A quarter inch a day in February is a much stronger signal than the same reading in July. Track the loss against the season, note whether it changed suddenly or crept, and bring those numbers to the visit; they narrow the search before any equipment comes out of the truck.

### Pressure side, suction side, or the vessel

Behavior under the equipment tells a technician where to look. Loss that speeds up while the pump runs points at plumbing after the pump, where return lines are pressurized and a split pushes water into the ground. Loss that continues or worsens with everything off points at suction plumbing, the skimmer throat, a main drain, or the shell and its fittings. Air pulled into the pump basket, bubbles at a return, and a filter pressure reading that has drifted low all support a suction-side story. Wet ground or a settling patch beside the equipment pad narrows it further. Dye testing at light niches, returns, skimmer joints and tile cracks handles the vessel side once the plumbing has been cleared.

### The auto-fill hides everything

A working automatic filler is the reason a pool leak so often shows up as a City Services bill instead of a falling waterline. The device does its job, the level never changes, and the loss runs quietly for months at whatever rate the crack allows. City conservation guidance lists the pool auto-refill device alongside the hose bib, the pump and the outdoor water line as places to inspect for leaks, and that ordering is fair. Isolate the filler before running a level test, but only when the pump will still have enough water to operate safely, and never run equipment below its required level.

### A patio slab is not automatically safe to cut

Concrete around a pool deserves the same caution as the floor indoors. The building code's marking requirement calls out patio slabs by name as a conspicuous place to stamp a post-tensioned slab, so any deck or patio pour adjacent to the house may carry tendons rather than mesh. Cutting one without mapping the steel first is a structural risk, not a shortcut. Cemented caliche under the deck complicates it further, since a saw cut and a shallow dig can quickly turn into breaking rock. That is exactly why the locate should be finished, marked and photographed before the first blade touches anything.

### The visit, in order

A methodical pool visit reads the pad before the pool. Valve positions, filter pressure, pump behavior, the filler and any water feature get checked and recorded. Lines are then isolated and pressure-tested individually, which distinguishes a return leak from a suction leak and rules out sound plumbing. Listening equipment and, where a line holds pressure poorly, tracer gas locate the escape point along the traced route. Dye work at fittings and visible cracks follows. The output should be a marked point with distances from two fixed features, a note of the method that found it, and a plain statement of what still needs confirming.

### What sets the range

Pool detection prices in this market spread widely because the work does. A single return line under an accessible section of decking is at one end. Suspected shell or bond beam trouble, a spa sharing plumbing with the pool, a water feature on its own run, or a pad rebuilt twice by different crews sits at the other. Repair scope moves it again, since replacing a fitting differs from rerouting a line around a deck or opening structural concrete. What should never change is the order: locate and mark first, then price the repair against a known failure rather than an average.

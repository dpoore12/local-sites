## symptom_1_title

Moving across Orlando or somewhere else in Orange County

## symptom_1

A cross-town move looks simple and then gets eaten by driving. Orlando spreads for miles, so a move from Audubon Park out to MetroWest, or from downtown to a new build near Lake Nona, means the truck crossing the same congested corridors twice with a full load in between. Crews that start at seven in the morning get the drive done before traffic sets up; a nine o'clock start can lose an hour of paid time to I-4 and 408 backups that nobody budgeted for.

Local moves are usually priced by the hour with a crew size and a truck attached, which means the number moves when the loading conditions move. Long carries matter here. Many Orlando apartment complexes and downtown buildings put the truck at a loading dock or a marked bay rather than at your door, and that walk gets billed. So does a shuttle: a full-size truck cannot get down some of the narrow brick streets and oak-lined lanes around Thornton Park and College Park, so the load transfers to a smaller vehicle.

What keeps it to one trip is an accurate inventory before the day. Garages here run hot and full — patio sets, bikes, storage bins, hurricane shutters and plywood panels stacked along the wall. Attic pull-downs hide holiday bins. Those are the items people forget to mention and they are what pushes a 26-foot truck into a second run.

Ask for the estimate in writing with the crew size, the hourly rate, the drive time and every accessorial spelled out, and ask that it carry the state registration number Florida requires on mover estimates. Then the total on load day is the total you already agreed to.

## symptom_2_title

Leaving Orlando for another state, or arriving from one

## symptom_2

An interstate move is a different legal animal from a move inside Florida. Once the truck crosses a state line the job falls under federal household goods rules rather than Florida's Chapter 507, so the paperwork changes: you should receive a written estimate, a bill of lading and the federal consumer rights booklet, and the mover operates under a USDOT number instead of a state mover registration.

Two things decide what this costs. Weight or cubic volume, and the delivery window. Orlando sits at the end of a long haul from the Northeast and the Midwest, and inbound and outbound loads are often consolidated with other shipments, which is why delivery is quoted as a spread of days rather than a time. If a firm arrival date matters — a closing, a lease start, a school year — say so before booking, because a dedicated truck costs more and has to be scheduled, not improvised.

Valuation is where people get hurt. Released value protection is the free default and it pays by weight, which is close to nothing on a flat-screen television or a laptop. Full value protection costs extra and covers repair or replacement. Decide before load day, in writing, item by item for anything high value.

Two Orlando specifics. Hurricane season runs June 1 through November 30 with peak activity from mid-August to mid-October according to the National Hurricane Center, and a named storm in the Gulf or off the Atlantic coast can stall a truck for days at either end. Second, if you are arriving here in July or August, expect closing calendars and apartment turnovers to be at their busiest, so book earlier than you think you need to.

## symptom_3_title

Moving out of or into an Orlando apartment or condo

## symptom_3

Apartment and condo moves fail on building rules far more often than on furniture. Around 44 percent of occupied homes in Orange County are rented and roughly 28 percent of the housing stock is in apartment complexes, so this is the most common move in the city, and nearly every managed property has a protocol.

Expect three requirements. A certificate of insurance from the mover, naming the property and its management company, filed with the office before load day — many buildings want it several business days ahead and will turn a crew away without it. A reserved elevator or loading window, often weekdays only, sometimes closed on the first and last days of the month when everyone else is turning over. And access details: gate codes, a dock height, a stairwell that must be padded, a parking bay that has to be coned off the night before.

The physical work is stairs and distance. A third-floor walk-up in a 1970s garden-style complex off Semoran or Curry Ford means every box goes up on a shoulder, and the crew size that makes that survivable is bigger than the one that would load a house. Downtown towers are the opposite problem: short carries, but everything queues through one service elevator.

Tight interior turns are where damage happens, so doorframes, corners and stair edges get padded before anything moves. Mattresses go in bags, not bare through a stairwell.

Give the crew the building's move-in packet when you book. The rules in it decide the arrival time, the crew size and the truck, and every one of those shows up on the written estimate before load day rather than as a surprise fee at the dock.

## symptom_4_title

You are out of time and the packing is not done

## symptom_4

Packing is the task everybody underestimates, and in Orlando it has a local wrinkle: a lot of what needs boxing has been sitting in an unconditioned garage in Florida humidity. Cardboard that has been stacked out there for a season goes soft, tape lets go, and boxes fail when they are lifted. Fresh cartons are worth more than they cost.

Packing is sold three ways. Full pack, where the crew boxes the whole house, usually the day before load day. Partial pack, which in practice means the kitchen, the garage and the closets — the three rooms that consume the most hours. And a fragile-only pack covering dishes, glassware, artwork, lamps and televisions, which is also the split that matters most for a claim, because carrier-packed cartons are treated differently from owner-packed ones if something arrives broken.

Kitchens are the real work. Dishes are packed on edge in dish barrels with paper between every plate, not stacked flat. Glassware gets individually wrapped and cell dividers. A full kitchen is a couple of hours for a trained packer and most of a weekend for the person who lives there.

Some things a moving crew cannot legally load: propane tanks, gasoline and other fuels, charcoal starter, paint and solvents, pool chlorine, fireworks, ammunition primers, aerosols. That list catches Orlando households hard because grills, generators and pool chemicals are standard here. Drain the mower, empty the grill tank, and set those items aside to transport yourself or dispose of.

Wardrobe cartons on load day handle hanging clothes in minutes, and clearly labelled cartons by destination room are what make unloading fast on the other end.

## qa_1_question

How do I check that an Orlando moving company is legitimate?

## qa_1_answer

Florida regulates this directly. Chapter 507 of the Florida Statutes requires any mover doing intrastate household moves — a move that starts and ends in Florida — to be registered with the Florida Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services before it operates or advertises. That registration number is not decorative. The statute requires it on every estimate and contract, in advertising, and on a sign on the driver's-side door of the truck in lettering at least an inch and a half tall. So you can check three things without any special knowledge: the number on the estimate, the number in the ad, and the number on the door of the truck sitting in your driveway. Ask for the number, then look the registration up with the department. Operating without one draws a cease and desist order and a civil penalty of up to $5,000, so a mover with nothing to show you is telling you something.

## qa_2_question

When should I book, and does hurricane season matter?

## qa_2_answer

Two calendars drive Orlando. The first is the monthly one: leases turn over at month end, so the last three days and the first two of any month are the hardest windows to get in this city, and they book weeks ahead. Midweek, mid-month is cheaper and easier to schedule everywhere from Baldwin Park to Azalea Park. The second is the storm calendar. The National Hurricane Center puts the Atlantic season at June 1 through November 30, with most activity between mid-August and mid-October and the statistical peak on September 10. That does not make a September move a bad idea, but it does change how you plan one. Keep the date away from the end of the month if you can, so there is slack to shift a day or two, keep documents and medications with you rather than on the truck, and understand that an interstate delivery window can stretch when a named storm closes a corridor. If a storm is forecast within days of your date, expect the schedule to be rearranged rather than driven into.

## qa_3_question

Why does the Orlando address change the crew and the truck?

## qa_3_answer

Because access decides the labor, and access varies wildly across this city. A 1920s bungalow in College Park has a short driveway, a narrow side lane and a mature oak canopy that a 26-foot box truck cannot always get under, which sometimes means a shuttle. Baldwin Park loads from alleys behind the houses. Thornton Park means on-street loading on tight brick streets near downtown. A new-build in Lake Nona is easy at the curb but sits behind a gate with visitor hours. MetroWest and the corridor complexes mean a dock, a service elevator and a certificate of insurance filed with management. Same amount of furniture, completely different day. That is why the crew size, the truck and the arrival window are set from the addresses at both ends rather than from a square-footage guess, and why the accessorial charges — stairs, long carry, shuttle, elevator — are written on the estimate before load day rather than added on the ramp.

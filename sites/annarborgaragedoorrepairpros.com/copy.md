### The bang, and then a door that fights back

Torsion springs give no warning and then make a lot of noise. People describe a single dull crack, like a plank dropped on concrete, and it often happens overnight with nobody in the garage. The next press of the button fills in the rest: the motor pulls hard, the top section rises two or three inches, and all that steel eases back onto the slab. Go inside and look at the shaft bolted across the header. A failed torsion spring leaves two slack halves with a gap between them wide enough to put a finger through, and the winding cone at that end has usually spun out of position.

### Springs are rated in openings, not in years

ANSI/DASMA 102, the sectional door specification, requires residential and commercial door systems to be designed to operate a minimum of 10,000 cycles when properly selected, installed, operated and maintained, and it counts one cycle as closed to fully open and back to closed again. The same document describes a residential door as one normally expected to run fewer than 1,500 cycles a year. Hold that against your own routine: four openings on an ordinary day works out near 1,460 a year, which makes a minimum-rated spring roughly a seven-year part. Springs built for higher cycle counts cost a little more in steel.

### Chloride is what shortens spring wire here

DASMA's technical sheet on spring cycle life is blunt about corrosion. Springs should be kept dry, because rust reduces the effective area of the wire and therefore its strength, and rust leaves pits from which fatigue cracks accelerate. That sheet also names chemicals containing chlorine as a cause of corrosion and premature failure. Public Works plows and salts 346 lane miles of Priority 1 and 2 streets down to bare pavement, and residents can collect up to five gallons of sand and salt mix per visit. Every vehicle coming in off Miller or Packard carries brine on its tires and rocker panels, it steams off in the warmer air of a closed garage, and it settles on the shaft, cones and bearing plates hanging directly above the opening.

### Winding bars, and why nothing else substitutes

A broken spring is not a spent spring. The surviving half still carries load, and on a two-spring shaft the intact one is fully wound. Bleeding off that tension takes steel winding bars sized to the cone, one bar holding while the other moves. Screwdrivers and cut lengths of rebar wander in the cone hole, slip under load, and leave at speed. Loosening set screws on a wound cone is the other classic mistake, because the cone will spin the moment they clear. For a homeowner the safe sequence is short: unplug the operator, leave the door down, and stay clear of the opening.

Extension hardware and torsion hardware are not interchangeable, and a low-clearance conversion needs its own brackets and track aboard the truck.

### The visit, in order

Numbers come off the failed spring first: wire diameter, inside diameter and overall length, plus door height and drum size, since that combination is what makes a replacement correct instead of merely similar. ### Halfway down, then straight back up

That is the safety chain behaving as federal rules require. Part 1211 of Title 16 covers every residential garage door operator built for sale in the United States on or after January 1, 1993. Under section 1211.7, apart from the first foot of travel down from the full up position, an operator has to begin reversing within two seconds of contact with an obstruction and then return the door to the full up position and stop there. Section 1211.6 requires a secondary means of entrapment protection on top of that, and section 1211.8 lists what counts: an external photoelectric sensor, or an edge sensor on the bottom of the door, which section 1211.12 says has to trip at 15 pounds of force or less. So a door that refuses to finish closing is usually a working system reacting to something real, and the two eyes mounted low on either jamb are where to look first.

### Cold mornings and the close-force setting

### What it looks like from inside the garage

The door is out of square. One corner sits lower than the other, or a section bulges away from the wall, or the gap along the top rail widens toward one side. Down at floor level you may find slack cable coiled near the jamb, a cable that has jumped the grooves on its drum, or a roller stem sitting proud of the track with the wheel out in open air. ### Stop running it

Whatever cables and rollers are still engaged are carrying the whole weight alone. Pressing the button to see how bad it is turns one section out of its track into a door across the hood of the car. Pull the plug at the ceiling outlet, keep people and vehicles clear of the opening, and leave the pry bar in the drawer, since levering a section back over the lip bends the track flange.

### The three ways a door leaves its track

A cable comes off its drum. Each lift cable anchors at the bottom bracket and winds into grooves on a drum at the end of the torsion shaft. When a spring fails and the door drops hard, or when a cable is allowed to slacken, it can climb out of those grooves, and that half of the door loses its lift and racks in the opening.

A roller gives up. Nylon rollers grow brittle in a cold garage and crack; steel ones seize once the bearing dries out and grit packs the race. A seized roller skids rather than rolls and eventually climbs the rail at the curve where the vertical track turns toward horizontal.

The track itself is bent. A bumper does it, and so does snow: a plow windrow pushed against the door, or a drift that refreezes into ice against the bottom section, loads the vertical track in a direction it was never meant to take.

### The bottom two feet take the whole winter

Everything that fails on a derailed door lives in the lowest part of the opening: bottom brackets, cable ends, the first two feet of vertical track, and the fasteners buried in the bottom section. That is also where wet salt lands, and where meltwater refreezes overnight against a bottom seal that can bond to the concrete. Rust at a bottom bracket is not cosmetic, because the bracket is the anchor point for a cable under full spring tension, and a corroded bracket bolt shearing during removal turns a cable job into bracket and track work.

### Detached garages, and a frame that has moved

Michigan builds to the International Residential Code, 2015 edition, adopted by reference in the state construction code rules at R 408.30500 with an effective date of February 8, 2022. The state's own amendment adds Table R301.2(5), which sets a ground snow load of 20 pounds per square foot for the City of Ann Arbor where most of Washtenaw County carries 25. Plenty of local garages went up long before any of that. Garages of the pre-war era, especially the freestanding ones tucked behind narrow lots in Water Hill between Miller, Brooks, Sunset and the railroad tracks, have settled on their slabs. A header that has dropped or a jamb pulled out of plumb keeps throwing rollers no matter how many new ones go in, which is why the opening gets measured, not just the hardware.

### The repair, step by step

The door is clamped in the track below the lowest roller so it cannot travel while anyone works on it. Sections come back into line one at a time, rollers are re-seated or replaced, and cables are respooled with matched tension on both drums so the door hangs level. Bent track is straightened where that is sound or swapped for new where it is not, and hinges get checked, since a section that came out of the track usually took a hinge or a stile with it. Bottom brackets and their fasteners are inspected for corrosion rather than wiped off and reused. The job finishes with the door lifted by hand: it should hold near waist height on its own. If it sinks or climbs, spring tension is off and the new rollers will grind themselves down too.

The failure mode here is snow, and it is worth understanding before choosing hinged leaves. ## svc_commercial_garage_door_repair_lede

Commercial doors are different machines with different rules. A rolling steel service door, a fire door with an automatic closing device, and a high-cycle sectional dock door each carry inspection obligations that residential doors do not.

## svc_commercial_garage_door_repair_body

### The equipment, and what each type fails at

Four families cover most of it. Rolling steel service doors coil into a barrel above the opening and fail at the barrel bearings, the curtain slats and the guides. Sectional commercial doors resemble residential ones but run on heavier track with high-cycle springs and jackshaft or trolley operators. Rolling steel fire doors look like service doors but include an automatic closing device that drops the curtain on a heat or alarm signal. Grilles and counter shutters are their own category. Operators are the other half of the job: three-phase power, motor starters, reversing edges and control stations behave nothing like a homeowner's chain drive. A working diagnosis names the door type before it names a part, and the residential side of the work is listed on the [service index](../services/).

### Fire doors and the annual drop test

This is the obligation most building owners are unaware of. DASMA's guidance for rolling steel fire doors states that automatic closing must be tested at installation and not less than annually as required by NFPA 80, that the requirement applies to all fire doors including ones that are never used in daily operation, and that the drop test is performed twice: once to check operation and full closure, and again after the automatic closing device is reset. It sets an average closing speed of 6 to 24 inches per second, calls for the work to be done by a trained door systems technician, requires a visual inspection before the test, and directs that repair parts come from the original manufacturer. A door that fails the test is documented and repaired rather than left in service.

### What a code inspection looks at

DASMA's inspection guidance for the code side identifies the items that get checked on any powered door. Torsion spring anchor pads must be fastened to structurally sound load-bearing members. An extension spring containment system must be present per the manufacturer's documentation. A manual release has to be reachable, with the residential requirement placing it within six feet of the floor. A control station is mounted at least five feet above the floor so it cannot be operated by a child. At least one entrapment protection device is required, and the manufacturer's warning labels must be in place. On commercial equipment those items are usually where a deferred maintenance program shows itself first.

### Cycles are the number that matters

Residential doors are designed to ANSI/DASMA 102's minimum of 10,000 cycles, with a cycle counted as closed to fully open and back, and a residential door expected to run fewer than 1,500 cycles a year. A loading door at a busy back-of-house can do that in a month. Springs, cables, bearings and hinges are specified in cycle classes for exactly this reason, and fitting standard-cycle springs to a high-traffic door produces a failure roughly on schedule. ## svc_emergency_garage_door_repair_lede

Some door failures wait until Tuesday and some do not. A car sealed in, a door stopped halfway up overnight, or a section hanging out of its track are the three that get moved to the front of the queue, and the reasons are security and load rather than convenience.

## svc_emergency_garage_door_repair_body

### What genuinely qualifies

Four conditions are treated as urgent. A door jammed shut with the only vehicle behind it. A door stuck open, which leaves the house connected to the street through an unlocked interior door. A section out of its track, because the remaining rollers and cables are carrying weight in a geometry nobody designed. Everything else, including noise, a dead remote and a slow opener, is a scheduled repair and belongs on the [main repair index](../services/).

### Make the opening safe before anyone arrives

Unplug the operator at the ceiling outlet so nobody presses a button out of habit, and keep people and vehicles out from under the opening. If the door is stopped partway, leave it where it is rather than trying to lower it; a door held by a single loaded cable can come down all at once. Lock the interior door between the garage and the house, since that door is now the only barrier. Do not pull the emergency release on a crooked or partly open door, because that transfers the entire weight from the operator to hardware that has already failed once.

This is the part most people do not expect. With 122 days a year below freezing in the GLISA climatology for the city, that is a season rather than an event. ## svc_garage_door_hinge_repair_lede

Few components cost less than a hinge, and few do more damage on their way out. Once a knuckle cracks, the section it holds starts moving where it should not, and the roller, the track and the panel edge absorb the difference.

## svc_garage_door_hinge_repair_body

### Hinges are numbered, and the numbers are not optional

Look at a sectional door from inside and the hinges carry stamped numbers, typically 1 through 5. The number sets the offset between the two leaves, which controls how far each section stands off from the one below as the door follows the curve of the track. A number 1 goes between the bottom two sections, and the number climbs as you go up. Fitting a hinge with the wrong number changes the geometry: the section either binds against its neighbor or stands proud, the roller stem sits at the wrong depth in the track, and the door starts to shudder at the curve. Replacing a cracked hinge with whatever is in the truck is a common shortcut and it produces a door that never runs right again. The wider set of repairs is on the [service index](../services/).

### Where they crack, and what you hear first

Steel hinges fail at the knuckle where the pin passes through, and at the leaf around the fastener holes. The audible signature is a single hard pop or clack at the same point in travel, usually as the failing section reaches the transition from vertical to horizontal track, because that is where the load changes direction. A hinge with a hairline crack still holds under static weight and opens under motion, which is why an inspection by eye with the door closed misses it. The other failure is at the stile: the sheet metal around the fastener tears, the hinge loosens, and the door begins to work its own holes bigger every cycle until the section edge deforms.

### Salt gets into the pin and the fastener

Corrosion is the local accelerant. Deicing brine arrives on tires and dries on the lowest hardware, and the hinges between the bottom two sections sit exactly in that zone. DASMA's technical sheet on spring cycle life notes that rust reduces the effective cross-section of steel and that chlorine-bearing chemicals cause corrosion and premature failure, and the same chemistry acts on a hinge pin and a self-tapping screw. A rusted pin binds before it breaks, so the hinge starts flexing at the leaf instead of pivoting at the pin. The result is a cracked leaf on a hinge whose pin looks intact. On the older detached garages of the Old Fourth Ward, a local historic district since 1983 with its survey updated in 2024, and the alley garages of Water Hill between Miller, Brooks and Sunset, the bottom hinges are frequently the most corroded parts on the door.

### The bottom bracket is not a hinge and must not be treated as one

There is one component on this list that will injure someone. The bottom corner bracket carries the lift cable, so it is under the full tension of the counterbalance whenever the door is down. Unbolting it with the springs loaded releases that tension violently. Manufacturers supply tamper-resistant bottom brackets for this reason, and the correct sequence is to secure the door, relieve or capture spring tension, and then work the bracket. Any advice that has you removing a bottom bracket to change a roller with the door closed and the springs wound is wrong.

### What a proper repair covers

Every hinge is checked at the knuckle and at the leaf with the door in motion, not only at rest. Cracked hinges are replaced with the correct stamped number, and torn stile metal is addressed rather than screwed through again, since a fastener in a torn hole holds nothing. Fasteners go into sound material; DASMA's code guidance requires spring anchor pads to be fastened to structurally sound load-bearing members, and the same principle applies to track brackets in a garage where the jamb has softened. Rollers are pulled and spun, because a hinge failure is often a seized roller's second symptom. Then the door is cycled and listened to before the ladder comes down. ## svc_garage_door_insulation_lede

Insulating a garage door is worth doing for specific reasons and oversold for others. The number printed on the box is usually the wrong number, and in this climate the useful gains are steadier temperatures, a quieter door and less condensation rather than a heated room.

## svc_garage_door_insulation_body

### R-value on the label, U-factor in reality

The industry's own guidance is blunt about this. DASMA's technical data sheet on the subject explains that R-value is a calculated figure for a door section alone, ignoring the joints between sections, the end stiles, the perimeter and the hardware, and states plainly that it should not be used for garage door assemblies. U-factor is the measured property of the complete installed assembly, tested to ANSI/DASMA 105, which accounts for the sections, joints, stiles, thermal breaks, air spaces, weather seals and track. Codes address thermal performance in terms of U-factor, and a lower U-factor is better. So a door advertised with a high section R-value and loose joints can underperform a lower-rated door that seals properly. Ask for the tested U-factor of the assembly, and compare the rest of the options on the [service list](../services/).

### What the upgrade actually buys in a Michigan winter

Expect three real effects. The garage swings less, which matters where the space holds a freezer, a water line, paint or a car battery, and the local baseline is 122 days a year below freezing in the GLISA climatology for the city. The door gets substantially quieter and stiffer, because a bonded polyurethane core damps panel drumming and resists the flex that works hinges loose. And an attached garage stops feeding cold air into the wall it shares with the house. What it does not do is make the garage warm. DASMA notes directly that an insulated door may not perform at the same level as the surrounding walls, and an uninsulated ceiling and slab will dominate the result.

### Condensation, and why an insulated door can look worse at first

Owners often report water on the inside of a new insulated door and assume a defect. DASMA's sheet on condensation describes the mechanism: droplets collect on a cold surface when humid air contacts it, and interior humidity rises with a snow-covered car parked inside, a laundry area on the other side of a wall, or a humidifier running in the house. Even double and triple glazing can condense. It is not a product failure. The response is ventilation, keeping standing meltwater off the slab, and accepting that a tighter door raises interior humidity because it stops the drafts that used to carry it away. Fogging inside a sealed window unit is different and can indicate a failed glazing seal.

### Thermal bowing, which is inherent and not a fault

Insulated sections with a bonded core bow, and DASMA says so explicitly: the warmer skin grows relative to the cooler one and the section curves toward the warmer side. The temperature difference across the door drives the amount, so a January morning with a heated garage inside and single digits outside produces the visible maximum. Larger doors bow more, darker colors facing direct sun bow more, thermally broken designs have greater potential bow than non-broken ones, and aluminum and plastic components expand more than steel. Practically, that argues against a very dark finish on a wide south-facing door, and it explains a door that seals cleanly in October and shows a gap at one edge in February.

### Weight, springs and the older housing stock

An insulated door is heavier than the single-skin door it replaces, sometimes by a third. The counterbalance has to be recalculated: springs sized to the old weight will leave the new door sinking, and the opener will carry the difference until a gear strips. That is the most common mistake on retrofit insulation kits too, since adding rigid foam or a kit to an existing door adds weight the springs were never wound for. With the median year a housing unit was built in Ann Arbor recorded as 1971 in the American Community Survey table for the city, plenty of the attached garages near Pattengill Elementary and the detached garages in the Northside carry original hardware, and the spring conversation belongs in the quote rather than after it. Related: [weather seal replacement](../garage-door-weather-seal-replacement/) and [full door replacement](../garage-door-replacement/).

## svc_garage_door_lock_repair_lede

Locks and handles are the part of a garage door people only think about twice: when the key stops turning in February, and when a forgotten slide bolt meets a motor that does not know it is there.

## svc_garage_door_lock_repair_body

### The hardware set, and what each piece does

Four items cover almost every residential door. A T-handle sits in the middle of the bottom section, turns a cylinder, and pulls two lock cables that drive spring-loaded bars out into the track on both sides. A slide bolt, or cane bolt, is a simple manual bar mounted inside on one or both sides, thrown by hand into a hole in the track. An inside slide lock is the same idea with a padlock hasp. On a one-piece door there may be a rim latch instead. Each fails differently: cables stretch and stop pulling both bars evenly, cylinders seize, and bolts bend where they enter the track. Identifying which is fitted comes before ordering anything, and the wider job list sits on the [service index](../services/).

### The lock that fights the opener

This is the single most expensive lock fault and it is entirely avoidable. When an opener is installed on a door that still has a working manual lock, someone eventually throws the bolt out of habit. The motor then drives a door that is pinned to the track. On a good day the operator's force limit trips and the door stops; on a bad one the bolt bends the track, tears the bottom section's stile, or shears the bracket. The correct practice on an automated door is to disable the manual lock: remove the bolts or wire the T-handle's cables back so they cannot extend, leaving the handle in place for appearance and for manual use if the opener is ever removed. That step is worth confirming after any [new opener installation](../garage-door-opener-installation/).

### Detached garages where the door is the only way in

Access changes the stakes. A great many garages here sit at the back of the lot with no service door and no interior connection to the house, which is the standard arrangement behind the older houses of the Old West Side, in the National Register since 1972 and under a local ordinance since 1978, and on the narrower lots of the Northside, where the neighborhood's school building dates from 1939. When the only lock on the only opening fails shut, the contents are inaccessible and there is no second route. When it fails open, the building is open. That is why a seized cylinder on a detached garage is treated as more urgent than the same fault on an attached one, where the house door provides another way in and another barrier.

### Cold, salt and the seized cylinder

The mechanism is specific. A key cylinder mounted on the outside of the bottom section sits a few feet above a slab that collects meltwater and brine off the tires. Moisture wicks into the keyway, freezes, and the pins stop moving; over seasons the same moisture, carrying chloride, corrodes the wafers and the cam. Forcing a key in that condition is how the key snaps off in the cylinder. Heavy oil makes it worse by holding grit and thickening in the cold, so a dry graphite or a proper PTFE lock lubricant is the right product. The same applies to slide bolts, which rust in the track hole and then need to be driven rather than slid. Rinsing brine off the bottom of the door in a thaw does more for lock life than any lubricant.

### Historic districts, visible hardware, and what the visit covers

A repair is a repair, but a change in appearance can be a review question. The city states that all exterior work in its historic districts is regulated by the Historic District Commission and requires an approved Certificate of Appropriateness, and it asks owners to check with staff before altering an existing accessory structure. Swapping a broken cylinder is maintenance; replacing plain hardware with decorative strap hinges and a new visible handle on a garage inside one of the city's 15 local historic districts is worth a call to preservation staff first. On the work itself: cylinders are rekeyed or replaced, lock cables are adjusted so both bars throw evenly, bent bolts are replaced rather than straightened, and the bottom section is checked where the handle mounts, since a lock that has been forced usually leaves a torn stile behind. ## svc_garage_door_opener_installation_lede

Choosing an opener is mostly a question about the building, not the brand. Headroom, whether the garage is heated, and the weight of the door decide the drive type and the force class, and getting those wrong is what makes a new unit sound worse than the one it replaced.

## svc_garage_door_opener_installation_body

### Four drive types, and the one older garages need

A chain drive is the cheapest and the loudest, and it is fine over a detached garage where nobody sleeps above it. A belt drive is a reinforced rubber belt on the same rail, noticeably quieter, and the default choice under living space. A direct drive moves the motor along a fixed rail and has fewer moving parts. ### Horsepower is the wrong number to shop on

Nominal horsepower is a marketing figure that says little about whether a unit will move your door. What matters is the force the unit can apply and the weight it is rated to lift, and those are properties of the door: width, number of layers, whether it is insulated, and whether it is properly counterbalanced. A correctly sprung door weighs almost nothing at the trolley, which is why a balance test comes before an opener selection rather than after it. Standard rails suit doors up to seven feet; an eight-foot door needs an extension kit and a taller opening needs a longer rail ordered with the unit. ## svc_garage_door_remote_programming_lede

A remote that has stopped working, a keypad that beeps and does nothing, or a new opener that will not learn a fob are radio and battery problems. None of them requires touching the springs, and most are settled in one visit.

## svc_garage_door_remote_programming_body

### Rolling codes, and why a fob cannot simply be copied

Every residential opener sold for decades has used a rolling code: transmitter and receiver both advance through a sequence, so the code sent this time is never reused. Pairing is a deliberate act. You press a learn button on the motor head, the receiver accepts the next transmitter it hears for about thirty seconds, and the pair is stored in memory that holds a limited number of devices. That memory is why a hardware store fob sometimes refuses to work at all, why an old fob stops after a replacement head goes in, and why erasing all devices and re-teaching each one is often faster than chasing a single dead remote. The rest of the opener faults live on the [repair index](../services/).

### Cold is hard on the cell before it is hard on the radio

An alkaline cell does not lose capacity in the cold so much as become unable to deliver it. Energizer's own application handbook states the recommended operating range runs to minus 18 degrees Celsius, that ion diffusion slows as temperature drops, and that a high-drain pull in a cold environment produces a large voltage sag because internal resistance has risen. A transmitter is a short high-drain pull. That is why a fob clipped to a visor overnight works from three feet in the morning and from thirty feet by afternoon. With 122 days a year below freezing in the University of Michigan GLISA climatology for the city, a marginal cell is diagnosed by carrying it indoors for an hour and trying again.

### Exterior keypads take weather nobody designs for

The wireless keypad screwed to the jamb outside is the one radio device permanently exposed to slush thrown off the driveway and to the salt residue that comes with it. Water tracks in behind the rubber keymat, corrodes the contact pads, and produces a keypad that beeps on some digits and ignores others. Freeze and thaw cycling splits the mat itself. Mounting matters as much as the unit: a keypad set low on a jamb in Water Hill, where the garages sit off the alleys between Miller, Brooks and Sunset, catches every plow spray, while the same keypad mounted higher and under the trim lasts years longer.

### Rentals near campus, and the fobs that left with the tenant

The city's downtown housing assessment recorded the downtown study area at 76.7 percent renter-occupied in 2020 and counted nearly 48,000 students that fall, and the blocks of the Old Fourth Ward carry a good share of that. In practice a garage in a rented house has accumulated fobs across several tenancies, and nobody knows how many still open the door. Clearing the receiver's memory and re-teaching only the transmitters currently in hand is a security step, not a convenience one. Since all rental units in the city are inspected on a 30-month cycle for a Certificate of Compliance under Housing Code Chapter 105, it is worth reporting a dead keypad or an unaccounted-for remote to the manager in writing rather than working around it.

### What is actually wrong, in rough order of frequency

A tired battery comes first, then a receiver that lost its memory after a power event, then a keypad with corroded contacts, then a transmitter with a cracked board from being dropped on concrete. Genuine receiver failure is last and is usually a logic board rather than the whole unit. A visit measures signal at the head, replaces cells with fresh ones rather than tested ones, reprograms every device in the household including the car's built-in buttons, and confirms range from the end of the driveway rather than from inside the garage. If the door is also slow or noisy while responding, the radio was never the whole story, and [a smart control retrofit](../smart-garage-door-opener/) or [lock and handle work](../garage-door-lock-repair/) may be the better next step.

## svc_garage_door_safety_inspection_lede

A safety inspection is a set of defined tests with pass and fail answers, not a walkthrough. Two of them take ninety seconds and are the ones that decide whether the door will stop for a person underneath it.

## svc_garage_door_safety_inspection_body

### What the law actually requires of the opener

Federal regulation sets the baseline. Title 16 Part 1211 applies to residential garage door operators manufactured for sale from January 1, 1993 onward. It requires the operator to reverse the door within two seconds of contacting an obstruction. It requires a secondary means of entrapment protection in addition to that inherent reversal, and specifies what qualifies: an external photoelectric beam or an edge sensor that trips at a force of 15 pounds or less. An opener with no beam and no sensing edge is either older than 1993 or has had its protection defeated, and both answers point at replacement rather than adjustment. Where the fault is narrower, the individual repairs are listed on the [service index](../services/).

### The two tests, done in order

DASMA's residential checklist gives the practical procedure. First, the reversal test at the beam: with the door closing, break the photoelectric beam with an object and confirm the door stops and returns fully open. Second, the contact reversal: lay a solid object flat on the floor in the door's path, close the door onto it, and confirm the door reverses on contact. A door that hesitates, or that stops without reversing, or that needs the object to be tall enough to be seen has failed. These are done deliberately, with hands clear, and never with a person or pet as the obstruction.

### The hand tests that tell you why it failed

The same checklist covers the mechanical half. Pull the release and lift the door by hand: it should move freely, it should not accelerate away from the force you are applying, the rollers should stay in the track, and it should stay in place 3 to 4 feet above the floor. A door that sinks is under-sprung and the operator has been compensating with force, which is the most common reason a contact reversal fails. Handles and grips are checked so hands cannot be caught in a pinch point between sections. Cables are inspected for broken strands at the bottom bracket where they flex and where salt collects.

### The code items, and the rental cycle

DASMA's code inspection guidance adds items an inspector looks for: torsion spring anchor pads fastened to structurally sound load-bearing members, an extension spring containment system where extension springs are used, a manual release accessible within six feet of the floor on a residential door, a wall control station mounted at least five feet above the floor so a child cannot reach it, at least one entrapment protection device, and the manufacturer's warning labels intact. This matters on rentals in particular. The city inspects all rental units on a roughly 30-month cycle and issues a Certificate of Compliance under Housing Code Chapter 105, and section 8:516(4) bars leasing a unit without one, so an owner near campus in the Old Fourth Ward has a documented interest in the door passing rather than in it merely working.

### The winter faults an inspection is built to catch here

Cold hides and creates failures in this climate, so the timing of the inspection changes what it finds. A bottom seal frozen to the slab makes the operator work against ice, which masks a force setting that is already too high. Brine dried on cable ends and bottom brackets corrodes the components under the most tension, and DASMA notes that rust reduces the effective cross-section of spring steel and that chlorine-bearing chemicals cause premature failure. Grease stiffened through the 122 days a year below freezing in the GLISA record for the city raises drag on every bearing. An inspection run in October catches those before a spring picks the coldest morning of the year. The report should list each test as passed or failed with the reason. ## svc_garage_door_sensor_repair_lede

The door heads for the floor, stops a foot short, and climbs back up. That is the photo-eye circuit refusing to finish the close, and in an Ann Arbor winter the reason is usually a filmed lens or a corroded splice rather than a failed opener.

## svc_garage_door_sensor_repair_body

### The reversal is a rule, not a malfunction

Federal law forces this behavior. Title 16 Part 1211 governs residential operators built for sale from January 1, 1993 onward, and section 1211.6 requires a secondary means of entrapment protection in addition to the motor's own force reversal. Section 1211.8 lists what satisfies it: an external photoelectric beam across the opening, or a sensing edge on the bottom of the door that trips at 15 pounds or less under section 1211.12. When the beam is broken, obscured or simply not received, the operator is designed to abandon the close and send the door back up. Sorting that from the other faults on the [full list of repairs](../services/) is the first thing worth doing, because a beam problem is cheap and a bound door is not.

### Where the beam actually breaks here

Two eyes sit within six inches of the slab, which is precisely the height that collects everything a Michigan February produces. Brine spray dries into a gray haze on the lens and thins the received signal until the receiver drops out on a bright day and works at dusk. Meltwater running off a car pools around the bracket foot and refreezes, tipping the mount a degree or two. Condensation adds a third mechanism: warm humid air off a snow-covered car meeting a cold lens leaves droplets, and DASMA's sheet on garage door condensation describes exactly that combination of a warm, humid garage and a colder surface. A lens wiped with a dry cloth in October behaves differently in January.

### Wiring is the failure people never look at

The two eyes are wired in series back to the motor head with light two-conductor bell wire, usually stapled along the jamb and up into the ceiling. A staple driven a millimeter too far pinches the insulation, and moisture from a detached garage's freeze and thaw cycling finishes the short. In the detached garages behind the narrow lots of the Northside, where the neighborhood's school building dates to 1939, that run is often long, spliced at least once, and sitting in bare wire nuts inside an unheated space. Rodents chew low-voltage wire in the same buildings. A pinched conductor and a dirty lens produce an identical symptom at the wall button, and only one of them is fixed with a rag.

### Read the indicator lights before anything comes apart

Most brands put a small LED on each eye. A steady light on both usually means the pair is aligned and communicating; a light out or flickering on the receiver side means it is not seeing its partner. Some heads also blink a count on the motor unit that maps to a sensor fault in the manual taped inside the cover. Note which light misbehaves and whether it changes when the sun angle changes, because a receiver that fails only in the late afternoon is a different repair from one that never lights at all. On a 1936 Cape Cod near Gardner Avenue in Lower Burns Park, a garage that has settled will also throw alignment slowly out over a season, which reads as an intermittent fault.

### What the visit corrects

Lenses are cleaned, brackets are re-aimed and locked, and the wire is traced end to end rather than jiggled at the head. Suspect splices are cut out and remade, and staples that have bitten through insulation get replaced with insulated clips. Alignment is verified with the door cycling, not by eye. Then the reversal is tested deliberately by breaking the beam mid-travel and confirming the door returns fully up. If the door also stalls without the beam involved, the fault is drag in the door and the honest answer is that the sensors were never the problem. That is where a door that [will not close for other reasons](../garage-door-wont-close/) or a full [safety inspection](../garage-door-safety-inspection/) picks the job up.

## svc_garage_door_tune_up_lede

A tune-up is not a wash and a spray of oil. It is a measurement, taken against the door's own specification, that says whether the counterbalance is still correct and which wear part is closest to failing.

## svc_garage_door_tune_up_body

### What the work is measured against

There is a published standard behind this. ANSI/DASMA 102 requires a sectional door system to be designed for a minimum of 10,000 cycles, counting one cycle as closed to fully open and back to closed, and describes a residential door as one normally expected to run fewer than 1,500 cycles a year. DASMA's consumer and home inspector checklist adds the tests: lift the door by hand and check that it moves freely, that it does not run faster than the force applied, that the rollers stay in the track, and that it stays put 3 to 4 feet above the floor. Those are pass or fail conditions, not opinions, and they are why this sits apart from the other jobs on the [repair index](../services/).

### The balance test is the entire diagnosis

With the operator disconnected, a correctly counterbalanced door holds its position anywhere in travel. A door that sinks is under-sprung and the opener is carrying weight it was never rated for, which is how a five-year-old head burns out a gear at seven. A door that climbs is over-sprung and slams itself open, working the top hinges and the bearing plate loose. Either condition also skews the force settings, so the reversal that should protect a child under the door is being triggered by ordinary drag instead. Restoring balance is a spring adjustment, not a lubrication job.

### Where lubricant belongs, and where it destroys things

Hinge pins, roller stems, the end and center bearings, the torsion spring coils and the opener rail take a light lithium or silicone lubricant. The tracks take none. A track is a guide surface, and oil on it collects the road salt and grit already sitting in every garage here into a slurry that grinds roller wheels flat. That matters more locally than it sounds: the city plows and salts 346 lane miles of priority streets to bare pavement and lets residents collect five gallons of sand and salt mix per visit, so there is abrasive in the building either way.

### The right month for it here is October

Timing follows the climate. Two things happen in a Michigan winter that a tune-up gets ahead of. Grease in bearings and hinge pins stiffens through the 122 days a year below freezing in the GLISA record for the city, so a marginal bearing that squeaks in November seizes in January. And brine dries onto bottom brackets, cable ends and the lowest two feet of track, where corrosion works on exactly the parts under the most tension. Washing that residue off and re-lubricating before the first hard freeze is worth more than the same visit in March, and it matters most on the unheated detached garages off the alleys in Water Hill, which swing through the full annual range, rather than the attached postwar garages near Pattengill Elementary, opened in 1957.

### Fasteners, and the old header problem

Hardware works loose because a door shakes its own frame thousands of times a year. Hinges, bracket bolts, track fasteners and the bearing plate all get torqued rather than simply nipped up. On older garages the header is the limiting factor: a lag screw overdriven into soft, dry framing turns without ever tightening, and re-driving it in the same hole achieves nothing, so it is moved to a sound fixing point. Cables get inspected at the bottom bracket where they flex and where salt collects, because a frayed cable is a scheduled part and a snapped one is an emergency.

### What you should get in writing

A tune-up worth paying for ends with a short written record: whether the door passed the hand balance test, the spring turn count if it was adjusted, which rollers and hinges are worn, the condition of both cables, and whether the reversal tests passed at the beam and at contact. That record turns an aging door into a maintenance schedule instead of a surprise. A failed reversal moves the job to a [safety inspection](../garage-door-safety-inspection/); a bearing found dry moves it to [noise work](../noisy-garage-door-repair/).

## svc_garage_door_weather_seal_replacement_lede

The strip of rubber along the bottom of a garage door is the part that fails first in this climate, and the reason is rarely the rubber alone. Slab pitch, ice and brine all work on the same six inches of the building.

## svc_garage_door_weather_seal_replacement_body

### Four seals, and knowing which one has gone

A door has more than one seal and they fail differently. The bottom seal, or astragal, is a rubber or vinyl profile that either slides into an aluminum retainer along the bottom edge or is nailed to a wooden bottom rail on older doors. The perimeter stop seal is the flexible flap on the jamb trim that the door closes against. A threshold seal is a separate rubber ramp bonded to the slab, working from below rather than from the door. Daylight along the bottom means the astragal; daylight up one side means the stop; water crossing an otherwise sound bottom edge usually means neither. Sorting that first avoids replacing the wrong part, and the rest of the repairs are on the [service index](../services/).

### Why the bottom seal goes first here

Three mechanisms stack up on that one component through a Michigan winter. Meltwater and salted slush run off a car and pool at the threshold, then refreeze overnight and bond the seal to the concrete, so the next open pulls the rubber against the retainer and tears it or drags the whole strip out of the channel. Repeated freeze and thaw cycling stiffens the material until it takes a permanent set and stops recovering its shape. And brine sits in the retainer, which is aluminum, and corrodes it from the inside so the channel loses its grip. A seal that looks intact from four feet away is often already hard, cracked at the folds and holding nothing.

### Water under the door is often the slab, not the seal

This is the correction worth making before money is spent. DASMA's technical sheet on water infiltration beneath a sectional door treats rainwater migrating past the bottom edge as a common condition rather than a defect, and its remedies are construction details: a slightly inclined lip or sill sloped 3 to 5 degrees away from the opening, or a rectangular notch cut into the slab below grade, at least half an inch deep and wide enough to take a 2-inch track and a 2-inch section. Neither exists in most older garages here, and behind the alley-served garages of the Old West Side the aprons frequently run flat or pitch back toward the door. No seal holds back standing water on a level slab. A threshold seal is the usual practical answer, and it redirects water rather than guaranteeing a dry floor.

### Sealing changes where the water goes, and the city has rules about that

There is a knock-on effect worth thinking about. A threshold seal bonded across the opening dams water at the apron, and in winter that dam becomes ice on the drive and, on a corner lot, potentially on the walk. The city requires residential property owners to clear snow from adjoining public sidewalks within 24 hours after any accumulation greater than an inch, and to treat ice with sand or salt within 18 hours of its forming, with one warning issued per season before fines. Where the apron pitches toward the walk, sealing the door without thinking about drainage moves your problem onto the sidewalk you are responsible for. On the postwar attached garages near Pattengill Elementary, opened in 1957, the drive usually falls to the street and this is less of a concern.

### What the job involves

The door is opened and supported, the old profile pulled and the retainer inspected rather than assumed good; a corroded or spread channel gets replaced with the seal because it will not hold a new bulb. The correct profile matters: a T-end bulb, a beaded end, and a U-shaped nail-on strip are not interchangeable, and the section's own width sets the size. Cut length is measured with a small allowance so the ends are not stretched thin at the corners. The slab is checked for high spots that stop a straight seal sitting down. If the door is out of level, the seal will show a wedge of daylight at one end no matter what is fitted, and that is a track and spring issue rather than a rubber one. Related work is covered under [insulation upgrades](../garage-door-insulation/) and [a door that will not close](../garage-door-wont-close/).

## svc_garage_door_wont_close_lede

When a door lifts normally and then balks at going down, five plausible causes sit behind it. They are separated in a fixed order, because checking them out of sequence is how a fifteen-minute fix turns into a replaced part that was never broken.

## svc_garage_door_wont_close_body

### Start by learning how it refuses

There are three distinct refusals and they are not interchangeable. The door travels partway down and returns fully up: the operator aborted the close, which means the beam, the sensing edge, or the close-force limit. The door does not move at all and the head clicks or the wall button lights: that is a control, lock or limit problem. The door starts down, stops dead, and stays where it is: that is drag in the door itself, or an obstruction, or the close limit set short. Ask which of the three is happening before anything else, then match it against the [full repair index](../services/). Every remaining step below assumes you have answered that first question honestly.

### The Ann Arbor cause that gets missed: the seal frozen to the slab

This one is genuinely local and it produces a fault no diagnostic tree from a warm state contains. Meltwater and salted slush run off a car and pool at the threshold. Overnight it refreezes, and the flexible bottom astragal bonds to the concrete. The next morning the opener starts the close, meets a door already stuck at the bottom on the way back up, or completes the close and cannot lift, and the safety system reads ice as an obstruction. DASMA's sheet on water infiltration under a sectional door describes the remedy in construction terms, a sill inclined 3 to 5 degrees or a notch cut in the slab lip, neither of which is present in most older garages here. Where the slab is flat or pitches inward, as it does behind many alley-facing garages in the Old West Side, the water has nowhere to go but under the seal.

### The photo eyes, checked properly

Look at the two eyes near the floor, clear anything in the beam, and wipe both lenses. Then look at the indicator lights rather than guessing: one dark or flickering light on the receiver means the pair is not communicating. Salt haze, a bracket knocked out of aim by a snow shovel, and a pinched wire under a staple are the three usual culprits, in that order. If the door closes normally when someone holds the wall button down continuously on a unit that allows it, the beam is the fault. Do not disable the eyes to force the door shut. That removes the entrapment protection Title 16 Part 1211 requires and leaves the actual problem in place.

### Close force, travel limits, and the manual lock

If the beam is sound, the next suspect is force. As a door stiffens, the operator needs more push, and when it exceeds its programmed limit it reads resistance as an obstruction. Winding the force up is the wrong response twice over. Next is the close travel limit, which can drift so the operator thinks the floor is an inch lower than it is, stalls against the slab and reverses. Then there is the manual slide bolt or T-handle lock: a door that was locked by hand and is now being driven by a motor will strain, bind and reverse, and on a detached garage in the Northside where the original hardware is still fitted, that is a regular finding.

### Drag in the door, and what the visit does about it

Pull the release with the door closed and lift it by hand. It should move freely and hold near waist height. A door that fights you, or drops, or hangs crooked has a mechanical fault, and the opener was reporting it accurately. Bent track, a cracked roller, a frayed cable, a broken spring and rust binding the bottom brackets all present this way. A visit works the same order in reverse: hand test the door first, correct the mechanical drag, then set the force and limits to the corrected door, then verify the reversal at the beam and at contact. Correcting the door before touching the operator is the difference between a repair and a symptom that returns in two weeks. ## svc_noisy_garage_door_repair_lede

Grinding, squealing, a single hard pop at the same point in travel: a noisy door is diagnosed by sound and by where in the cycle the sound happens. Ann Arbor adds two causes that a warm climate does not.

## svc_noisy_garage_door_repair_body

### Sound, position, and what each pairing means

A door is a machine with about a dozen moving parts, and each one fails audibly in its own way. A dry squeal that rises with speed is usually roller bearings or hinge pins. A rhythmic grinding that tracks the door's travel is a seized roller skidding along the track instead of turning. One hard pop as the top section reaches the curve is a hinge that has cracked at the knuckle and is snapping through its own play. A deep rumble concentrated above the opening is a worn end bearing or center bearing letting the torsion shaft wander. A slapping rattle at the ceiling belongs to the opener chain, not the door. Note the sound and where in the travel it happens before booking anything from the [repair list](../services/), because those two facts point at different hardware.

### Cold stiffens the grease and the door goes loud in December

Michigan gives the mechanism away by the calendar. With 122 days a year below 32 degrees in the GLISA record for the city, hinge pins, roller bearings and shaft bearings run for months in grease that has thickened toward paste. A bearing already dry from a summer of dust starts to squeal only when it is cold and quiets by April, which is exactly when most people decide to ignore it. The second mechanism is chemical rather than thermal: DASMA's technical sheet on spring cycle life notes that rust reduces the effective cross-section of spring wire and that chlorine-bearing chemicals cause corrosion and premature failure. The road salt arriving on tires does the same thing to hinge pins and roller stems, and a rusted pin binds before it breaks.

### Attached garages carry the sound into the house

Where the noise matters is a function of floor plan. The postwar attached garages on the streets near Pattengill Elementary, which opened in 1957, generally share a wall and often a ceiling with living space, so a door that grinds is a bedroom problem and not just a garage problem. Detached garages behind the older houses of Lower Burns Park, where the neighborhood's elementary school dates to 1925, hide the same fault until it becomes a failure. There is a civic dimension too. Chapter 119 of the city code caps sound crossing a residential property line at 61 dB(A) during the day and 55 dB(A) between 10 p.m. and 7 a.m., which is a real consideration for a door on a narrow lot cycling at shift-change hours.

### The wrong fix, and it is sold constantly

Spraying an aerosol lubricant over everything, including the tracks, is the standard homeowner response and it makes the door worse. The track is a guide surface, not a bearing surface; oil on it collects the grit and salt already in the garage and turns the rollers into grinding paste. Lubrication belongs on hinge pins, roller stems, the torsion shaft bearings, the spring coils and the opener rail, applied sparingly with a lithium or silicone product rather than a penetrating oil. Turning the opener force up to push a stiff door through its noise is the other standard mistake, because it hides the drag and dulls the reversal that would otherwise have flagged it.

### What quieting a door actually involves

Rollers get pulled and inspected rather than judged by sight, since a steel roller can spin freely in the hand and still be dragging under load. Hinges are checked at every knuckle for hairline cracks and at the stiles for fastener holes that have started to tear. End and center bearings are spun with the tension off. Loose hardware is torqued to spec, not simply tightened, because an overdriven lag screw in a soft old header holds nothing. Nylon rollers with sealed bearings are the single biggest quiet-per-dollar change on a door built with plain steel wheels. Once it is quiet, the balance test tells you whether it stays quiet, which is where a [tune-up](../garage-door-tune-up/) or dedicated [hinge and bracket work](../garage-door-hinge-repair/) belongs.

## svc_smart_garage_door_opener_lede

## svc_smart_garage_door_opener_body

### What the feature set actually is

Three separate capabilities get sold as one word. Remote operation lets you open and close the door from a phone anywhere, which requires a network connection and a device that can drive the opener's terminals or speak its radio protocol. Status and alerts tell you the door's position and notify you when it moves or has been left open, which needs a position sensor mounted on the door itself. Integration ties the door to a camera, a car, a smart lock or a delivery service. Most people who ask for a smart opener actually want the second capability, because the recurring problem is a door left up all afternoon rather than one that needs opening from a distance. ### Retrofit or replace: how to tell

Openers made from the late 1990s onward generally have accessible low-voltage terminals and a learn button, and a retrofit controller wires across those terminals, mounts a tilt or contact sensor on the top section, and joins your network. That is a one-visit job on a working opener. Two situations call for a new unit instead. An opener with no photoelectric beam or sensing edge predates the entrapment protection that Title 16 Part 1211 has required of units built since January 1993, and it should be replaced rather than made remotely operable, since remote closing without a working reversal is a genuinely bad combination. The other is a head whose control board is already intermittent, where adding a controller simply gives the fault a new way to present.

### Getting a signal to a garage the house cannot see

This is the practical obstacle in this city, not the electronics. Detached garages sit at the back of the lot, often across a yard and behind masonry, and the router is at the front of the house. The fix is a mesh node or an access point in a covered outdoor location or in the garage itself, and the cost of that should be part of the conversation before the module is ordered.

### Cold, damp and the enclosure

An unheated garage cycles through the entire Michigan year, and consumer electronics screwed to its ceiling cycle with it. That is the ordinary state of an Ann Arbor garage from December to March. Mounting the module high, away from the door's air movement, keeping connections out of the drip line and using proper insulated connectors rather than bare twists is most of the reliability.

### Rentals, cameras and permission

## svc_torsion_spring_conversion_lede

Converting a door from extension springs to a single torsion assembly changes where the load sits and how the door behaves when a spring finally breaks. On older Ann Arbor garages it is often the most useful upgrade available short of a new door.

## svc_torsion_spring_conversion_body

### What you have now, and how to recognize it

Extension springs run horizontally along the tracks on both sides of the door, stretching as the door comes down. Torsion springs sit on a shaft above the opening and store energy by twisting. DASMA's guidance on one-piece doors describes the older arrangement directly: the counterbalance usually comprises extension springs attached near the bottom corners of the door, and inspectors are told to verify that the springs are contained by a secure cable running through the center of the springs. That containment cable is the tell. If it is missing on your door, a spring failure sends a length of loaded steel across the garage rather than leaving it hanging on the cable, and that alone is a reason to look at the counterbalance. The rest of the spring work is on the [service index](../services/).

### Why a torsion conversion is worth the money

Three concrete gains. The load moves to a shaft anchored above the opening, so the door hangs from a single balanced system instead of two independent springs that inevitably drift out of match with each other. A door balanced on a torsion shaft holds its position through the full travel, which makes the hand test meaningful and lets an opener run at a lower force. And a torsion spring can be specified by cycle rating, so the same door can be built for a longer service life instead of taking whatever the original builder fitted. The side tracks also lose the spring hardware, which frees the space along the walls that extension springs occupy.

### Headroom decides whether it is possible

The measurement that governs is the clear space between the top of the opening and the ceiling or joists. A standard torsion assembly wants roughly 12 inches. Low-headroom kits use a double track and reduce that requirement, and where there is genuinely no room a wall-mount jackshaft operator driving a torsion shaft placed beside the opening becomes the answer. This is the routine obstacle on old stock. The city's own downtown housing needs assessment recorded 31.3 percent of downtown rental units as built in 1949 or earlier, and the American Community Survey table for the city puts the median year a housing unit was built at 1971, so a great many garages here were framed with no thought of a torsion tube. A 1936 Cape Cod garage of the kind documented on Gardner Avenue in Lower Burns Park is a typical measurement problem.

### The header has to carry it

A torsion assembly transfers the entire counterbalance load into the wall above the door through two or three anchor points. DASMA's inspection guidance requires that torsion spring anchor pads be fastened to structurally sound load-bearing members, which on an old garage means opening up what is behind the drywall or the boards rather than trusting a nailed-on ledger. In the detached garages behind the houses of the Old West Side, the header is often a light built-up member sized for a door half the weight of a modern insulated one. Where the framing will not take it, the honest answer is to add a proper spring pad or reframe the header as part of the job, and to price that openly, rather than to fasten into whatever the drill finds.

### What the conversion includes

The extension springs, pulleys, cables and their brackets come out. A shaft is sized to the opening, bearing plates and a center bracket are fixed into sound framing, springs are specified by the door's actual measured weight and the cycle life wanted, and new lift cables and drums are fitted and set. Then the door is balanced by hand before the opener is reconnected, and the operator's force and limits are reprogrammed to the changed door. The door's weight should be measured rather than estimated from a chart; a door with a replacement bottom section or an insulation kit added weighs something no chart knows. ## q_garage_door_opener_not_working_lede

"Opener not working" is four or five different problems wearing the same complaint, and the fix depends on exactly what the motor does when the button is pressed.

## q_garage_door_opener_not_working_body

### Press the wall button first, not the remote

If the wall-mounted button inside the garage does nothing either, the problem is upstream of the remote entirely: power to the unit, a tripped breaker, or the operator itself. If the wall button moves the door but the remote does not, the fault is narrower and usually cheaper -- most remotes use a rolling code that can fall out of sync after a battery change or a signal interruption, and the fix is a resync, not a replacement.

### The motor runs but the door doesn't move

A motor that hums, clicks, or runs its full cycle sound without the door actually traveling usually means the trolley has disengaged from the carriage, often from the emergency release cord being pulled or bumped. Pulling that cord manually reconnects the door to the track hardware -- it does not move the door itself, and a door that will not budge by hand afterward is worth stopping on, since that points at something under tension rather than a disengaged trolley.

### The door reverses before it closes, or won't close at all

Openers built since January 1993 are required to reverse within 2 seconds of hitting an obstruction and carry secondary entrapment protection, per 16 CFR 1211 -- and the photo-eye sensors near the floor are the most common reason a compliant opener refuses to close. Direct sunlight hitting the sensor at the wrong angle, especially low winter sun, or a spiderweb and dust buildup across the beam, both read to the opener as an obstruction. Wipe both lenses and check that they still show a solid LED with nothing between them before assuming a bigger fault.

### It worked yesterday and does nothing today

Total, sudden failure with no warning points at power more often than mechanics: a tripped GFCI outlet in the garage, a breaker, or in cold weather a battery backup unit that has failed and no longer bridges a brief outage. Ann Arbor averages 122 days a year below freezing, and cold hits battery capacity hardest right when a backup unit is being asked to do its job -- worth checking before assuming the motor itself has failed.

### What this actually routes to

A dead remote with a working wall button is a remote and keypad repair. Photo-eye sensor failures are a sensor repair. A disengaged trolley that won't reconnect, or a motor that runs but strains, is an opener repair. All three get diagnosed on the same visit if you're not sure which applies -- the details above are for narrowing it down before the call, not for skipping the inspection.

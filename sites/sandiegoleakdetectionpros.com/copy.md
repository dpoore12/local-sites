## symptom_1_title

The meter keeps showing water use after the property is quiet

## symptom_1

An active meter when the property is supposed to be still is a better starting clue than a bill. Shut off or pause ordinary water use first: faucets, toilets that may be cycling, washing equipment, ice makers, irrigation programs, pool auto-fill, and any hose connection. Then watch the leak indicator or low-flow dial for several quiet minutes. Continued movement means water is passing through the meter on the customer side. It does not tell you whether the loss is a toilet, irrigation valve, service line, pool fill, indoor supply pipe, or another component.

The practical next step is separating systems, not picking a location from the meter alone. A technician may close the house-side shutoff, isolate irrigation, inspect pool equipment, compare pressure before and after a controlled interval, and check accessible fixtures. If the meter settles when one system is isolated, that directs the rest of the work. If it does not, the route may need acoustic listening, pressure testing, or tracer methods depending on the pipe material and access.

San Diego’s terrain makes a pressure reading worth taking alongside the meter test. High static pressure can expose a weak valve, connector, appliance hose, or small supply-line failure; a regulator that is not holding can create a similar pattern. But a high number on a gauge is not proof of a hidden leak. The technician needs to see both static and flowing pressure, how the meter behaves, and what changes when sections are shut off. Do not leave a suspected active loss running while waiting for a visit. If water is entering the house or is close to electrical equipment, keep clear of the area and arrange urgent help.

## symptom_2_title

A floor edge, wall base, or yard area stays damp without an obvious source

## symptom_2

A damp baseboard, lifted flooring edge, soft spot beside a path, or patch of soil that remains wet after irrigation is off deserves a methodical check. Water follows framing, compacted fill, conduits, and slope, so the place where it shows up is not automatically the point where a pipe failed. In a San Diego house, moisture can come from an indoor supply line, a drain connection, a shower enclosure, a refrigerator line, a condensate drain, roof or window intrusion, irrigation, pool equipment, or a buried service route. The goal is to rule out the easy visible paths before opening a finish.

Start by protecting the area. Move absorbent belongings, photograph the edges, and mark a spreading boundary lightly with removable tape or pencil. Do not drill into a wall, pull up flooring, or dig beside a suspected line to “see where it goes.” Avoid outlets, cords, and appliances in the wet area. If water is actively reaching electrical equipment, stay out of the area rather than trying to shut it off while standing in moisture.

The test sequence depends on the clue. Indoor work can include checking accessible connections, isolating hot and cold supply, pressure testing a closed section, moisture measurements, acoustic listening, or thermal imaging when conditions support it. Outside, the technician looks at irrigation zones, grade, valves, the service route, and whether the meter or gauge responds while the suspected system is isolated. A wet yard can also be drainage rather than a pressurized leak. The useful result is not simply “there is moisture.” It is an explanation of which system the evidence supports, the area that still needs confirmation, and the least disruptive repair access once a repair is approved.

## symptom_3_title

Pressure is weak, surging, or unusually forceful at several fixtures

## symptom_3

Pressure trouble across several fixtures needs testing before anyone calls it a leak. One weak shower or slow kitchen faucet often begins at that fixture: an aerator, cartridge, stop valve, filter, or local obstruction. Whole-house changes point in more directions. A failing pressure regulator can restrict flow or allow pressure to climb. A partly closed main valve, treatment equipment, shared meter conditions, a service-line issue, or an active leak can also change what you feel at a tap.

This is especially relevant in San Diego. City Public Utilities says pressure differs by location and demand, and California plumbing rules require a property regulator where local pressure exceeds 80 psi. A home on one side of a mesa or canyon should not assume a gauge reading from another neighborhood applies at its meter. The regulator belongs to the property owner, so a failing one can be mistaken for a city problem or a hidden pipe failure. The right question is not whether the water feels “strong.” It is what the gauge says when water is still, what it does when fixtures run, and whether the regulator holds the setting.

At the visit, the technician checks static and flowing pressure, identifies the regulator and shutoffs, compares hot against cold, and looks for restrictions at filters or treatment equipment. Isolation testing can show whether a pressure loss follows the house plumbing, irrigation, pool equipment, or a particular branch. Do not turn a regulator upward to make a weak shower feel better until the cause is known. Extra pressure can strain old fixture connections and hide the evidence needed to diagnose a regulator problem. Likewise, a lower gauge reading does not automatically mean a buried leak. The proposed repair should follow the tested component, not a shortcut based on one faucet.

## symptom_4_title

The pool level falls faster than the conditions can explain

## symptom_4

Pool water changes with sun, wind, splash-out, filter cleaning, and normal evaporation, so a low level alone does not identify a leak. Start with a simple comparison. Mark the pool waterline and place a bucket filled with pool water on a step, with its water level close to the pool level. After a day, compare the drop while noting whether the pump ran and whether the automatic fill system was active. If the pool loses more than the bucket, a leak is possible. It is still only a screening check, because weather, swimmers, a backwash setting, and auto-fill behavior can distort the result.

The next clue is whether the rate changes with equipment operation. A larger loss while the pump runs can point toward pressure-side plumbing after the pump. Loss that continues with the system off can involve a suction or return line, skimmer, light niche, fitting, shell condition, or an auto-fill component. Air in the pump basket, unusual bubbles at returns, wet soil around the equipment pad, or filter-pressure changes are useful observations, not final proof.

In coastal San Diego, exposed metal around pool, irrigation, and backflow equipment merits a close visual inspection. The City’s water-facility guidance recognizes airborne salt and wet-dry chloride cycling as a corrosion risk for exposed metal surfaces. That does not mean every pool leak is corrosion, and it says nothing about a hidden line by itself. It does explain why fittings, valves, clamps, and equipment connections should be checked before a deck is disturbed.

A proper pool-leak visit separates the vessel, plumbing, and operating equipment. Depending on the symptoms, testing can include equipment-pad inspection, pressure isolation, dye testing at accessible fittings, listening methods, and line tracing. Do not run a pump if the water level is below the safe operating level for the equipment. The aim is to narrow the suspected path first, then discuss a repair that targets that path rather than opening deck on speculation.

## qa_1_question

Why is a pressure regulator important for San Diego leak detection?

## qa_1_answer

San Diego Public Utilities says water pressure changes with location and demand, and it notes that a regulator is required when local pressure exceeds 80 psi. The regulator is on the property owner’s side of the responsibility line. When it fails open, fixtures may suddenly see more pressure than they were designed to handle; when it fails closed or drifts, flow can fall across the house. Either pattern can be confused with a hidden leak. A technician checks the gauge under static and flowing conditions, the regulator’s behavior, and the meter response before proposing repair. A neighborhood pressure reading is useful context, but it is not a substitute for testing the plumbing at the actual address.

## qa_2_question

Does San Diego’s terrain make one neighborhood’s pressure reading irrelevant to another?

## qa_2_answer

It can. The City says pressure varies by location and demand, and its water system is organized around pressure zones. A coastal flat, a home above a canyon, and a property on a mesa can sit under very different conditions even within the same service area. That is why a technician reads the pressure at the property, then checks what happens when water is flowing and when sections are isolated. The gauge helps distinguish a supply-side condition, a regulator issue, a restriction, and a possible leak. It should never be used alone to declare that a buried pipe has failed. The repair plan follows the test results at the property, not a general rule about a ZIP code.

## qa_3_question

Could an older sewer lateral be part of a moisture or drainage problem?

## qa_3_answer

Possibly, especially when the evidence includes drain trouble, odor, repeated backups, or moisture that does not match meter activity. The City explains that older laterals of concrete, clay, or cast iron can develop root intrusion or deteriorated sections, and that a property owner is generally responsible for the lateral to the City main. A sewer-lateral issue is not diagnosed by a water-pressure test, and a water leak is not diagnosed by a drain backup. The first job is identifying which system fits the evidence. If the clues point to drainage, a camera or cleanout inspection may be more useful than pressurizing a water line. That distinction keeps the investigation from opening the wrong surface or approving the wrong repair.

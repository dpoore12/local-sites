## symptom_1_title
Standing water after a pipe, appliance, or toilet overflow

## symptom_1
A supply line that lets go, a washing-machine hose, a refrigerator line, or an overflow can put a surprising amount of water into a room before anyone sees it. The first job is to stop the source if it is safe: close the fixture stop valve, the appliance valve, or the house main. Do not keep running a toilet that is backing up. If water is near outlets, a power strip, or an appliance, stay out of it until power to that area can be shut off safely.

Then the work is physical, not cosmetic. Standing water is extracted from hard floors and carpet. A meter checks baseboards, lower drywall, cabinets, flooring edges, and the room below. Carpet can sometimes be lifted and dried with air forced underneath it; the pad commonly does not survive a heavy saturation and is removed. Drywall with intact seams and clean-water exposure may dry in place with targeted airflow. Swollen drywall, loose seams, saturated insulation, and cabinet backing that cannot be reached are different: they are opened or removed so the framing can actually dry.

Drying equipment normally stays until daily or return moisture readings show the materials have returned to an appropriate dry target, often several days rather than one overnight run. The 24-to-48-hour window matters because wet materials need prompt drying to reduce mold risk; it is not a guarantee that mold appears the moment a clock changes. For a sudden internal discharge, most Texas homeowner and renter policies commonly provide coverage, subject to the actual policy and deductible. Photograph the source and the damage before cleanup where you can do so safely, then focus on getting the water out.

## symptom_2_title
Water came in from outside, a creek, street, or heavy rain

## symptom_2
Water that entered from outside is a flood-restoration problem, not simply a broken-pipe drying job. In Austin, that distinction deserves attention. Creeks and drainage routes can change quickly in heavy rain, and a low room, garage, crawlspace, or ground-floor unit may take in water even when the plumbing inside is fine. Do not drive through or walk into moving water to check a property. Once conditions are safe and the water has stopped rising, document the water line, exterior entry point, and affected rooms before anything is discarded.

The restoration approach starts by treating the water as potentially contaminated. Technicians use protective gear, remove remaining water, separate the affected area from clean rooms where the work requires it, and clean exposed hard surfaces before drying. Saturated carpet, pad, cellulose insulation, ceiling tile, and swollen drywall often have to be removed after an outside-water event because drying the visible face does not make the concealed material sanitary or dry. Wet framing, concrete, masonry, and some hard floors may be dried in place if readings and condition support it. Air movers and dehumidifiers remain in place until the structural materials, not just the room air, are dry.

Coverage is a separate question from the emergency work. Texas insurance guidance says most home policies do not cover flood damage; flood coverage is generally separate. That is why the source needs to be recorded accurately rather than called a “leak” after the fact. Restoration should begin as soon as it is safe, while the claim and policy questions are handled through the insurer. Austin’s FloodPro map can help a property owner understand mapped floodplain and drainage information, but it does not change what happened inside the house that day.

## symptom_3_title
Sewage backup, black water, or a toilet that will not stop backing up

## symptom_3
A sewage backup is not a mop-and-fan job. If a toilet is overflowing with waste, a drain is pushing dirty water back into a shower or tub, or water smells like sewage, keep people and pets out of the area. Do not run the washing machine, dishwasher, showers, or additional toilets until the plumbing cause is addressed; adding more water can make the backup travel farther. Avoid using household fans, too. Moving contaminated droplets through the house makes a small cleanup larger.

The affected area is isolated before removal work begins. Technicians wear appropriate protective equipment, remove contaminated water, bag porous contents that cannot be cleaned safely, and clean and disinfect hard surfaces. Carpet, pad, upholstery, paper goods, insulation, and drywall that absorbed sewage usually cannot be restored in place. Baseboards and lower wall sections may need to come off so wet insulation, plate framing, and the back of the wall can be assessed. Cabinets are evaluated from the toe-kick and backing outward; a surface that looks fine can hide contaminated water beneath it.

After cleaning, the remaining structure is dried with measured equipment. Containment stays in place while removal and cleaning are underway, then drying continues until moisture readings support closing the area back up. A plumbing repair and a restoration job are connected but separate: clearing the line stops the source; removing contamination and drying the building addresses what the backup already touched.

Some homeowners policies include limited coverage for water or sewage entering through sewers or drains, while others exclude it or require an added endorsement. That is policy-specific. The useful immediate action is to preserve photos, keep damaged materials available until the adjuster gives direction where possible, and prevent anyone from treating the water as clean.

## symptom_4_title
Wet drywall, a soft baseboard, ceiling stain, or a musty room with no puddle

## symptom_4
Not every water job starts with a floor full of water. A slow drip behind a refrigerator, an upstairs bathroom leak, a failed shower pan, or a line inside a wall can show itself as a soft baseboard, paint that bubbles, a ceiling stain, cupped flooring, or a room that suddenly smells musty. By the time the surface changes, water may already be inside a wall cavity, beneath flooring, or in insulation. Turning up the air conditioning or putting a fan in the doorway does not tell you whether those materials are dry.

The inspection uses moisture readings to map beyond the visible edge. Baseboards may be removed to check the bottom plate and drywall. Small access openings can reveal wet insulation or a plumbing path. Thermal imaging may help direct the inspection, but a meter confirms moisture. The goal is to keep removal limited to material that cannot be dried or cleaned, while opening enough to avoid sealing moisture into the wall.

For clean water caught early, drywall with no swelling and intact seams can sometimes be dried in place. Air movers are positioned to move air across wet surfaces or into an opened cavity, and dehumidifiers pull the released moisture from the air. Delaminated drywall, saturated insulation, buckled laminate, and particleboard cabinets that have swollen are normally removed because their structure does not return after drying. Ceiling drywall that is sagging should not be touched from below; keep the area clear because it can fall.

Equipment remains until follow-up readings show drying has reached the target. If materials have been wet longer than about 24 to 48 hours, or there is visible growth or a persistent odor, the scope may include containment and removal rather than drying alone. The important distinction is not whether a stain looks old. It is whether moisture is still present and the source has been corrected.

## qa_1_question
Is this a flood claim or a burst-pipe claim?

## qa_1_answer
The source, not the amount of water, is the starting point. A sudden internal release — such as a burst pipe, broken washer hose, or toilet overflow — is commonly covered by Texas homeowner and renter policies, subject to the policy terms. Water that enters from outside after rain, creek overflow, or street flooding is flood damage, which most home policies do not cover without separate flood coverage. A long-term leak is another category and is often excluded. Do not relabel the event to make it fit a policy. Photograph the source, water line, and rooms, keep receipts, and ask the insurer how your actual policy applies. Restoration work still needs to start promptly: extraction, controlled removal, and drying are about preventing the building from getting worse, not deciding coverage.

## qa_2_question
How long will drying equipment stay in the house?

## qa_2_answer
There is no responsible one-number answer. A small clean-water spill on tile may take little more than extraction and a short drying period. A wet wall, carpeted room, cabinet run, or ceiling cavity can take several days because materials dry at different rates and moisture can travel under flooring or behind a baseboard. Air movers accelerate evaporation; dehumidifiers remove that moisture from the air. Equipment stays until meter readings show the affected materials are dry enough, not until the room stops feeling damp. The EPA recommends drying water-damaged areas and items within 24 to 48 hours to help prevent mold growth, but that is a prevention target, not a promise that all material can stay. Swollen drywall, soaked insulation, and contaminated porous material may need removal instead of more drying time.

## qa_3_question
What gets removed and what can be dried in place?

## qa_3_answer
Cleanliness of the water, how long it sat, and what the material is made of decide that. Clean water caught quickly may allow carpet, hardwood, sound drywall, framing, and some cabinets to be dried with extraction and measured equipment. Carpet padding, swollen particleboard, delaminated drywall, and saturated insulation are commonly removed because they do not dry reliably or regain their structure. With sewage or outdoor floodwater, porous materials that absorbed water are usually removed rather than treated as salvageable. Hard, non-porous surfaces can often be cleaned and dried. The scope should identify the source category, the materials that will remain, the materials that will be removed, where containment is needed, and the readings that will be used to decide when drying is complete.

## pricing_body

### Contamination sets the price before square footage does

ANSI/IICRC S500 sorts water by contamination rather than volume, and that sorting moves the number first. Clean supply-line water, gray water from an appliance overflow, and grossly contaminated water are three different jobs on one floor plan. Austin firms publish the gap plainly: roughly three to four dollars per square foot for clean water, four to six and a half for gray plus a stated contamination markup, and seven and up for the contaminated category with a larger markup and material replacement. Two nuances get lost online. Category can shift with time and temperature, so response speed is a cost factor. And the standard body has stated that wind-driven rain is not automatically the worst category; storm water is assessed, not assumed.

### Square feet times a rate, plus equipment days

Most posted pricing here is a rate, not a total, so both arithmetic steps behind the table are worth showing. A 500 square foot affected area at three to eight dollars per square foot is $1,500 to $4,000 of mitigation. Drying equipment bills by the day on top: three air movers and one dehumidifier for four days runs about $448 at posted low daily rates with a $65 setup charge, or about $1,031 at high rates with a $175 setup charge. That is why an invoice looks nothing like the rate someone read online. Duration is not arbitrary either: published drying times run one to two days for tile, three to five for drywall, and five to seven for hardwood that often gets replaced anyway.

### The Austin specifics that change a loss

Expansive clay soils move with drought and downpour, and a slab-on-grade home with no basement lets water spread laterally across a wide floor area instead of collecting in one low room. Affected square footage climbs fast on a small leak, so wall cavities and cabinet toe kicks get checked before anyone signs off. Source matters as much: a meaningful share of city land sits in floodplain and flash floods are fast, so a scope establishes where water came from before deciding how clean it was.

### Permits, and the utility bill nobody expects

Mitigation is not permitted work, but the repair that caused the loss often is. The failed supply line, drain, water heater connection or sewer lateral is plumbing work, and the City of Austin homeowner permitting fees, fiscal year 2026 schedule lists a residential plumbing permit at $200.43, up from $184.70. Reconstruction touching structure, plumbing or electrical carries its own permit and review time. Then there is the water that ran: a leak unnoticed for a billing cycle shows up as a spike, and the utility publishes an adjustment path under City Code section 15-9-141, requested within 90 days of the repair with the paid invoice, account number, service address and dates of the high bills.

### Two contracts, not one

Drying a house and rebuilding it are separate scopes, often separate contracts. Posted mitigation figures stop at extraction, containment, drying and monitoring; they exclude drywall, paint, flooring and trim. Reconstruction is priced after the space is dry, and local rebuild ranges run from one room to several to structural work.

### Reading a restoration estimate

A complete estimate states the water category and the reasoning, the measured affected area, and the evaporation load. It lists equipment by type and count with a daily rate and expected days, not one drying charge. It says what is removed and hauled away, whether flood cuts are planned and to what height, and who signs off that the structure is dry, since that signature starts a rebuild. National averages describe a modest clean-water loss in a house with a basement in a mild climate; they omit the plumbing permit, the floor area a slab gives water to travel, the extra equipment days hardwood demands, and the rebuild that follows a serious loss.

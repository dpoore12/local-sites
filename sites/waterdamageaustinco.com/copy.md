## pricing_lede

Water damage is priced in two halves that get confused constantly: mitigation, which is extraction, containment and drying, and reconstruction, which is putting the house back. The ranges below are built from prices Austin area restoration firms post publicly, converted where they are quoted per square foot or per equipment day, and set against the city and utility figures that surround a loss. None of it is a quote.

## pricing_body

### Contamination sets the price before square footage does

ANSI/IICRC S500 sorts water by contamination rather than volume, and that sorting moves the number first. Clean supply-line water, gray water from an appliance overflow, and grossly contaminated water are three different jobs on one floor plan. Austin firms publish the gap plainly. Clean water runs roughly three to four dollars per square foot. Gray water runs four to six and a half plus a stated contamination markup. The contaminated category starts at seven, with a larger markup and material replacement. Two nuances get lost online. Category can shift with time and temperature, so response speed is a cost factor. And storm water is assessed rather than assumed into the worst category.

### Square feet times a rate, plus equipment days

Most posted pricing here is a rate, not a total, so both arithmetic steps behind the table are worth showing. A 500 square foot affected area at three to eight dollars per square foot is $1,500 to $4,000 of mitigation. Drying equipment bills by the day on top: three air movers and one dehumidifier for four days runs about $448 at posted low daily rates with a $65 setup charge, or about $1,031 at high rates with a $175 setup charge. That is why an invoice looks nothing like the rate someone read online. Published drying times run one to two days for tile, three to five for drywall, and five to seven for hardwood.

### The Austin specifics that change a loss

A slab-on-grade home with no basement lets water spread sideways across a wide floor area instead of collecting in one low room. Affected square footage climbs fast on a small leak, so wall cavities and cabinet toe kicks get checked before anyone signs off. Source matters as much, so a scope establishes where water came from before deciding how clean it was.

### Permits, and the utility bill nobody expects

Mitigation is not permitted work, but the repair behind the loss often is. A failed supply line, drain, water heater connection or sewer lateral is plumbing work, and the City of Austin homeowner permitting fees, fiscal year 2026 schedule lists a residential plumbing permit at $200.43, up from $184.70. Reconstruction touching structure, plumbing or electrical carries its own permit and review time. Then there is the water that ran. A leak unnoticed for a billing cycle shows up as a spike. The utility publishes an adjustment path under City Code section 15-9-141, requested within 90 days of the repair. It wants the paid invoice, account number, service address and the dates of the high bills.

### Two contracts, not one

Drying a house and rebuilding it are separate scopes, often separate contracts. Posted mitigation figures stop at extraction, containment, drying and monitoring, and exclude drywall, paint, flooring and trim. Reconstruction is priced after the space is dry.

### Reading a restoration estimate

A complete estimate states the water category and the reasoning, the measured affected area, and the evaporation load. It lists equipment by type and count with a daily rate and expected days, not one drying charge. It says what is removed, whether flood cuts are planned and to what height, and who signs off that the structure is dry, since that signature starts a rebuild. National averages describe a modest clean-water loss in a house with a basement in a mild climate, and they omit the plumbing permit, the floor area a slab gives water to travel, and the rebuild that follows a serious loss.

## svc_water_extraction_lede

Extraction is the hour-by-hour part of a water loss. Pull the standing water, find how far it traveled behind the baseboards, and start the record an adjuster will read weeks later. On a slab house, water spreads sideways and quietly.

## svc_water_extraction_body

<h2>The first read on a wet floor</h2>
<p>Standing water is the visible part of the problem. What decides the next two weeks is where it went: under a cabinet toe kick, beneath a floating floor, into the bottom plate behind the baseboard, or down through the ceiling of the room below. Extraction begins with a moisture survey that marks the wet edge past the point you can see. That boundary is what both the drying plan and the claim file are built on. Drawing it after the wet material has already gone to the curb is guesswork, and it is the single most common reason a file falls apart later.</p>
<h2>Contamination is sorted before equipment is chosen</h2>
<p>The consensus document for this work is ANSI/IICRC S500, fifth edition, issued in 2021. It sorts water by contamination rather than by volume. The cleanest tier is described as water from a source that does not pose substantial harm to people, which covers a failed supply line, a tub overflow with nothing in it, or a toilet tank. That status is temporary, not permanent. The standard notes that clean water deteriorates as it mixes with soils and as hours pass, and that time and temperature affect how quickly contaminants multiply. A Friday night leak found on Sunday is no longer the job it was on Friday.</p>
<h2>Class describes evaporation, and it sets the schedule</h2>
<p>The same standard assigns a class based on how much wet surface area there is and how permeable the affected materials are when drying starts. The lowest class holds water on the surface of dense materials, with little or no wet carpet or cushion. The middle classes involve carpet, pad, and gypsum board across floors, walls, and ceilings, which describes most finished rooms here. The highest class is the one that stretches a timeline: deeply bound water in plaster, hardwood, concrete, masonry, stone, and closed structural cavities. That water evaporates slowly, and it needs specialty drying rather than more fans pointed at a wall.</p>
<h2>The deadlines the Texas Insurance Code sets for your insurer</h2>
<p>Chapter 542 of the Insurance Code puts a schedule on the carrier, and a homeowner who knows it can hold the file to it. Within 15 days of receiving notice of the claim, the insurer must acknowledge it, begin investigating, and request the items, statements, and forms it reasonably believes it needs. It then has until the 15th business day after receiving those items to accept or reject the claim in writing. If it cannot decide in that window, it must give its reasons and then decide no later than the 45th day. Payment is due within five business days of the notice of acceptance. Miss the schedule and the statute adds interest on the amount of the claim at 18 percent a year, plus reasonable attorney's fees. Those deadlines stretch by 15 days when the commissioner has designated a weather-related catastrophe.</p>
<h2>Do not run this one yourself</h2>
<p>Water sitting near outlets, a plugged-in appliance, or a submerged power strip is an electrical hazard first and a cleanup second. Leave it until power to that area is off. A sagging, water-loaded ceiling should not be poked from underneath, and the room below it should be emptied of people rather than furniture. Shop vacuums and box fans move a little water and a lot of humid air, which is how one wet room becomes three.</p>
<h2>The trap that produces a second visit</h2>
<p>Carpet that feels dry on top, and a baseboard that looks fine, both hide the same failure. Pad and gypsum give water up slowly, and the readings taken along the wall base at the end of the job are what separate a finished dry from a rebuild done over moisture. The Environmental Protection Agency puts the target plainly: dry water-damaged areas and items within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold growth, and control moisture, because there is no practical way to eliminate spores indoors. That window is a reason to move, not a reason to panic on hour 49.</p>
<h2>How the visit runs</h2>
<p>A technician confirms the source and whether it has actually stopped, then extracts standing water from hard surfaces and carpet. Wet material gets mapped with meters, photographed, and measured, and the affected square footage is written down. Unsalvageable porous material comes out under a documented scope. Air movers and dehumidifiers are then placed to match the wet surface area, and readings are logged on return visits until the materials hit the dry target. Keep every invoice, including the plumbing repair, because that paperwork is what turns an event into a paid claim. If the leak ran long enough to spike the water bill, Austin Water publishes an adjustment path under City Code section 15-9-141 that has to be requested within 90 days of the repair.</p>

## svc_basement_water_damage_restoration_lede

Very few homes here have a true basement, and that is exactly why below-grade water gets mishandled. A walkout lower level, a garden unit, a crawlspace, or a sunken storage room all trap water in the one place drying equipment works hardest.

## svc_basement_water_damage_restoration_body

<h2>What a basement means on an Austin lot</h2>
<p>Below-grade space in this market usually is not a full basement. It is a lower level on a lot that falls away toward a creek, a partially buried garage on a sloped drive, a crawlspace with a dirt floor, or a below-grade utility room holding the water heater and the electrical panel. Zilker and Allandale sit on very different ground from Onion Creek or East Cesar Chavez, and the drainage each lot gets is a matter of where the water goes after it leaves the roof. What all of these spaces share is a low point that water finds and cannot leave without help.</p>
<h2>Seepage is a coverage problem before it is a drying problem</h2>
<p>The restoration standard lists seepage driven by hydrostatic pressure, along with sump pump failures and condensate, in the middle contamination tier rather than the clean one. Gray water carries microorganisms and the nutrients they feed on. Left in place, that water can be reclassified into the grossly contaminated tier, and the standard notes 48 hours as the point where that reclassification becomes a live question. The insurance picture is just as unfriendly. The Texas Department of Insurance states that most home policies do not cover water damage from gradual leaks or seepage, and that the exclusion carries through to mold arising from it. Sudden and accidental damage, such as a burst pipe or a broken washer hose, is the version most policies do cover.</p>
<h2>Concrete holds water long after the floor looks dry</h2>
<p>A below-grade room is mostly dense material: slab, block, poured wall, stone, sometimes plaster. Those assemblies fall into the class of loss defined by deeply bound water and a low evaporation rate once the bulk water is gone. The practical effect is that the floor can read dry at the surface while the wall cavity behind furring strips is still wet, and a dehumidifier alone will not finish the job. Flooring installed over a slab that was never verified dry is how a homeowner pays for the same floor twice.</p>
<h2>The appraisal clause when the only fight is the number</h2>
<p>Sometimes coverage is agreed and the amount is not. Texas addressed that directly in 2025. Senate Bill 458 added Chapter 1813 to the Insurance Code, effective September 1, 2025, and it requires residential property and personal auto policies issued or renewed in this state to contain an appraisal provision. The chapter describes appraisal as a dispute resolution process used solely to determine the amount of loss when that amount is in dispute. The determination binds both sides, except in cases of fraud, accident, or material mistake. It is not a path for arguing whether the seepage was covered in the first place.</p>
<h2>Texas licenses mold work, and 25 square feet is the hinge</h2>
<p>The Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation licenses mold work in two separate lanes: assessment, meaning the people who inspect and test, and remediation, meaning the people who clean and remove. A single company may hold both credentials, but it is not allowed to perform assessment and remediation on the same project. The agency states that in most instances visible growth under 25 contiguous square feet can be cleaned up by people who are not licensed. At or above that area, licensed work and a filed notification come into play, and the notification carries a $25 fee. Water damage gets an explicit carve-out: when delay would increase contamination, the licensee may treat it as an emergency and file the notification by the following working day.</p>
<h2>The certificate that protects the next transaction</h2>
<p>When licensed remediation is performed, the contractor must issue a Certificate of Mold Damage Remediation no later than the tenth day after the project stop date. The licensed assessment consultant who performed the post-remediation evaluation co-signs it. The certificate states that the contamination was remediated and whether the underlying cause was corrected. A seller has to hand buyers copies of every certificate from the preceding five years. There is a reason to want one in the drawer: under the state's rules, a certificate showing the underlying cause was corrected prevents an insurer from making an underwriting decision on the home based on that previous mold damage or claim.</p>
<h2>What not to do down there</h2>
<p>Do not enter standing water in a room that houses the panel, a sump, or a gas appliance until power is off and the space is confirmed safe. Do not seal a damp below-grade wall with paint or a coating and call it solved. Do not throw out the failed sump pump or the split hose before it is photographed, because that part is the evidence for a sudden-failure argument.</p>
<h2>How the visit runs</h2>
<p>The visit starts outside, looking for where water enters, then moves to extraction, then to a meter survey of slab, walls, and any cavity behind them. Contents come off the floor. Porous material that will not recover is removed under a written scope, and the remaining assemblies are dried with equipment sized to a low evaporation rate, verified by readings rather than by feel.</p>

## svc_sewage_cleanup_lede

A drain backup is a contamination job that happens to involve water. The goal on day one is to isolate the area, stop anything else from draining into it, and keep the cleanup from spreading through the rest of the house on people's shoes.

## svc_sewage_cleanup_body

<h2>What the standard actually calls this water</h2>
<p>ANSI/IICRC S500 puts sewage in its most severe tier, describing water that is grossly contaminated and can contain pathogenic, toxigenic, or other harmful agents that can cause significant adverse reactions in people who contact or consume it. Two details in that definition matter in a house. A waste line backflow that originates beyond any trap counts, regardless of what the water looks like or what color it is. So does rising water from a river or stream. Color and odor are not the test. Origin is the test, and a clear-looking backup from beyond a trap is handled with the same protective measures as an obvious one.</p>
<h2>Stop adding water to the problem</h2>
<p>The most useful thing a homeowner does in the first ten minutes is turn things off. Every fixture that drains feeds the same line, so a washing machine cycle, a dishwasher, a shower, or a second toilet can push the backup farther into the structure while you are looking at the first mess. Keep people and pets out of the affected rooms. Resist the urge to aim a household fan at the floor, because that moves contaminated droplets and aerosols into rooms that were clean when the day started.</p>
<h2>What leaves the building, and why</h2>
<p>Porous material that absorbed contaminated water is generally removed rather than treated. That list usually includes carpet and cushion, pad-backed matting, upholstered pieces, paper goods, wet insulation, and gypsum board along the bottom of the wall. Cabinet boxes are judged from the toe kick and the back panel, not the door faces. Hard, non-porous surfaces can often be cleaned, disinfected, and dried in place. The order of operations is what a careless crew gets backwards: containment and removal come first, and structural drying comes after the contamination is gone, not at the same time.</p>
<h2>Where these backups come from in this city</h2>
<p>Some backups are entirely private, in the lateral between the house and the main, and some are not. The City Auditor's review of Austin Water reported roughly 4,000 miles of water mains and a main failure rate of 9.2 breaks per 100 miles, against an industry average of 25 and an optimized benchmark of 15. That is drinking water rather than wastewater, but it is a useful reminder that buried pipe fails on its own schedule. The utility's own history shows how fast a system event reaches a kitchen. A citywide precautionary boil water notice was issued on the evening of February 5, 2022 and rescinded late on February 8. A treatment error at the Ullrich plant had sent high-turbidity water to the filters. An earlier notice ran from October 22 to October 28, 2018. Flooding on the Lower Colorado had driven raw water turbidity from a typical 5 or 6 units to a peak of 387.</p>
<h2>Coverage here is written narrowly</h2>
<p>The Department of Insurance is blunt about backups: whether water or sewage entering through sewers or drains is covered depends on the policy, and some policies cover it while others require it to be added. Document the origin precisely and early. Photograph the fixture, the water line on the wall, and the affected rooms before anything is bagged, and keep the plumber's invoice describing what was found in the line. If porous contents are hauled away for health reasons, photograph and list them first.</p>
<h2>The notice Texas requires before you sue</h2>
<p>For a first-party residential claim, Chapter 542A adds a step most homeowners have never heard of. Written notice must go to the insurer no later than the 61st day before a suit is filed, and it is a prerequisite to filing. The notice has to state the acts or omissions complained of, the specific amount alleged to be owed, and any attorney's fees already incurred. Within 30 days of receiving it, the insurer may ask to inspect, photograph, and evaluate the property, and that inspection is to be completed by the 60th day where reasonably possible. A suit filed early can be dismissed without prejudice, and an insurer that never got a complying notice can seek abatement.</p>
<h2>Testing is not the fix</h2>
<p>If growth follows a backup, the federal guidance points at the water rather than the laboratory. The Environmental Protection Agency states that no federal limits have been set for mold or mold spores, and that airborne threshold values for mold do not exist, so a sample cannot show a building complies with a standard. The agency's advice is to correct the moisture source and clean up what grew. On health, stay with what the agency says: molds produce allergens and irritants, and contact or inhalation may cause allergic responses in sensitive people, including hay fever type symptoms and asthma attacks.</p>

## svc_flood_damage_restoration_body

<h2>Flood is a separate word to an insurer</h2>
<p>The Federal Emergency Management Agency states plainly that most homeowners insurance does not cover flood damage, and that flood coverage is bought as its own policy for the building, the contents, or both. There is usually a 30-day waiting period before a National Flood Insurance Program policy takes effect, with narrow exceptions tied to a lender requirement or a map change. The state consumer guidance closes the loop on the follow-on damage: mold from a flood is not covered, because the home policy does not cover the flood that caused it. None of that changes the physical work, and none of it is a reason to describe outside water as a leak.</p>
<h2>The rainfall numbers under this city were revised upward</h2>
<p>Design storms here are not what they were. Austin's watershed department explains that the local 100-year storm has been 10.2 inches of rain in 24 hours, while the updated federal rainfall study shows a 100-year storm exceeding 13 inches in parts of the city, an amount that resembles the old 500-year figure. The revision draws on 57 years of rainfall records collected since the previous study in 1961. The practical translation for a property owner is that a house can be outside a mapped boundary and still sit in the path of the storm that boundary was drawn for.</p>
<h2>Onion Creek and Shoal Creek, in numbers</h2>
<p>City records give the local history its scale. On Halloween in 2013, Onion Creek at US 183 crested at a record 41 feet after rising 11 feet between 6:15 and 6:30 in the morning, and 659 homes were damaged, 259 of them severely, with 15 destroyed. Closer to downtown, Shoal Creek carried a peak discharge of 16,000 cubic feet per second on May 24, 1981, after about 6 inches of rain, in a flood that killed 13 people and caused $35.5 million in damage. Later Shoal Creek peaks are smaller and still serious: 7,990 cubic feet per second in December 1991, 8,700 in November 2001, and 11,000 in May 2015. About 10 percent of land in the city lies in the floodplain, with more than 9,000 buildings in the 100-year floodplain and more than 300 bridges subject to flooding.</p>
<h2>The most dangerous mistake is made in a vehicle</h2>
<p>Roughly 75 percent of flood-related deaths in Texas happen in vehicles, according to the city's flood safety guidance. Driving around a barricade is not a technicality either: it is a Class B misdemeanor carrying up to 180 days in jail, a fine up to $2,000, or both. Do not return to a property while water is still moving across the route, and check the city's flood closure map before you go. Once the water drops, the loss will still be there, and so will the evidence.</p>
<h2>Storm water is assessed, not assumed</h2>
<p>Restorers do not automatically treat every drop of storm water as the worst category. The S500 consensus body issued a position statement making that explicit: top-down water ingress, wind-driven rain, and water from weather-related events are not automatically classified in the grossly contaminated tier. Rising water from rivers and streams is, and so is water that picks up contamination on its way inside. The determination looks at the originating source together with the quality of the water after it contacts the materials on site. That distinction decides what can be dried and what has to be cut out.</p>
<h2>Chapter 542A governs the forces-of-nature claim</h2>
<p>A flood, rainstorm, hail, or wind claim on residential property falls under Chapter 542A of the Insurance Code, which sets out its own pre-suit procedure. A policyholder must serve written notice at least 61 days before filing suit, and that notice is a prerequisite rather than a formality. It must state the acts or omissions at issue, the specific amount claimed as owed, and attorney's fees incurred, calculated from contemporaneous time records at a customary rate. The insurer may request an inspection within 30 days of the notice, to be completed by the 60th day where that is reasonably possible. Filing before the 61st day exposes the case to dismissal without prejudice.</p>
<h2>How the visit runs</h2>
<p>Access and safety are confirmed first, including whether the structure was inundated and whether power and gas were compromised. The water line is photographed at every wall before removal begins. Standing water is pumped and extracted, then porous material that absorbed outside water is cut out to a documented height, bagged, and logged. Hard surfaces are cleaned before drying starts. Dehumidification and airflow run against measured readings, and the file grows as it goes: origin, category reasoning, affected square footage, materials removed, equipment days, and the readings that closed it. That record is what the deadlines in the Insurance Code operate on.</p>

## svc_appliance_leak_cleanup_lede

Appliance leaks are the quiet category. A dishwasher, an ice maker line, or a washer connection puts water under a cabinet or behind a machine where nobody looks, and the cleanup scope is set by how many weeks it ran rather than by how much water is on the floor.

## svc_appliance_leak_cleanup_body

<h2>Four machines, four different failure modes</h2>
<p>A washing machine fails at the supply connection or the hose, and it fails at full line pressure, which is why it is the loudest of the group. A dishwasher usually fails at the door gasket, the pump seal, or the supply elbow under the tub, and the water goes straight under the cabinet run. A refrigerator ice line fails at the saddle valve or the plastic tubing behind the unit, dripping onto flooring that is trapped under a heavy appliance. Water softeners, filters, and under-sink reverse osmosis units fail at compression fittings and small plastic connectors. The first three wet a floor assembly. The last one usually wets a cabinet base first.</p>
<h2>The slow ones cost more than the fast ones</h2>
<p>The Environmental Protection Agency's WaterSense program reports that household leaks waste around 180 gallons a week, much of it from fixtures nobody thinks about. Austin Water puts a slow faucet or showerhead drip at up to 20 gallons a day on its own. Neither number sounds alarming until it lands in one place. Twenty gallons a day under a dishwasher for a month is enough to delaminate a cabinet base, swell a particleboard subfloor patch, and start growth inside a closed toe-kick space with no airflow. That is the actual reason appliance leaks turn into demolition jobs: not volume, but duration in a cavity that never dries.</p>
<h2>Hard water works on valves and connectors</h2>
<p>Austin Water reports system hardness averaging 93 parts per million as calcium carbonate, published as 5.4 grains per gallon, ranging up to 126 ppm. A USGS guide classifies 61 to 120 ppm as moderately hard and 121 to 180 as hard, and identifies calcium and magnesium as the compounds behind deposits in pipes and hot-water appliances. Fill valves, solenoids, and small screens are where that shows up first. A dishwasher inlet valve that no longer seats, or an ice maker valve that seeps after a cycle, is doing so with mineral help. Replacing the appliance without replacing the supply line and valve leaves the failure point in place.</p>
<h2>Where the water actually travels</h2>
<p>Cabinet toe kicks are closed boxes. Water that enters one spreads along the run under the cabinet floor, out of sight and out of the air. From there it follows the subfloor seam, and on a slab it wicks under vinyl plank or laminate for feet beyond the wet spot. In a two-story house the same leak reaches the ceiling below through the floor penetration for the supply line. Reading the floor in front of the appliance tells you almost nothing. Readings taken along the cabinet base, at the wall behind, and from the room below are what define the job.</p>
<h2>What insurance usually does with these</h2>
<p>The Texas Department of Insurance describes the split clearly: most home and renters policies cover sudden and accidental water damage, and it names a broken washing machine hose among its examples, while gradual leaks and seepage are generally excluded, along with mold that results from them. That means the same puddle can be a covered loss or a denied one depending on how long the leak ran and how the file describes it. Photograph the failed part before it goes in the trash, keep the appliance repair or replacement invoice, and let dated moisture readings establish the timeline rather than an estimate offered from memory.</p>
<h2>Salvage decisions in a kitchen</h2>
<p>A cabinet box with a swollen, crumbling particleboard base does not come back, and drying it in place wastes days. Solid plywood boxes usually do come back if the water is removed from underneath and air is introduced into the void. Vinyl plank and laminate over a wet slab trap the moisture they were installed to resist, so a section comes up to release it. Hardwood adjacent to the leak gets metered rather than judged by eye. Where a countertop and a sink cabinet must be preserved, drying through the toe kick with a small-diameter injection setup is the difference between a targeted repair and a new kitchen.</p>
<h2>How the visit runs</h2>
<p>Shut the valve serving the appliance, then pull the unit far enough to see the failure and photograph it. Extract water, then find the wet boundary with meters at the cabinet base, subfloor, wall, and ceiling below. Unsalvageable material comes out under a documented scope, with square footage recorded. Air moves into cavities, not just across the room, and dehumidification is sized to the wet surface. Readings are logged on each return visit until the assembly hits target. Replace the supply line and the valve while the space is open; the second leak in the same spot is almost always the part that was left alone.</p>

## svc_burst_pipe_water_damage_lede

A split supply line is a pressure event, not a drip. Sixty to eighty gallons an hour can run through a half-inch line until someone finds the shutoff, and in this city the split almost always happened during a hard freeze days before anyone saw water.

## svc_burst_pipe_water_damage_body

<h2>Why a freeze splits a pipe here</h2>
<p>Ice does not push a hole through copper at the coldest spot. It forms a plug, and continued freezing behind that plug drives pressure down the line until the weakest fitting, solder joint, or thin-wall section lets go. That is why the break is often several feet away from the frost, and why houses that lost a pipe still had water pressure right up until the thaw. The lines at risk in Austin are predictable: attic runs above insulation, garage walls, exterior hose bibs, and supply piping in uninsulated cavities on the north face. Homes built for a mild climate rarely have those runs buried deep in conditioned space, so a two-day cold snap reaches them.</p>
<h2>The freeze record this city actually has</h2>
<p>The National Weather Service office in New Braunfels documented pockets of one to two inches of combined sleet and freezing rain across parts of the Austin area on January 24 and 25, 2026, concentrated in Travis and Williamson counties, with parts of the region holding below freezing for more than 48 hours before warming through the middle of that week. NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information puts the February 2021 event in a different category again: central Texas, Austin included, sat at six to nine consecutive days of freezing temperatures, the longest such streak in the state's records, across a cold spell that ran nearly nine full days. Two days below freezing is enough to split a line. Six is enough to split several on the same street.</p>
<h2>The damage appears at the thaw, not in the cold</h2>
<p>A cracked line under pressure behind an ice plug leaks nothing. Water starts moving when the plug clears, which is often mid-morning on the warm-up day while the house is empty. That timing explains the pattern: an attic split runs down a wall cavity for hours, saturates insulation, loads the ceiling below, and reaches the slab before anyone gets home. Bottom plates, cavity insulation, and the underside of the roof deck all hold water that a surface reading misses. The wet edge gets mapped with meters before demolition, because the difference between one wet wall and four is invisible from the hallway and decides the entire drying plan.</p>
<h2>What Austin Water tells residents to do in the cold</h2>
<p>The utility's cold weather guidance is specific, and following it changes outcomes. Let one cold-water faucet run slowly, choosing the one farthest from the main shutoff so the drip pulls water through the length of the system. Open cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls. Leave the thermostat at 65 degrees or higher when the house is empty. If power goes out for more than a day, the utility says to stop dripping and shut the water off at the meter, since an unheated house plus running water is its own flood risk. If a pipe has already burst, close the shutoff valve first. When thawing, never use an open flame; the utility points to a hair dryer, heating pad, or warm towels, with a nearby faucet open and work moving from the faucet end back toward the frozen section.</p>
<h2>Where the shutoff is, and what it takes to use it</h2>
<p>The city shutoff sits in the meter box, usually near the sidewalk in front of the property line, and it takes a meter key rather than a hand. Hardware stores carry them for a few dollars. Boxes on the newer My ATX Water meters use a spring-loaded lid, which surprises people who expect to flip it open. Practicing this in October is worth more than any product sold in January, because the minutes between finding water and stopping it are the entire difference between a two-room dry-out and a whole-floor rebuild.</p>
<h2>Repair, replacement, and the permit line</h2>
<p>Austin's exemption list treats a repair and a replacement differently. Repairing a leak in a pipe, valve, or fixture without replacing or rearranging piping is exempt from permitting. Once a defective drain, waste, or vent line has to come out and go back in with new material, the city requires a permit and an inspection. Austin adopted the 2024 Uniform Plumbing Code along with the 2024 international codes, all effective July 10, 2025, so the standard a repipe is inspected against changed recently. Ask the plumber which path the work took, and keep that invoice. A drying file that cannot show when and how the source was stopped is a weak claim file.</p>
<h2>How the visit runs after a burst</h2>
<p>Verify the water is off and the split is isolated, then extract standing water and open the smallest amount of material that gets air into the wet cavity. Insulation that took water is removed rather than dried, since it holds moisture against framing and loses its rating. Wet framing, plates, and subfloor are metered and logged, dehumidifiers are sized against the wet surface area, and readings are taken on each return visit until materials hit target. Freeze events arrive in clusters, so equipment and crews get committed by written scope, address by address, rather than by phone order of arrival.</p>

## svc_carpet_water_extraction_and_drying_lede

Carpet is easy to extract and easy to get wrong. The decision that matters is not how much water comes up on the first pass, it is whether the pad and the subfloor under it can reach dry before the material becomes a disposal item.

## svc_carpet_water_extraction_and_drying_body

<h2>The pad decides the job</h2>
<p>Carpet face fibers release water reasonably well under a good extraction tool. The cushion beneath does not. Bonded urethane pad in particular holds water like a sponge and gives it up slowly, and in most losses the honest choice is to remove the pad and dry the carpet in place, or to float the carpet on air while the subfloor dries beneath it. Extraction over the pad, repeated until the water table drops, is the technique that keeps a carpet's warranty alive when the material is worth saving. Deciding that on readings rather than on preference is the whole skill.</p>
<h2>Clean water does not stay clean</h2>
<p>Water that started at a supply line becomes something else as it sits, mixes with soils in the carpet, and warms. That is why the same carpet is salvageable on hour four and a disposal item on hour sixty. Where the water was contaminated to begin with, from a drain, a sewer line, or the street, the pad and usually the carpet come out regardless of how good they look. There is no reliable way to restore a contaminated cushion in place, and the cost of trying it is a call-back with a smell nobody can locate.</p>
<h2>Street water is a different category</h2>
<p>Austin Watershed Protection reports over 1,100 miles of smaller ditches and drainage pipes handling stormwater ahead of the creeks, and notes that storm drain systems can be undersized in neighborhoods built before drainage rules took effect in the early 1980s, and in annexed areas. When a street ponds and water enters at a door sill, the carpet in that room took outdoor water, complete with whatever the pavement was carrying. That is handled as a removal and cleaning job, not a dry-in-place job, and the entry route gets photographed because the coverage treatment for surface water differs from the treatment for a broken pipe.</p>
<h2>Subfloor and tack strip are the real targets</h2>
<p>Under the carpet and pad sits the material that actually holds the schedule: plywood or oriented strand board on a framed floor, or concrete on a slab. Both give moisture up slowly, and both keep re-wetting the material above them if the drying stops too early. Tack strip that stayed wet loses its grip and its nails corrode. Bottom plates and the base of the drywall wick water up the wall and hold it, which is why the readings that end a carpet job are taken at the wall base and in the subfloor, not in the middle of the room.</p>
<h2>Equipment placement, not equipment count</h2>
<p>Air movers work by moving a thin layer of air across a wet surface at speed, and dehumidifiers work by removing the water that evaporation puts into the air. Too many fans with too little dehumidification produces a humid room and a slow dry. The Environmental Protection Agency's moisture guidance points to keeping indoor relative humidity below 60 percent and ideally in the 30 to 50 percent range, which is a reasonable operating target for a drying chamber as well. Windows stay closed, the air conditioning stays on, and containment keeps the effort concentrated on the wet rooms instead of the whole house.</p>
<h2>What the timeline usually looks like</h2>
<p>A single wet room with clean water, caught early, pad removed, and equipment sized correctly is typically a three-day dry with readings taken daily. Add a second and third room, wet wall bases, or a slab and the timeline stretches. Add contamination and it becomes a removal job first with drying after. What does not happen is a same-day finish. Equipment picked up early, before readings say the subfloor and plates are dry, is the single most common cause of a musty room a month later, and the second job is never cheaper than finishing the first one.</p>
<h2>How the visit runs</h2>
<p>Confirm the source is stopped, then extract from the carpet face and through the pad as the technique requires. Pull and dispose of pad where the water or the material says it goes, and detach and float the carpet where the subfloor needs air under it. Meter the carpet, pad, subfloor, tack strip line, wall base, and adjacent rooms, and record the affected square footage with photographs. Size dehumidification to the wet surface, log readings each visit, and reinstall or replace pad only after the substrate hits target. Keep the invoices, including the plumbing repair, in one file.</p>

## svc_ceiling_water_damage_repair_lede

A wet ceiling is a load-bearing problem before it is a cosmetic one. Gypsum board holds several pounds of water per square foot, sags at the fasteners, and lets go without warning, so the first decisions are about safety and where the water came from.

## svc_ceiling_water_damage_repair_body

<h2>Four sources, and they do not look alike</h2>
<p>Water reaching a ceiling comes from a roof or flashing failure, a supply or drain line in the floor above, an attic air conditioning unit or its condensate line, or a bathroom on the upper level. Each has a signature. A roof leak worsens during rain and quiets between storms. A supply leak runs constantly. A drain leak appears when a fixture upstairs is used. An air conditioning condensate problem shows up in cooling season, often as a stain directly under an attic air handler or along the drain line's path. The diagnosis is not academic, because a ceiling repaired without the source stopped is a repair scheduled to fail.</p>
<h2>The mechanical side has its own local rules</h2>
<p>Austin adopted the 2024 Uniform Mechanical Code together with the 2024 Uniform Plumbing Code and the 2024 international codes, all effective July 10, 2025, with those adoptions carried in City Code Chapter 25-12. Attic equipment, secondary drain pans, and condensate routing all fall under that framework. In practice the failures are mundane: a primary drain line clogged with biological growth, a secondary pan with no functioning drain or float switch, or a line discharging into the pan it was meant to protect. Any ceiling stain under an air handler deserves an inspection of the pan, the float switch, and the discharge point before the drywall discussion starts.</p>
<h2>Safety before diagnosis</h2>
<p>A ceiling that is sagging, bulging, or has water visibly pooling behind it gets the room below it cleared of people. Recessed cans, ceiling fan boxes, and junction boxes give water a direct path to energized wiring, so the circuit serving that area is switched off at the panel before anyone opens the assembly. A controlled relief hole made from a safe position drains the pocket and reduces the collapse risk, which is not the same thing as pushing a screwdriver up into a bulge while standing under it. Wet insulation multiplies the weight, and that weight is why these ceilings fail suddenly rather than gradually.</p>
<h2>What can be dried and what has to come out</h2>
<p>Ceiling board that is flat, firm, and still bonded at the fasteners can often be dried with airflow above it in the attic or joist space. Board that has sagged, softened, or lost its bond is removed. Blown or batt insulation over the wet area comes out, because it holds water against framing and against the back of the board while contributing nothing to drying. Joists, trusses, and the underside of the deck get metered, and those readings, not the appearance of the paint, set the schedule. Textured ceilings add a step: matching a knockdown or popcorn texture is a finish task, and it is held until the framing behind it is verified dry.</p>
<h2>Humidity control decides whether growth follows</h2>
<p>The Environmental Protection Agency's guidance on moisture is direct about the target: keep indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, and ideally between 30 and 50 percent, and act on condensation quickly. It notes a humidity meter can be bought for 10 to 50 dollars. An open, wet ceiling cavity in an Austin summer is a humidity problem the moment the assembly is opened, since attic air is far wetter than conditioned air. That is why dehumidification is part of the plan rather than an upsell, and why containment sometimes goes up to keep the drying environment separate from the rest of the house.</p>
<h2>The record that supports the repair</h2>
<p>Photograph the stain pattern before anything is opened, then photograph what is found above it, since the entry point and the stain are frequently in different places and that gap is the whole explanation of the loss. Log dated readings at the ceiling, at the framing, and in the room above. List removed insulation and board with square footage. Keep the plumbing, roofing, or mechanical invoice that stopped the source, because a ceiling repair invoice with no cause-of-loss record behind it is the hardest kind of claim to support.</p>
<h2>How the visit runs</h2>
<p>Make the area safe and confirm the source has been stopped. Relieve trapped water in a controlled way, remove wet insulation and unsalvageable board under a documented scope, and record measurements. Introduce air into the cavity and above the assembly rather than only into the room, and size dehumidification to the wet surface area. Meter framing on each return visit until it reaches target. Repair, texture, and paint come last, in that order, and the difference between a ceiling that looks right in two years and one that stains again is almost always whether anyone waited for the wood.</p>

## svc_commercial_water_damage_restoration_lede

On a commercial loss the water is only half the problem. The other half is that the building has tenants, customers, inventory, and a landlord, and the drying plan has to work around all of them without shutting the operation down for a week.

## svc_commercial_water_damage_restoration_body

<h2>The clock is revenue, not just materials</h2>
<p>A wet retail floor, a flooded server closet, or a saturated restaurant kitchen costs money by the hour whether or not anything else gets ruined. That changes the sequencing. Areas that keep the business running get extracted, contained, and dried first, work is often staged outside operating hours, and the plan defines which sections stay open. Containment is doubly important because it separates the work zone from the areas serving customers, controls noise and dust in adjacent tenant spaces, and keeps humid drying air out of areas holding inventory. A written sequence with times, rather than a general promise of urgency, is what makes that workable.</p>
<h2>Bigger buildings mean bigger bound-water problems</h2>
<p>Commercial construction concentrates the hardest drying materials. Concrete slabs and topping slabs, masonry and block walls, gypsum over metal studs, and closed chases hold water inside the material and give it up slowly, and heavy commercial flooring assemblies trap it. Sizing follows the affected surface area and the material class, which frequently means desiccant dehumidification and ducted air rather than a room full of portable units. Multi-level buildings add the vertical path: water leaving a fourth-floor restroom reaches three tenant spaces and an elevator pit, and the drying plan has to address each one.</p>
<h2>Backflow assemblies are a commercial obligation here</h2>
<p>Austin Water requires backflow prevention on connections including irrigation, industrial fluid and chemical uses, and auxiliary or reclaimed water supplies, and those assemblies must be inspected, maintained, tested, and reported annually. Testing has to be performed by a Backflow Prevention Assembly Tester licensed by the state environmental agency and registered with the utility, and the specific requirements for a building are set during the plumbing inspection in the permit process. This matters after a loss for two reasons: a failed or improperly installed assembly can be the source of contaminated water inside the building, and the annual testing record is part of the documentation a carrier or a landlord will ask to see.</p>
<h2>The codes the rebuild answers to</h2>
<p>Austin adopted the 2024 International Building Code, Fire Code, Existing Building Code, and Property Maintenance Code, together with the 2024 Uniform Plumbing and Mechanical Codes, all effective July 10, 2025, under City Code Chapter 25-12. Commercial reconstruction after a water loss touches several of those at once: fire-rated assemblies that were cut for drying access have to go back as rated assemblies, penetrations get firestopped, and mechanical and plumbing work follows the current adopted editions. That is a scope conversation to have during mitigation rather than after, because how a wall is opened affects what putting it back requires.</p>
<h2>Contents, records, and tenant property</h2>
<p>Commercial contents are rarely simple. Inventory, equipment, paper records, and electronics all have different handling, and in a multi-tenant building they may belong to different parties with different policies. Items get inventoried and photographed by location before they move, with tenant property tracked separately from the landlord's building materials. Electronics that took water are isolated and left for a specialist rather than powered up to test. Paper records that are wet and valuable are a stabilization problem with a short window, and the decision about freezing them to buy time gets made on day one or not at all.</p>
<h2>Coverage on the commercial side has different limits</h2>
<p>Where the water arrived from outside, flood coverage under the National Flood Insurance Program caps a non-residential building at 500,000 dollars with a separate 500,000 dollars for contents, and a non-residential unit can insure contents up to that same figure. Property owners who need more look to supplemental private policies. Commercial policies may also carry business interruption coverage, which turns the drying schedule into a documented financial matter, so daily records of equipment, access, and progress feed both claims at once.</p>
<h2>How the visit runs</h2>
<p>Walk the building with whoever controls access, confirm the source is stopped, and identify which areas must stay operational. Extract and pump, then set containment that separates work zones from occupied space. Meter every affected assembly, including the levels below the source, and photograph before removal. Remove unsalvageable material under a written scope with measurements. Size dehumidification and air movement to the wet surface and material class, log daily readings and ambient conditions, and report progress to the owner or property manager on a fixed schedule. Final readings against unaffected reference areas close the mitigation before reconstruction starts.</p>

## svc_crawl_space_water_removal_lede

A wet crawl space is a house problem, not a storage problem. The air under a pier and beam floor moves upward through the assembly, so standing water below the joists ends up as humidity, odor, and eventually damage in rooms nobody would connect to it.

## svc_crawl_space_water_removal_body

<h2>How water gets under the floor</h2>
<p>Three routes cover most of it. Surface water enters through a low grade, a downspout dumping beside the foundation, or a driveway that slopes back toward the house. A plumbing failure in the crawl space, most often a drain line or a supply line, releases water directly under the floor. And in some conditions groundwater rises into a shallow crawl space after a heavy rain. The routes matter because each one has a different fix: a grading and drainage correction, a plumbing repair, or a sump and vapor barrier strategy. Pumping the water out without identifying the route just schedules the next visit.</p>
<h2>Neighborhoods near a creek carry documented exposure</h2>
<p>City flood studies put real numbers on that exposure. Austin's Shoal Creek flood risk reduction summary reports 157 residential structures affected in the creek's 5-year floodplain, the area with a 20 percent chance of flooding in a given year. Structures in that band are not dealing with a rare event; they are dealing with a recurring one. For a house on piers in a corridor like that, a crawl space that takes water repeatedly is the expected condition rather than a surprise, and the response has to include drainage and a vapor strategy, not just an extraction visit each time it rains.</p>
<h2>Standing water becomes indoor humidity</h2>
<p>Warm air rises through a framed floor, and it carries whatever moisture is under the house with it. That is the mechanism behind musty odors in rooms with no visible water, and behind cupped floorboards over a wet crawl space. Federal moisture guidance points at keeping indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent, which is difficult to achieve while a pond sits under the floor. It is also why a crawl space job includes readings taken in the living space above, not only in the crawl space, and why the job is not complete when the water is gone but the wood is still wet.</p>
<h2>What gets checked while access is open</h2>
<p>A crawl space visit is one of the few chances to see the underside of the building. Joists, girders, and subfloor get metered for moisture and inspected for rot or fungal growth. Insulation between joists that took water is removed rather than dried, since wet batts hold moisture against framing and sag out of contact anyway. Ductwork gets checked for crushed sections and for condensation, since a poorly insulated duct in a humid crawl space produces water on its own. Piers, supports, and any evidence of prior water lines are photographed, and the vapor barrier condition is documented.</p>
<h2>Contamination changes the scope</h2>
<p>Water that arrived from a sewer line or from the street is contaminated, and a crawl space with contaminated soil is a removal and cleaning job before it is a drying job. Saturated debris, cardboard, wet insulation, and torn barrier material come out. Soil grading and a new vapor barrier are often the honest fix rather than an attempt to sanitize what is there. Protective equipment is not optional for that work, and access routes get planned so contaminated material is not dragged through the living space on the way out.</p>
<h2>Keeping it dry after the pump-out</h2>
<p>Long-term dryness comes from drainage and vapor control, not equipment. Downspouts extended away from the foundation, grade sloped away, and any interior source repaired are the first fixes. A properly lapped and sealed vapor barrier over the soil cuts moisture migration. A sump with a working pump handles water that still finds its way in. Ventilation strategy depends on the specific crawl space, and in a humid climate more outside air is not automatically an improvement, since humid air condensing on cool surfaces under the house creates the problem it was meant to solve.</p>
<h2>How the visit runs</h2>
<p>Confirm the source, then pump and extract standing water and remove saturated debris and insulation under a documented scope with photographs. Meter joists, girders, subfloor, and the flooring above, and record readings with locations. Place dehumidification and air movement in the crawl space, sized against the wet area, and monitor conditions in the living space at the same time. Return visits log readings until the framing reaches target against dry reference points. The drainage and vapor recommendations get written down, because the same crawl space flooding twice is a maintenance issue, not a restoration one.</p>

## svc_emergency_water_removal_lede

Emergency work is measured in gallons per minute and in hours. Get the water out, get the power situation safe, get air on the wet material, and record what the building looked like before any of it changed. Everything after that is scheduling.

## svc_emergency_water_removal_body

<h2>The first three minutes belong to the shutoff</h2>
<p>Nothing else matters while water is still arriving. Inside the house, the fixture stop or the main valve stops a supply failure. Outside, the city shutoff sits in the meter box near the sidewalk, usually just inside the property line, and it takes a meter key that hardware stores sell for a few dollars. The newer meter boxes here use a spring-loaded lid. On a water heater, closing the cold inlet stops the refill. Knowing which valve to reach for before there is water on the floor is worth more than any equipment on the truck, and it is the one part of this job a homeowner can complete alone.</p>
<h2>Power comes before pumps</h2>
<p>Standing water plus energized circuits is the hazard that puts people in hospitals. Austin Water's own winter guidance is blunt about not using electrical appliances while standing in water. Practically, that means the circuits serving the affected area go off at the panel before anyone wades in, and it means a submerged power strip, a plugged-in appliance, or a wet outlet is treated as live until proven otherwise. If the panel itself is in the wet area or the water is deep, that is an electrician or a utility call rather than a judgment call. Nobody should be moving equipment through standing water to get a fan running faster.</p>
<h2>Who to call when the source might not be yours</h2>
<p>Not every emergency is on the customer's side of the meter. Austin Water runs a dispatch line around the clock at 512-972-1000 for suspected utility problems such as a main break or a meter box full of water, while non-emergency reports made through 3-1-1 are investigated within one to three business days. That distinction matters at two in the morning: a suspected main issue is an immediate dispatch, and a slow seep near the sidewalk is a next-business-day report. Reporting it also creates a dated municipal record, which is useful later if the cause of loss is disputed.</p>
<h2>Extraction capacity is the whole game early on</h2>
<p>Truck-mounted and portable extraction moves water at a rate no shop vacuum approaches, and on volume losses submersible pumps come first. The order is bulk water out, then extraction from carpet and hard surfaces, then removal of material that is holding water the equipment cannot reach. Removing an inch of water from a floor in twenty minutes rather than three hours is what keeps a hardwood floor salvageable and keeps a wall cavity from wicking up past the outlets. This is also where a decision gets made about how much material has to be opened to get air into cavities, which is set by readings rather than by habit.</p>
<h2>Storm timing can be checked, not guessed</h2>
<p>When the water is coming from outside, timing information exists. The Lower Colorado River Authority operates a network of 275 automated weather and river gauges providing near-real-time readings across the basin, and it notes that a release at Mansfield Dam takes roughly an hour and a half to reach Tom Miller Dam. For a business or a homeowner deciding whether to move contents up or stage sandbags, that is actionable data. It also matters for entry decisions: a crew does not walk into a building with moving water around it or drive a flooded low water crossing to reach an address.</p>
<h2>Record first, remove second</h2>
<p>The photographs that decide a claim are the ones taken before the cleanup. Standing water against a fixed reference, the entry point, the failed component, the contents in place, and the affected rooms all get captured before anything is carried out. Removed material is measured and listed. Every dated moisture reading from the first visit sets the baseline the rest of the file is compared against. A crew that arrives, works hard for six hours, and leaves with no record has made the claim harder to pay even though the mitigation itself was correct.</p>
<h2>How the visit runs</h2>
<p>Confirm the source is stopped and the affected circuits are off. Pump and extract standing water, then map the wet boundary with meters and photograph the loss before removal begins. Take out unsalvageable porous material under a written scope with square footage recorded. Set air movement into cavities and dehumidification sized to the wet surface area, with containment where it shrinks the drying space. Log readings on the first visit and on every return until materials reach target. On multi-address events, work is committed by written scope in sequence, which is a slower answer than a promise but a more accurate one.</p>

## svc_hardwood_floor_water_damage_lede

Wood floors fail on a delay. The boards look fine on day one, cup by day three, and crown weeks later if they were sanded too early, so the decisions that save a hardwood floor all get made in the first 48 hours while it still looks salvageable.

## svc_hardwood_floor_water_damage_body

<h2>What water actually does to a board</h2>
<p>Wood takes on moisture unevenly. Water sitting on top or wicking from the subfloor swells the bottom of each board faster than the finished face, and the board rolls up at its edges, which is cupping. If that swollen wood is sanded flat before it releases the moisture, the surface goes concave in the other direction as it dries and the floor crowns. Adhesive-set floors can also release from the substrate, and engineered products can delaminate at the wear layer. None of that is visible in hour one. It is the reason a floor is metered rather than judged by eye, and the reason drying starts before anyone decides whether the floor is a loss.</p>
<h2>Where the water is hiding</h2>
<p>On a slab house the moisture is usually beneath the flooring, either in the adhesive layer or in the concrete itself, and concrete releases water slowly. On a pier and beam or framed floor, the wet zone includes the subfloor, the joists, and often the space below. Readings are taken through the board face and the subfloor where access allows, and a pin meter on an inconspicuous edge gives a real number instead of an impression. Baseboard, shoe molding, and the bottom plate along the affected wall are metered as well, because water that wicked into the wall base will keep feeding the floor edge for days.</p>
<h2>Specialty drying beats more fans</h2>
<p>Water bound inside dense material does not leave because air is blowing across the surface. Hardwood, plywood subfloor, and concrete give moisture up slowly, and the industry treats them as a separate drying problem from carpet and drywall. That is where floor drying mats, negative-pressure systems that pull moisture up through the board assembly, and tightly managed dehumidification earn their keep. The target is to remove water from the assembly faster than the boards can deform, without dropping the surrounding air so low that the top of the board dries and shrinks while the bottom stays wet. Aggressive is not the same as fast here.</p>
<h2>Humidity is the control variable, not the fan count</h2>
<p>The Environmental Protection Agency's moisture guidance sets a practical range for indoor conditions: below 60 percent relative humidity, and preferably between 30 and 50 percent. For wood, that range is not just a mold threshold, it is a dimensional stability question, since the board is trying to reach equilibrium with whatever air surrounds it. Drying a floor in an open house during an Austin summer, with humid outside air moving through, is fighting the equipment. Containment, closed windows, and functioning air conditioning are part of the plan, and a hygrometer in the room is worth more than an extra air mover.</p>
<h2>Freeze losses land hard on wood floors</h2>
<p>Multi-day freezes here produce upstairs and attic line failures, and the water they release runs down through floor assemblies. The National Weather Service office covering Austin documented pockets of one to two inches of combined sleet and freezing rain across parts of the metro on January 24 and 25, 2026, with parts of the area holding below freezing more than 48 hours. Those events send water into houses at the thaw, often while nobody is home, which means the floor has already been wet for hours before anyone starts extracting. Hours matter on wood more than on almost any other finish, so freeze-loss floors get metered early and often.</p>
<h2>Salvage, refinish, or replace</h2>
<p>Three outcomes are realistic. A floor dried promptly and held at the right conditions can flatten enough to need only a screen and recoat, or nothing at all. A floor that cupped but dried fully can often be sanded flat once readings say the boards are at a stable moisture content, which is a matter of weeks, not days. A floor that stayed wet, delaminated, or lifted off its adhesive is a replacement. Deciding among these requires readings taken over time. Anybody offering the verdict on day one, in either direction, is guessing, and both guesses cost money.</p>
<h2>How the visit runs</h2>
<p>Confirm the source is stopped, extract standing water, and remove baseboard and shoe where needed to release trapped water and let air reach the board edges. Meter the boards, subfloor, plates, and the space below where accessible, and record the wet footprint with photographs. Place specialty floor drying with dehumidification sized to the assembly, and hold the room's relative humidity in the target band. Log readings on each return visit until the wood reaches a stable moisture content in line with the unaffected areas. Any sanding or refinishing waits for that number, because the alternative is a crowned floor and a second bill.</p>

## svc_mold_prevention_after_water_damage_lede

Mold after a water loss is a moisture control failure, not bad luck. Spores are already in the building; what decides whether they grow is how wet the materials got, how long they stayed that way, and what the humidity did during the days that followed.

## svc_mold_prevention_after_water_damage_body

<h2>The window is short and the target is specific</h2>
<p>Federal guidance on moisture is unambiguous about acting fast, and it is equally specific about conditions. The Environmental Protection Agency advises keeping indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, with 30 to 50 percent as the preferable range, and notes that a humidity meter can be purchased for 10 to 50 dollars. It also emphasizes responding quickly to condensation and wet surfaces. Those two ideas together are the whole prevention strategy: get the materials dry quickly, and hold the air dry while you do it. Everything else, including the products people buy, is secondary to those two numbers.</p>
<h2>Spores are not the target, water is</h2>
<p>There is no way to eliminate mold spores from indoor air, and no product application changes that. Spores are present in ordinary house dust and in outdoor air that comes in every time a door opens. They germinate when they land on a material with enough available moisture for long enough at a suitable temperature. That is why prevention is a drying and humidity job rather than a spraying job, and why an antimicrobial applied to a wet surface that stays wet accomplishes very little. Removing the water source, removing unsalvageable wet porous material, and driving the assembly to a stable dry state is the intervention with evidence behind it.</p>
<h2>The places growth actually starts</h2>
<p>Growth begins where moisture, organic material, and still air overlap. In practice that means the paper facing on the back of drywall, the underside of a carpet pad, the base of a wall cavity behind a baseboard, cabinet toe kicks, the top of a bottom plate, wet fiberglass insulation, and the back side of a vinyl-faced material that is trapping moisture. All of those are hidden, which is the point. A room that looks dry with a wet plate behind the baseboard is a room that will develop an odor in a few weeks, and the odor is usually the first thing anybody notices.</p>
<h2>Cleaning wet material is not the same as drying it</h2>
<p>Wet porous material that took contaminated water comes out. Wet porous material that took clean water may be salvageable, decided by readings and by how long it sat, not by appearance. Semi-porous and non-porous surfaces are cleaned and dried. The order matters: clean first, then dry, because moving air across a soiled wet surface distributes what is on it. Where material is removed, cuts are made to a line set by readings and the debris is bagged and hauled out on a planned route rather than carried through the whole house.</p>
<h2>What insurance typically does and does not cover</h2>
<p>The Texas Department of Insurance sets out the usual pattern. Most home and renters policies cover sudden and accidental water damage and will cover mold that appears on an item damaged in that loss. Gradual leaks and seepage are generally not covered, and neither is mold resulting from them. Mold from flooding is not covered because flooding itself is not. The department also notes that most policies do not include mold cleanup and testing after a damaged item is removed, and that coverage for mold cleanup arising from a covered loss can sometimes be added. Read the policy for those specifics rather than assuming either direction.</p>
<h2>When prevention becomes remediation</h2>
<p>At some point drying stops being the answer because growth has already established. Visible growth across a wall, a strong persistent odor, or material that has been wet for weeks is a remediation question, and in Texas that work sits under a licensing framework once the affected area passes a defined size. The honest boundary is worth stating up front on any job: a fast, documented dry-out is prevention, and a job that has been sitting since a leak two months ago is not going to be solved by putting fans in the room. Mixing those two up wastes a homeowner's money.</p>
<h2>How the visit runs</h2>
<p>Stop the source, extract, and remove wet unsalvageable porous material under a documented scope with photographs and measurements. Clean the surfaces that remain, then set containment and dehumidification, holding the working space inside the target humidity band. Meter framing, plates, subfloor, and cavities daily, and log ambient conditions alongside them. Equipment stays until materials reach target against unaffected reference readings. The file should end with a dated set of final readings, which is the only meaningful evidence that a preventive dry-out actually prevented anything.</p>

## svc_roof_leak_water_damage_lede

Roof leaks are rarely found where they show. Water enters at a flashing or a fastener, runs down the deck or a truss, and drops into insulation feet away, so the stain on the ceiling is a report of where gravity gave up rather than where the roof failed.

## svc_roof_leak_water_damage_body

<h2>Rain intensity is the local test</h2>
<p>The Lower Colorado River Authority describes the Hill Country reach of the Colorado River as Flash Flood Alley and attributes the risk partly to unusually high rainfall rates over steep terrain and shallow soil. Those same rates hit roofs. A roof assembly that handles a slow soaking rain can be overwhelmed when water backs up at a valley, a wall intersection, or a clogged gutter and rises above the flashing lap. That is why leaks appear during one storm and not the next, and why chasing a leak by looking straight up at the stain wastes an afternoon. Intensity, wind direction, and where water pooled matter more than total rainfall.</p>
<h2>Winter events put a different load on a roof</h2>
<p>Central Texas roofs also take ice. The National Weather Service office serving Austin recorded a quarter to half an inch of freezing rain across the metro on February 11, 2021, then snow totals in and around the city on February 15 that included 6.6 inches five miles north-northeast of Austin and 7.5 inches at Lakeway. In the January 30 through February 2, 2023 storm, the same office logged reports of half an inch to three quarters of an inch of ice from the Hill Country east into the Austin metro, with more than a quarter inch widespread along the Interstate 35 corridor. Ice loads gutters, blocks drainage paths at eaves, and then melts into the assembly rather than off it, which is how a January storm becomes a February ceiling stain.</p>
<h2>Finding the entry point before the drying starts</h2>
<p>The wet path is mapped from the attic side wherever there is access. Insulation is lifted, the deck underside and truss faces are inspected for staining and tracking, and readings are taken across the top plate and down the wall cavity if water reached it. From below, a moisture meter and thermal scan mark the wet boundary in the ceiling assembly and separate a single point of entry from a broad saturation. This matters for the repair as much as the drying, because a roofer sent to the stain and not to the entry point does the wrong repair and both parties learn about it during the next storm.</p>
<h2>What wet insulation and drywall do</h2>
<p>Blown insulation that has taken water compacts, loses much of its thermal value, and holds moisture against framing and the back of the ceiling board. Drying it in place is slow and usually pointless, so it comes out over the wet footprint. Gypsum ceiling board that is sagging, has lost its bond at the fasteners, or has visible seam movement is structurally compromised and gets removed, not dried. Board that is wet but flat and firm can often be dried from the attic side with air moving above it. Truss and deck framing gets metered and dried; that wood is what determines when the ceiling can be closed back up.</p>
<h2>Standing water in a ceiling is a safety problem</h2>
<p>A ceiling holding water is heavy and it fails downward without a warning sound. It should not be probed from below by whoever is standing on the ladder, and the room under it should be cleared of people first, furniture second. Water traveling along a top plate reaches recessed lights, ceiling fan boxes, and junction boxes, so power to that circuit gets shut off before anyone opens the assembly. A drain hole in the right place, made from a controlled position with the circuit dead, is a legitimate move. Poking upward at a bulge is not.</p>
<h2>The claim record on a wind or storm event</h2>
<p>Storm losses turn on cause and timing, so the file gets built while the evidence is intact. Photographs from the attic showing the entry point and the tracking, dated moisture readings at the ceiling and wall, and the roofing invoice describing what was repaired all belong in the same folder. Where a storm damaged the roof and the interior at once, the interior mitigation record and the roof repair record are read together by whoever reviews the loss. Keep the weather date; a documented ice or hail date in the file removes an argument about whether the leak was long-running wear.</p>
<h2>How the visit runs</h2>
<p>Confirm the leak has been stopped or temporarily covered, since drying under an active leak is money spent twice. Extract standing water, remove saturated insulation and any unsalvageable ceiling board under a documented scope, and record square footage. Air is introduced above and below the assembly, dehumidification is sized to the wet surface area, and framing readings are logged on each return until they hit target. Ceiling texture and paint are the last step, held until the wood behind them is dry, because a finished ceiling closed over wet framing is the most common reason this job gets done twice.</p>

## svc_slab_leak_water_damage_lede

A slab leak does not announce itself with a puddle. A pressurized line under concrete pushes water sideways through the fill and up at the perimeter or through a seam, so the first symptoms are a warm spot, a water bill that moved, and flooring that lifted for no visible reason.

## svc_slab_leak_water_damage_body

<h2>What is actually leaking under there</h2>
<p>Post-tension and conventional slabs both carry pressurized supply lines and gravity drain lines beneath or within them. A supply leak runs continuously at line pressure and produces the classic signs: a section of floor that feels warm on a hot-water line, the sound of water with every fixture closed, and a rising bill. A drain leak only leaks when something upstream is used, so it hides longer and shows up as a persistent damp area near a bathroom or kitchen wall. Distinguishing the two decides the repair method, because a supply line can often be rerouted overhead while a drain line generally has to be reached.</p>
<h2>Hard water and the age of the pipe</h2>
<p>Austin Water publishes system hardness averaging 93 parts per million as calcium carbonate, or 5.4 grains per gallon, with values reported as high as 126 ppm. A USGS guide places that upper figure in the hard band and identifies calcium and magnesium as the source of deposits inside piping. Mineral scale narrows the bore, changes flow velocity, and concentrates wear at bends and joints, which is one contributing factor in pinhole failures on older under-slab copper. Age, soil chemistry, and installation quality all play a part, so this is a reason to inspect rather than a diagnosis on its own. It does mean that one under-slab pinhole on a house of a certain vintage is a reason to look at the rest of the run.</p>
<h2>Meter data now dates the leak</h2>
<p>Austin Water's meter replacement program set out to install 250,000 new meters over four years beginning in 2021, and on those accounts consumption is collected and transmitted daily, with readings reaching the customer portal within about 24 hours. Customers can also set their own high-use and leak notifications. For a slab leak that is a real diagnostic tool, because a continuous overnight draw with the house asleep is the signature of a supply leak under concrete. The utility's own no-cost check still works too: read the meter, use no water for a few hours, and read it again.</p>
<h2>Locating without opening the whole floor</h2>
<p>The point of a proper locate is to break the smallest possible piece of concrete. Pressure isolation tells you whether the leak is on the hot side, the cold side, or a drain. Acoustic listening, tracer gas, and thermal imaging narrow the position, and readings across the floor establish the wet footprint that drying will have to address. Opening the slab at a guess costs more than the locate did, and it puts a hole in the floor that still has to be closed whether the leak was there or not. On a post-tension slab the tendon layout has to be respected, which is a reason the coring decision belongs to people who confirmed it rather than assumed it.</p>
<h2>The wet part is bigger than the hole</h2>
<p>Water in the fill under a slab wicks into the concrete, and concrete gives moisture up slowly. Flooring installed over it traps that moisture, which is why vinyl plank cups, laminate swells at the seams, and tile releases from the mortar bed after a leak that seemed small. Bottom plates, baseboard, and the lower few inches of drywall along the affected wall take water by wicking, not by splashing. The drying scope therefore covers the slab surface, the perimeter framing, and enough of the floor covering removed to let the concrete release moisture, all set by meter readings rather than by the size of the visible stain.</p>
<h2>The repair choices and what they cost you in time</h2>
<p>Spot repair breaks and patches the slab at the leak. Rerouting abandons the failed segment and runs new line through walls and ceiling, which avoids further concrete work but adds drywall repair. Repiping addresses a system with several failures rather than one. Each path changes the drying timeline, because concrete cannot begin releasing moisture until the water stops arriving. Ask for the plumber's locate documentation and the method chosen, and keep it with the mitigation file. On covered losses the carrier reads the cause, the repair, and the drying record as one story.</p>
<h2>How the visit runs</h2>
<p>Confirm the leak is isolated or repaired, then extract any standing water and lift flooring over the wet footprint under a documented scope. Meter the slab, the plates, and the drywall base, and record the affected square footage with photographs. Dehumidification is sized against the wet surface, and specialty drying is used where moisture is bound in concrete and hardwood rather than sitting on it. Readings are logged on each return visit until materials reach target, and the floor is not reinstalled on a slab that has not been verified dry, because a moisture-blocked flooring failure six weeks later is a second job nobody wants to pay for.</p>

## svc_storm_and_flash_flood_cleanup_lede

Storm water is outdoor water. It arrives carrying street runoff, yard chemicals, and whatever the creek picked up, so a flash flood cleanup starts as a contamination job and only becomes a drying job after the unsalvageable material is out of the building.

## svc_storm_and_flash_flood_cleanup_body

<h2>Why the water gets here so fast</h2>
<p>The Lower Colorado River Authority calls the Hill Country stretch of the Colorado River Flash Flood Alley and describes it as one of the greatest flash flood risks in the country, pointing to steep terrain, shallow soil, and unusually high rainfall rates as the combination behind it. The authority runs a monitoring network of 275 automated weather and river gauges feeding near-real-time readings, and notes that once water is released at Mansfield Dam it reaches Tom Miller Dam in roughly an hour and a half. Lake Travis sits full at 681 feet above mean sea level with another 33 feet of flood pool above that, holding 776,062 acre-feet. Those figures explain the local pattern: rain that fell somewhere you cannot see can put water in a building here on a short clock.</p>
<h2>The creeks have documented peaks</h2>
<p>Small urban creeks carry startling volumes during these events. The USGS gauge on Waller Creek at 23rd Street in Austin, with a peak record running from 1951 to 1980, logged its highest annual peak at 4,020 cubic feet per second and a gage height of 9.00 feet on October 11, 1973. That is a creek most people step over on a walk downtown. A building near a channel like that is not exposed to a slow rise; it is exposed to a short, violent stage change followed by a drop, which is why the interior evidence of how high water got is often gone within hours and has to be recorded immediately.</p>
<h2>Storm losses are not always creek losses</h2>
<p>Austin Watershed Protection reports more than 1,100 miles of smaller ditches and drainage pipes carrying stormwater before it ever reaches a creek, and says storm drain capacity may be undersized in neighborhoods built before drainage regulations took effect in the early 1980s and in areas annexed later. That is the mechanism behind a large share of local water intrusion: the street cannot take the volume, water ponds, and it enters at a door sill, a garage slab, or a window well. The distinction matters because the entry route, the water's contamination level, and the coverage question all differ from creek overbank flooding, and the file needs to say which one happened.</p>
<h2>Central Texas floods have a body count, and it shapes the safety rules</h2>
<p>The National Weather Service's Texas flood record includes the October 17 and 18, 1998 event, when 31 people died, damages ran to roughly 750 million dollars, and more than 30 inches of rain fell in 36 hours south of San Marcos, with many of the deaths occurring at low water crossings. Its account of the 2002 South Central Texas floods lists 12 fatalities and around 48,000 homes damaged. Those are not distant statistics for anyone doing cleanup work here. Nobody enters a building with moving water around it, nobody drives a flooded crossing to get to a job, and nobody works in standing water on an energized circuit.</p>
<h2>What comes out of the building</h2>
<p>Porous material that took outdoor floodwater is removed rather than dried. That includes carpet and pad, wet fiberglass insulation, particleboard and paper-faced material, and drywall to a line set by readings above the visible water mark. Contents get sorted into salvageable, cleanable, and discarded, with the discarded items photographed and listed before they leave. Silt and organic debris are cleaned off hard surfaces before any air moves, because drying a contaminated surface just distributes what is on it. Only after removal and cleaning does the drying equipment go in.</p>
<h2>Documentation while the water line is still visible</h2>
<p>The single most useful record on a storm loss is the height of the water, photographed against a fixed reference like a door frame, and the date and time it was taken. Add photographs of the entry point, the exterior grade and drainage condition, dated moisture readings, and an itemized list of removed material with square footage. Flood water arriving from outside the building is treated differently by policies than a pipe failure inside it, and the difference is decided on the facts recorded that day. Keep receipts for anything bought during the emergency, including pumps and fuel.</p>
<h2>How the visit runs</h2>
<p>Confirm the building is safe to enter and that power to affected areas is off, then pump and extract standing water. Remove unsalvageable porous material and debris under a written scope with photographs and measurements. Clean hard surfaces, then set containment and dehumidification sized to the wet area, with air introduced into wall cavities rather than aimed at painted surfaces. Readings are logged on each return visit until materials reach target. Because these events hit many addresses at once, equipment goes out on committed written scopes rather than on verbal promises, and the sequence is published so a homeowner knows what happens on day two and day five.</p>

## svc_structural_drying_and_dehumidification_lede

Structural drying is the part of the job that gets measured. Extraction removes the water you can see; drying removes the water inside framing, subfloor, plaster, and concrete, and it is finished when readings say so rather than when the room feels comfortable.

## svc_structural_drying_and_dehumidification_body

<h2>Evaporation, capture, and airflow have to balance</h2>
<p>Drying a building is three things happening at once. Air moving across a wet surface pulls moisture into the air, a dehumidifier removes that moisture from the air, and the drier air returns to the surface to take more. Break any leg and the system stalls. Fans with no dehumidification make a humid room that redeposits moisture on cool surfaces, including surfaces that were never wet. Dehumidification with no airflow dries the air near the machine and nothing else. Sizing is calculated against the wet surface area and the class of materials involved, not against the size of the room or the number of units on the truck.</p>
<h2>Bound water is a separate problem</h2>
<p>Water sitting on top of a dense material and water inside it behave differently. Concrete, hardwood, plaster, masonry, stone, and closed wall cavities hold moisture that evaporates slowly, and the industry treats deeply bound water as its own drying class with its own timeline. That is where specialty equipment applies: injection drying into wall cavities, floor drying mats on wood, and desiccant dehumidification where a refrigerant unit cannot pull the air low enough to keep moving water out of dense material. Adding more air movers to a bound-water problem raises the noise and the power bill without shortening the job.</p>
<h2>Tighter houses dry differently</h2>
<p>Austin adopted the 2024 International Energy Conservation Code alongside the 2024 building, residential, plumbing and mechanical codes, all effective July 10, 2025, under City Code Chapter 25-12. Newer and retrofitted buildings here are consequently tighter and better insulated than the stock built decades ago. A tight envelope is an advantage during drying because the equipment controls the air rather than fighting outdoor humidity, but it also means moisture that gets into an assembly has fewer ways out on its own. Older leaky construction sheds some moisture passively and gains humid outside air at the same time. Both need containment; they need different sizing.</p>
<h2>Containment is a cost control</h2>
<p>Sealing off the wet rooms with poly and taped edges shrinks the volume of air the equipment has to condition, which shortens the job and lowers the power draw. It also keeps humid, dusty drying air out of the parts of the house still in use, and where the water was contaminated it keeps the work area under control. On occupied homes containment is often what makes staying in the house realistic. It is not a line item added for appearance; a drying chamber half the size dries in materially less time with the same equipment.</p>
<h2>The daily record is the deliverable</h2>
<p>A defensible drying file has a moisture map from day one, daily readings at the same marked locations, temperature and relative humidity logged inside the chamber and outside it, and an equipment list with placement. That record does two jobs. It tells the technician whether the plan is working while there is still time to change it, and it tells the carrier why the equipment stayed as long as it did. Readings that only exist at the start and the end of a job are not a record, they are two data points and an assertion.</p>
<h2>Freeze weeks stress the equipment supply</h2>
<p>The Weather Service office serving Austin recorded pockets of one to two inches of combined sleet and freezing rain in parts of the metro on January 24 and 25, 2026, and NOAA's climate center reports that central Texas, Austin included, saw six to nine consecutive days of freezing temperatures during the February 2021 event, the longest such run in the state's record. Those weeks produce hundreds of burst-pipe losses within a couple of days. Dehumidifiers and air movers become the constraint, and the honest answer is a scheduled sequence with a written scope rather than a promise that everything happens at once.</p>
<h2>What a completed dry looks like</h2>
<p>Materials reach a target moisture content set against unaffected reference areas of the same material in the same building, not against a generic number. Wall cavities are verified, not assumed, and framing behind removed drywall is metered before anything closes up. Relative humidity in the space is stable in a normal range without the equipment running. At that point equipment comes out, a final reading set goes in the file, and the rebuild can start. Closing a wall over framing that never hit target is the mistake that turns a drying invoice into a mold remediation invoice.</p>

## svc_toilet_overflow_cleanup_lede

Two overflows can look the same on a bathroom floor and be completely different jobs. Water that came over the rim from the tank is a drying problem. Water that came up from the drain is a contamination problem, and it gets handled with containment from the first minute.

## svc_toilet_overflow_cleanup_body

<h2>Where the water came from decides everything</h2>
<p>A running fill valve with a stuck flapper sends tank water over the rim. A clog sends bowl water and whatever was in it onto the floor, and a main line backup sends water from beyond the fixture. The industry consensus standard sorts water by contamination rather than by how much of it there is, and that sorting drives whether porous material can be dried in place or has to come out. Getting it wrong in the first hour is expensive both ways: containment skipped on a contaminated event spreads the problem through the house, and a clean tank overflow treated as sewage means material was thrown away that meters said was salvageable.</p>
<h2>Volume math on a modern fixture</h2>
<p>The Environmental Protection Agency's WaterSense program notes that the federal standard caps toilets at 1.6 gallons per flush, WaterSense-labeled models use 1.28 gallons or less, and toilets predating the standard can use up to 6 gallons per flush. Those numbers matter twice. A single overflow event moves a few gallons, which sounds survivable, but a fixture that keeps refilling and spilling while nobody is home moves that volume repeatedly. Austin Water puts a leaking toilet at roughly 200 gallons a day on its own, and gives a test that takes no tools: add coloring to the tank, wait 30 minutes without flushing, and color appearing in the bowl means water is passing through.</p>
<h2>The path through a bathroom floor</h2>
<p>Bathroom floors are the worst place in a house to spill water. Tile over a mortar bed hides the wet substrate completely, and grout lines pass water while the surface reads dry. The toilet flange penetration gives water a direct route to the subfloor and, in a two-story house, to the ceiling below. Vanity toe kicks, the wall behind the tank, and the bottom plate at the tub wall all take water and hold it. On a slab, water tracks under the tile toward the doorway and into the hall floor assembly, which is why the readings that matter are often taken in the next room.</p>
<h2>Cleaning, then drying, in that order</h2>
<p>On a contaminated event the sequence is not negotiable. Bulk water and solids come out first, hard surfaces are cleaned before they are dried, and porous material that absorbed the water is removed rather than sanitized in place. Sanitizing a wet carpet pad does not make it a clean material again. Only after the removal and cleaning does drying equipment go in, because running air movers over contaminated surfaces spreads what should have been contained. Containment, negative air where warranted, and clean routes for hauling material out are part of the scope, not add-ons.</p>
<h2>The permit line on the fixture itself</h2>
<p>Austin's list of work exempt from permits covers clearing stoppages, and it explicitly includes removing and reinstalling a water closet as part of that work, plus repairing leaks in pipes, valves, or fixtures where nothing is replaced or rearranged. So pulling the toilet to clear a blockage and resetting it on a new wax ring sits inside the exempt category. Once the drain, waste, or vent piping under it turns out to be defective and needs replacing with new material, the city's exemption stops applying and a permit with an inspection is required. Ask which side of that line the repair fell on and keep the paperwork with the drying file.</p>
<h2>What gets replaced after a contaminated overflow</h2>
<p>Carpet and pad in the affected area come out. Wet drywall along the bottom edge is cut back to where readings say the material is dry. A particleboard vanity base that took contaminated water does not return to service. Solid wood trim usually cleans up and dries. Subfloor is metered from above and, where accessible, from below, and the flange condition gets examined while the fixture is off, since a rocking toilet on a corroded flange is the reason many of these events repeat. Set expectations on the tile: it often survives, and the mortar bed under it is what takes the drying time.</p>
<h2>How the visit runs</h2>
<p>Stop the source at the fixture supply stop, then establish whether the water was clean or contaminated and set containment accordingly. Extract, remove unsalvageable porous material under a written and photographed scope, and clean hard surfaces before any air movement starts. Meter the subfloor, wall bases, adjacent rooms, and the ceiling below, then place dehumidification sized to the wet area. Log readings on return visits until materials hit target. Keep the plumbing invoice for the blockage or fixture repair, because a claim file that documents a sudden stoppage reads very differently from one that documents a fixture that had been running for weeks.</p>

## svc_water_damage_insurance_claims_lede

A water claim is won or lost in the first day of documentation. The mitigation work can be textbook and still produce a partial payment if nobody recorded the cause, the timing, and the extent while the evidence was still in the building.

## svc_water_damage_insurance_claims_body

<h2>The coverage line most policies draw</h2>
<p>The Texas Department of Insurance describes the usual division plainly. Most home and renters policies cover sudden and accidental water damage, with a burst pipe, an overflowing toilet, and a broken washing machine hose as typical examples. Gradual leaks and seepage are generally excluded, as is mold that results from them. Mold that develops on an item damaged in a covered loss is usually covered, but flood-related mold is not, because flooding itself is not covered by a standard homeowners policy. The department also notes most policies leave out mold cleanup and testing once a damaged item has been removed, while coverage for mold cleanup from a covered loss can sometimes be added.</p>
<h2>Sudden versus gradual is a documentation contest</h2>
<p>Nothing about the water on the floor announces how long it ran. What establishes that is evidence: the failed component photographed before it is thrown out, the plumber's invoice describing what failed, dated moisture readings from the first visit, and utility data. On accounts with the newer meters here, daily consumption reaches the customer portal within roughly 24 hours, and a homeowner can also set high-use notifications, which can pin the start of a leak to a day. That kind of record is what turns a description into a demonstration, and it is the difference between a covered sudden failure and a denied maintenance issue.</p>
<h2>Flood coverage is a separate policy with its own limits</h2>
<p>Water arriving from outside the building generally requires a flood policy. Under the National Flood Insurance Program, maximum coverage for a single-family home is 250,000 dollars on the building and 100,000 dollars on contents, and renters can insure contents up to 100,000 dollars with no building coverage. Non-residential buildings can carry 500,000 dollars on the building and 500,000 dollars on contents. Property owners who need more than those caps have to look at supplemental private coverage. Knowing the ceiling matters before the loss, because after a creek event the limit is the limit.</p>
<h2>What belongs in the file</h2>
<p>Photographs of standing water against a fixed reference such as a door frame, with date and time. The failed component, in place if possible. Room-by-room images before removal. A moisture map and dated readings at marked locations, repeated on every visit. An itemized list of removed material with measurements and square footage. Every invoice: the plumber, the roofer, the mitigation, the equipment, and any emergency purchases. Contents inventory with model numbers where they matter. That set answers the questions an adjuster asks in one pass instead of three.</p>
<h2>Mitigation is usually a duty, not a favor</h2>
<p>Standard policies obligate the insured to protect the property from further damage after a loss. That cuts both ways. It means reasonable emergency work does not have to wait for an adjuster to walk the house, and it means a homeowner who leaves water sitting for a week can see part of the resulting damage attributed to that delay. Keep receipts for anything spent protecting the property. Document a temporary repair, like a tarp or a shutoff, as well as the permanent one, and note the dates on both.</p>
<h2>Local help that is not insurance</h2>
<p>A water loss often exposes plumbing that needed work anyway, and a repair bill can be the obstacle. Austin Water runs a Plumbing Repair Program with grants of up to 15,000 dollars for qualifying customers, and the utility's leak guidance also gives homeowners no-cost diagnostics: reading the meter with all water off, and dye-testing a toilet tank and checking the bowl after 30 minutes without flushing. The utility puts a leaking toilet at roughly 200 gallons a day. Those checks are also useful evidence when a claim depends on whether a fixture had been leaking for weeks.</p>
<h2>How the documentation gets built on site</h2>
<p>The first visit produces photographs, a moisture map, and a written scope of what will be removed and why, before removal starts. Readings are logged at the same marked spots on each return, with ambient temperature and humidity alongside. Removed material is measured and listed. Equipment on site is documented with placement and run time. At completion, a final reading set against unaffected reference areas closes the file. Presented in that order, the record explains itself, and a claim that explains itself is the kind that gets paid without a second inspection.</p>

## svc_water_heater_leak_cleanup_lede

A water heater rarely fails politely. Forty to eighty gallons leave the tank, then the supply keeps feeding it until someone closes a valve, and the water lands in a garage, a hall closet, or an attic platform directly above finished ceilings.

## svc_water_heater_leak_cleanup_body

<h2>Two failures that look identical on the floor</h2>
<p>A leaking fitting, a weeping temperature and pressure relief valve, or a failed drain valve puts water on the floor with an intact tank. A rusted-through tank does the same thing and cannot be repaired. Telling them apart matters immediately, because one is a shutoff-and-repair and the other is a replacement plus a cleanup on a tank that will keep emptying. The tell is where the water tracks from: a wet ring under the tank center with clean fittings above usually means the tank itself. Either way, closing the cold inlet valve stops the refill, and cutting power or gas to the unit stops it from firing on an empty or partly empty tank.</p>
<h2>Hard water is the local variable</h2>
<p>Austin Water publishes tap water hardness averaging 93 parts per million as calcium carbonate, which the utility converts to 5.4 grains per gallon, with reported values from 70 up to 126 ppm across the system. A USGS water-supply guide places 61 to 120 ppm in the moderately hard band and 121 to 180 in the hard band, and states plainly that calcium and magnesium compounds are largely responsible for deposits that form in hot-water heaters and pipes. That is the mechanism behind the sediment layer at the bottom of a tank, the crusted relief valve, and the stiff shutoff that will not close when it finally matters. It also means a heater failure here is usually the end of a long process, which is worth knowing before the replacement goes in.</p>
<h2>Where the water goes depends on the install</h2>
<p>Garage installs are the mildest case: water runs toward the door, wets the bottom of drywall and any stored cardboard, and stays on one level. An interior closet install is worse, because the pan and its drain line are often the only defense and both are commonly blocked or missing. Attic installs are the expensive version. Sixty gallons on an attic platform saturates insulation across joists, loads the ceiling below, and follows top plates into wall cavities, so the visible stain is a fraction of the wet area. Any attic or interior heater deserves a look at the pan, the drain line's discharge point, and whether that line was ever connected.</p>
<h2>Meter and bill evidence for a slow prior leak</h2>
<p>Many tank failures follow weeks of a slow seep that nobody logged. Austin Water's leak guidance gives a homeowner a check that costs nothing: read the meter, use no water for a few hours, then read it again, since movement with everything closed points to a leak on the property. The utility's dispatch line runs around the clock at 512-972-1000 for suspected utility-side problems, while non-emergency reports through 3-1-1 are investigated within one to three business days. On accounts with the newer meters, daily usage data reaches the customer portal within 24 hours, which can date the start of a leak more precisely than memory.</p>
<h2>The permit question on a replacement</h2>
<p>Swapping a heater is not finish work. Austin's exemption list covers repairing a leak in a valve or fixture where no piping is replaced or rearranged, and it covers cosmetic work like paint, tile, and cabinets, but it does not exempt water heaters. Where connections, venting, or piping are replaced, the plumbing permit and inspection path applies, measured against the 2024 Uniform Plumbing Code adopted here effective July 10, 2025. Keep the permit number and the invoice with the drying documentation; a carrier reading the file later wants the repair and the mitigation to line up in time.</p>
<h2>What gets dried and what gets thrown out</h2>
<p>Wet fiberglass insulation comes out. Cardboard, particleboard shelving, and anything with a paper face that took water and sat is not a drying candidate. Drywall wet along the bottom edge is cut where readings say it is wet, not at a convenient line, and the cavity is dried with air moving through it rather than across the paint. Framing, plates, and subfloor get metered, and hardwood or engineered flooring adjacent to a garage or closet install is checked from below wherever there is access. Scorch or rust on the burner assembly of a gas unit that sat in water is a replacement question for the plumber, not a cleaning question.</p>
<h2>How the visit runs</h2>
<p>Confirm the inlet valve is closed and power or gas is off, then extract standing water and remove saturated insulation and unsalvageable porous material under a written scope. Map the wet edge with meters, including up wall cavities on an attic install and along the ceiling below. Place dehumidification and air movement matched to the wet surface, log readings each visit, and hold equipment until materials reach target rather than until the floor feels dry. Photograph the failed tank, the pan, and the drain line before the plumber hauls the unit off, because the cause of loss is easiest to prove while the evidence is still in the room.</p>

Water damage is priced in two halves that get confused constantly: mitigation, which is extraction, containment and drying, and reconstruction, which is putting the house back. The ranges below are built from prices Austin area restoration firms post publicly, converted where they are quoted per square foot or per equipment day, and set against the city and utility figures that surround a loss. ## pricing_body

### Contamination sets the price before square footage does

ANSI/IICRC S500 sorts water by contamination rather than volume, and that sorting moves the number first. Clean supply-line water, gray water from an appliance overflow, and grossly contaminated water are three different jobs on one floor plan. Austin firms publish the gap plainly. Clean water runs roughly three to four dollars per square foot. Gray water runs four to six and a half plus a stated contamination markup. The contaminated category starts at seven, with a larger markup and material replacement. Two nuances get lost online. Category can shift with time and temperature, so response speed is a cost factor. And storm water is assessed rather than assumed into the worst category.

### Square feet times a rate, plus equipment days

Most posted pricing here is a rate, not a total, so both arithmetic steps behind the table are worth showing. A 500 square foot affected area at three to eight dollars per square foot is $1,500 to $4,000 of mitigation. Drying equipment bills by the day on top: three air movers and one dehumidifier for four days runs about $448 at posted low daily rates with a $65 setup charge, or about $1,031 at high rates with a $175 setup charge. That is why an invoice looks nothing like the rate someone read online. Published drying times run one to two days for tile, three to five for drywall, and five to seven for hardwood.

### The Austin specifics that change a loss

A slab-on-grade home with no basement lets water spread sideways across a wide floor area instead of collecting in one low room. Affected square footage climbs fast on a small leak, so wall cavities and cabinet toe kicks get checked before anyone signs off. Source matters as much, so a scope establishes where water came from before deciding how clean it was.

### Permits, and the utility bill nobody expects

Mitigation is not permitted work, but the repair behind the loss often is. A failed supply line, drain, water heater connection or sewer lateral is plumbing work, and the City of Austin homeowner permitting fees, fiscal year 2026 schedule lists a residential plumbing permit at $200.43, up from $184.70. Reconstruction touching structure, plumbing or electrical carries its own permit and review time. Then there is the water that ran. A leak unnoticed for a billing cycle shows up as a spike. The utility publishes an adjustment path under City Code section 15-9-141, requested within 90 days of the repair. It wants the paid invoice, account number, service address and the dates of the high bills.

### Two contracts, not one

Drying a house and rebuilding it are separate scopes, often separate contracts. Posted mitigation figures stop at extraction, containment, drying and monitoring, and exclude drywall, paint, flooring and trim. Reconstruction is priced after the space is dry.

### Reading a restoration estimate

A complete estimate states the water category and the reasoning, the measured affected area, and the evaporation load. It lists equipment by type and count with a daily rate and expected days, not one drying charge. It says what is removed, whether flood cuts are planned and to what height, and who signs off that the structure is dry, since that signature starts a rebuild. National averages describe a modest clean-water loss in a house with a basement in a mild climate, and they omit the plumbing permit, the floor area a slab gives water to travel, and the rebuild that follows a serious loss.

## svc_water_extraction_lede

Extraction is the hour-by-hour part of a water loss. Pull the standing water, find how far it traveled behind the baseboards, and start the record an adjuster will read weeks later. On a slab house, water spreads sideways and quietly.

## svc_water_extraction_body

### The first read on a wet floor

Standing water is the visible part of the problem. What decides the next two weeks is where it went: under a cabinet toe kick, beneath a floating floor, into the bottom plate behind the baseboard, or down through the ceiling of the room below. Extraction begins with a moisture survey that marks the wet edge past the point you can see. That boundary is what both the drying plan and the claim file are built on. Drawing it after the wet material has already gone to the curb is guesswork, and it is the single most common reason a file falls apart later.

### Contamination is sorted before equipment is chosen

The consensus document for this work is ANSI/IICRC S500, fifth edition, issued in 2021. It sorts water by contamination rather than by volume. The cleanest tier is described as water from a source that does not pose substantial harm to people, which covers a failed supply line, a tub overflow with nothing in it, or a toilet tank. That status is temporary, not permanent. The standard notes that clean water deteriorates as it mixes with soils and as hours pass, and that time and temperature affect how quickly contaminants multiply. A Friday night leak found on Sunday is no longer the job it was on Friday.

### Class describes evaporation, and it sets the schedule

The same standard assigns a class based on how much wet surface area there is and how permeable the affected materials are when drying starts. The lowest class holds water on the surface of dense materials, with little or no wet carpet or cushion. The middle classes involve carpet, pad, and gypsum board across floors, walls, and ceilings, which describes most finished rooms here. The highest class is the one that stretches a timeline: deeply bound water in plaster, hardwood, concrete, masonry, stone, and closed structural cavities. That water evaporates slowly, and it needs specialty drying rather than more fans pointed at a wall.

### The deadlines the Texas Insurance Code sets for your insurer

Chapter 542 of the Insurance Code puts a schedule on the carrier, and a homeowner who knows it can hold the file to it. Within 15 days of receiving notice of the claim, the insurer must acknowledge it, begin investigating, and request the items, statements, and forms it reasonably believes it needs. It then has until the 15th business day after receiving those items to accept or reject the claim in writing. If it cannot decide in that window, it must give its reasons and then decide no later than the 45th day. Payment is due within five business days of the notice of acceptance. Miss the schedule and the statute adds interest on the amount of the claim at 18 percent a year, plus reasonable attorney's fees. Those deadlines stretch by 15 days when the commissioner has designated a weather-related catastrophe.

### Do not run this one yourself

Water sitting near outlets, a plugged-in appliance, or a submerged power strip is an electrical hazard first and a cleanup second. Leave it until power to that area is off. A sagging, water-loaded ceiling should not be poked from underneath, and the room below it should be emptied of people rather than furniture. Shop vacuums and box fans move a little water and a lot of humid air, which is how one wet room becomes three.

### The trap that produces a second visit

Carpet that feels dry on top, and a baseboard that looks fine, both hide the same failure. Pad and gypsum give water up slowly, and the readings taken along the wall base at the end of the job are what separate a finished dry from a rebuild done over moisture. The Environmental Protection Agency puts the target plainly: dry water-damaged areas and items within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold growth, and control moisture, because there is no practical way to eliminate spores indoors. That window is a reason to move, not a reason to panic on hour 49.

### How the visit runs

A technician confirms the source and whether it has actually stopped, then extracts standing water from hard surfaces and carpet. Wet material gets mapped with meters, photographed, and measured, and the affected square footage is written down. Unsalvageable porous material comes out under a documented scope. Air movers and dehumidifiers are then placed to match the wet surface area, and readings are logged on return visits until the materials hit the dry target. Keep every invoice, including the plumbing repair, because that paperwork is what turns an event into a paid claim. If the leak ran long enough to spike the water bill, Austin Water publishes an adjustment path under City Code section 15-9-141 that has to be requested within 90 days of the repair.

## svc_basement_water_damage_restoration_body

### What a basement means on an Austin lot

Below-grade space in this market usually is not a full basement. It is a lower level on a lot that falls away toward a creek, a partially buried garage on a sloped drive, a crawlspace with a dirt floor, or a below-grade utility room holding the water heater and the electrical panel. Zilker and Allandale sit on very different ground from Onion Creek or East Cesar Chavez, and the drainage each lot gets is a matter of where the water goes after it leaves the roof. What all of these spaces share is a low point that water finds and cannot leave without help.

### Seepage is a coverage problem before it is a drying problem

The restoration standard lists seepage driven by hydrostatic pressure, along with sump pump failures and condensate, in the middle contamination tier rather than the clean one. Gray water carries microorganisms and the nutrients they feed on. Left in place, that water can be reclassified into the grossly contaminated tier, and the standard notes 48 hours as the point where that reclassification becomes a live question. The insurance picture is just as unfriendly. The Texas Department of Insurance states that most home policies do not cover water damage from gradual leaks or seepage, and that the exclusion carries through to mold arising from it. Sudden and accidental damage, such as a burst pipe or a broken washer hose, is the version most policies do cover.

### Concrete holds water long after the floor looks dry

A below-grade room is mostly dense material: slab, block, poured wall, stone, sometimes plaster. Those assemblies fall into the class of loss defined by deeply bound water and a low evaporation rate once the bulk water is gone. The practical effect is that the floor can read dry at the surface while the wall cavity behind furring strips is still wet, and a dehumidifier alone will not finish the job. Flooring installed over a slab that was never verified dry is how a homeowner pays for the same floor twice.

### The appraisal clause when the only fight is the number

Sometimes coverage is agreed and the amount is not. Texas addressed that directly in 2025. Senate Bill 458 added Chapter 1813 to the Insurance Code, effective September 1, 2025, and it requires residential property and personal auto policies issued or renewed in this state to contain an appraisal provision. The chapter describes appraisal as a dispute resolution process used solely to determine the amount of loss when that amount is in dispute. The determination binds both sides, except in cases of fraud, accident, or material mistake. It is not a path for arguing whether the seepage was covered in the first place.

### Texas licenses mold work, and 25 square feet is the hinge

The Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation licenses mold work in two separate lanes: assessment, meaning the people who inspect and test, and remediation, meaning the people who clean and remove. A single company may hold both credentials, but it is not allowed to perform assessment and remediation on the same project. The agency states that in most instances visible growth under 25 contiguous square feet can be cleaned up by people who are not licensed. At or above that area, licensed work and a filed notification come into play, and the notification carries a $25 fee. Water damage gets an explicit carve-out: when delay would increase contamination, the licensee may treat it as an emergency and file the notification by the following working day.

### The certificate that protects the next transaction

When licensed remediation is performed, the contractor must issue a Certificate of Mold Damage Remediation no later than the tenth day after the project stop date. The licensed assessment consultant who performed the post-remediation evaluation co-signs it. The certificate states that the contamination was remediated and whether the underlying cause was corrected. A seller has to hand buyers copies of every certificate from the preceding five years. There is a reason to want one in the drawer: under the state's rules, a certificate showing the underlying cause was corrected prevents an insurer from making an underwriting decision on the home based on that previous mold damage or claim.

### What not to do down there

Do not enter standing water in a room that houses the panel, a sump, or a gas appliance until power is off and the space is confirmed safe. Do not seal a damp below-grade wall with paint or a coating and call it solved. Do not throw out the failed sump pump or the split hose before it is photographed, because that part is the evidence for a sudden-failure argument.

### How the visit runs

The visit starts outside, looking for where water enters, then moves to extraction, then to a meter survey of slab, walls, and any cavity behind them. Contents come off the floor. Porous material that will not recover is removed under a written scope, and the remaining assemblies are dried with equipment sized to a low evaporation rate, verified by readings rather than by feel.

## svc_sewage_cleanup_lede

A drain backup is a contamination job that happens to involve water. The goal on day one is to isolate the area, stop anything else from draining into it, and keep the cleanup from spreading through the rest of the house on people's shoes.

## svc_sewage_cleanup_body

### What the standard actually calls this water

ANSI/IICRC S500 puts sewage in its most severe tier, describing water that is grossly contaminated and can contain pathogenic, toxigenic, or other harmful agents that can cause significant adverse reactions in people who contact or consume it. Two details in that definition matter in a house. A waste line backflow that originates beyond any trap counts, regardless of what the water looks like or what color it is. So does rising water from a river or stream. Color and odor are not the test. Origin is the test, and a clear-looking backup from beyond a trap is handled with the same protective measures as an obvious one.

### Stop adding water to the problem

The most useful thing a homeowner does in the first ten minutes is turn things off. Every fixture that drains feeds the same line, so a washing machine cycle, a dishwasher, a shower, or a second toilet can push the backup farther into the structure while you are looking at the first mess. Keep people and pets out of the affected rooms. Resist the urge to aim a household fan at the floor, because that moves contaminated droplets and aerosols into rooms that were clean when the day started.

### What leaves the building, and why

Porous material that absorbed contaminated water is generally removed rather than treated. That list usually includes carpet and cushion, pad-backed matting, upholstered pieces, paper goods, wet insulation, and gypsum board along the bottom of the wall. Cabinet boxes are judged from the toe kick and the back panel, not the door faces. Hard, non-porous surfaces can often be cleaned, disinfected, and dried in place. The order of operations is what a careless crew gets backwards: containment and removal come first, and structural drying comes after the contamination is gone, not at the same time.

### Where these backups come from in this city

Some backups are entirely private, in the lateral between the house and the main, and some are not. The City Auditor's review of Austin Water reported roughly 4,000 miles of water mains and a main failure rate of 9.2 breaks per 100 miles, against an industry average of 25 and an optimized benchmark of 15. That is drinking water rather than wastewater, but it is a useful reminder that buried pipe fails on its own schedule. The utility's own history shows how fast a system event reaches a kitchen. A citywide precautionary boil water notice was issued on the evening of February 5, 2022 and rescinded late on February 8. A treatment error at the Ullrich plant had sent high-turbidity water to the filters. An earlier notice ran from October 22 to October 28, 2018. Flooding on the Lower Colorado had driven raw water turbidity from a typical 5 or 6 units to a peak of 387.

### Coverage here is written narrowly

The Department of Insurance is blunt about backups: whether water or sewage entering through sewers or drains is covered depends on the policy, and some policies cover it while others require it to be added. Document the origin precisely and early. Photograph the fixture, the water line on the wall, and the affected rooms before anything is bagged, and keep the plumber's invoice describing what was found in the line. If porous contents are hauled away for health reasons, photograph and list them first.

### The notice Texas requires before you sue

For a first-party residential claim, Chapter 542A adds a step most homeowners have never heard of. Written notice must go to the insurer no later than the 61st day before a suit is filed, and it is a prerequisite to filing. The notice has to state the acts or omissions complained of, the specific amount alleged to be owed, and any attorney's fees already incurred. Within 30 days of receiving it, the insurer may ask to inspect, photograph, and evaluate the property, and that inspection is to be completed by the 60th day where reasonably possible. A suit filed early can be dismissed without prejudice, and an insurer that never got a complying notice can seek abatement.

### Testing is not the fix

If growth follows a backup, the federal guidance points at the water rather than the laboratory. The Environmental Protection Agency states that no federal limits have been set for mold or mold spores, and that airborne threshold values for mold do not exist, so a sample cannot show a building complies with a standard. The agency's advice is to correct the moisture source and clean up what grew. On health, stay with what the agency says: molds produce allergens and irritants, and contact or inhalation may cause allergic responses in sensitive people, including hay fever type symptoms and asthma attacks.

## svc_flood_damage_restoration_body

### Flood is a separate word to an insurer

The Federal Emergency Management Agency states plainly that most homeowners insurance does not cover flood damage, and that flood coverage is bought as its own policy for the building, the contents, or both. There is usually a 30-day waiting period before a National Flood Insurance Program policy takes effect, with narrow exceptions tied to a lender requirement or a map change. The state consumer guidance closes the loop on the follow-on damage: mold from a flood is not covered, because the home policy does not cover the flood that caused it. None of that changes the physical work, and none of it is a reason to describe outside water as a leak.

### The rainfall numbers under this city were revised upward

Design storms here are not what they were. Austin's watershed department explains that the local 100-year storm has been 10.2 inches of rain in 24 hours, while the updated federal rainfall study shows a 100-year storm exceeding 13 inches in parts of the city, an amount that resembles the old 500-year figure. The revision draws on 57 years of rainfall records collected since the previous study in 1961. The practical translation for a property owner is that a house can be outside a mapped boundary and still sit in the path of the storm that boundary was drawn for.

### Onion Creek and Shoal Creek, in numbers

City records give the local history its scale. On Halloween in 2013, Onion Creek at US 183 crested at a record 41 feet after rising 11 feet between 6:15 and 6:30 in the morning, and 659 homes were damaged, 259 of them severely, with 15 destroyed. Closer to downtown, Shoal Creek carried a peak discharge of 16,000 cubic feet per second on May 24, 1981, after about 6 inches of rain, in a flood that killed 13 people and caused $35.5 million in damage. Later Shoal Creek peaks are smaller and still serious: 7,990 cubic feet per second in December 1991, 8,700 in November 2001, and 11,000 in May 2015. About 10 percent of land in the city lies in the floodplain, with more than 9,000 buildings in the 100-year floodplain and more than 300 bridges subject to flooding.

### The most dangerous mistake is made in a vehicle

Roughly 75 percent of flood-related deaths in Texas happen in vehicles, according to the city's flood safety guidance. Driving around a barricade is not a technicality either: it is a Class B misdemeanor carrying up to 180 days in jail, a fine up to $2,000, or both. Do not return to a property while water is still moving across the route, and check the city's flood closure map before you go. Once the water drops, the loss will still be there, and so will the evidence.

### Storm water is assessed, not assumed

Restorers do not automatically treat every drop of storm water as the worst category. The S500 consensus body issued a position statement making that explicit: top-down water ingress, wind-driven rain, and water from weather-related events are not automatically classified in the grossly contaminated tier. Rising water from rivers and streams is, and so is water that picks up contamination on its way inside. The determination looks at the originating source together with the quality of the water after it contacts the materials on site. That distinction decides what can be dried and what has to be cut out.

### Chapter 542A governs the forces-of-nature claim

A flood, rainstorm, hail, or wind claim on residential property falls under Chapter 542A of the Insurance Code, which sets out its own pre-suit procedure. A policyholder must serve written notice at least 61 days before filing suit, and that notice is a prerequisite rather than a formality. It must state the acts or omissions at issue, the specific amount claimed as owed, and attorney's fees incurred, calculated from contemporaneous time records at a customary rate. The insurer may request an inspection within 30 days of the notice, to be completed by the 60th day where that is reasonably possible. Filing before the 61st day exposes the case to dismissal without prejudice.

### How the visit runs

Access and safety are confirmed first, including whether the structure was inundated and whether power and gas were compromised. The water line is photographed at every wall before removal begins. Standing water is pumped and extracted, then porous material that absorbed outside water is cut out to a documented height, bagged, and logged. Hard surfaces are cleaned before drying starts. Dehumidification and airflow run against measured readings, and the file grows as it goes: origin, category reasoning, affected square footage, materials removed, equipment days, and the readings that closed it. That record is what the deadlines in the Insurance Code operate on.

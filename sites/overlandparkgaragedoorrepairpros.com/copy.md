### Why every row has two ends

The failed component sets the floor and the collateral damage sets the ceiling. A spring is the clearest example. On its own it is a known part and a practiced procedure. ### The sound, and what to look at from ten feet back

A parting torsion spring is loud enough to wake the whole house, and the door that follows will lift a few inches and quit. Stand well back from the opening and look at the shaft above the header. A failed wound spring shows a clean gap between coils, often an inch or two wide, and the broken end sits cocked on the shaft. On an older single door you may instead see long coils running beside the horizontal tracks; there, one spring hangs slack while its partner stays stretched. Either way the door now hangs on lift cables that were never meant to carry it by themselves.

### Torsion or extension depends on when your street went in

### Cycle life is the clock that actually runs out

ANSI/DASMA 102 sets the floor: a residential door system is designed to operate a minimum of 10,000 cycles when properly selected, installed and maintained. A household that raises the door four times a day spends about 1,460 cycles a year, so builder-grade hardware at that rating is used up in roughly seven years. Add a second driver, a trash cart trip and a dog walk and the clock speeds up. DASMA's guidance on cycle life points at two variables a homeowner controls, lubrication on the manufacturer's schedule and keeping the door closed in a climate of extreme heat or cold, and Kansas supplies both extremes.

### Why a January morning is when it lets go

Cold does not break sound steel. It removes the margin around wire that has already cracked. The 1991 to 2020 normals for the Kansas City station count 93.3 nights a year at or below 32 degrees, 26.4 of them in January alone, and Overland Park's own adopted code table works from a 6 degree winter design temperature. On January 24, 2026 the weather service had Johnson County under a winter storm warning with wind chills near 17 below and four to six more inches of snow coming. On that kind of morning the bottom seal is frozen to the slab, the drum bearings are stiff with chilled grease, and the first lift of the day asks the spring for more torque than it has left to give.

### The winding cone is where people get hurt

A residential torsion spring holds hundreds of inch-pounds of stored torque and releases it through whatever is sitting in the cone. Bars cut for that cone, worked two at a time, are the only correct tool. A screwdriver shank, a bolt or a length of rebar will kick out. Nothing on a two-spring shaft should be treated as discharged just because one side broke, and the cables stay loaded even then. Keep the door on the floor, unplug the head so nobody presses the wall button out of habit, and resist the urge to lift by hand to judge how bad it is.

### What a rushed swap leaves behind

Fitting one spring to a two-spring shaft is the usual shortcut. New wire and tired wire no longer match in torque, the door climbs with one shoulder high, and the survivor is dragged to its own failure within a season. The second shortcut is reusing cables that already show broken strands where they wrap the drum. The third is bolting the opener back up without balancing anything: a door that will not hold near waist height with the carriage disconnected is not balanced, and turning the force up simply hides that from you until the gear or the top section pays for it.

Matched springs go on as a pair, drums are reseated and both cables tensioned alike, the door is balanced by hand, then travel and force are reset last. In this market a spring job is a parts-and-labor repair with a reasonably tight range, and what moves it is wire size, door width, whether cables and end bearings go at the same time, and whether the visit is a scheduled weekday or a subzero Sunday. An opener is three systems in one housing: a motor and gear train, a safety circuit written into federal rules, and a logic board that sits in an unheated garage through 93 freezing nights a year. Each fails differently.

## svc_garage_door_opener_repair_body
### Three noises, three separate faults

The sound narrows it down faster than any guess about the brand. A motor that hums for a second and stops usually has a starting or control component, or a door too heavy to move. Grinding while the chain sits still points at a stripped drive gear inside the head. A rattling travel with the door creeping and stalling is friction somewhere in the track, not weak electronics. And a motor running its full travel while the door never leaves the floor almost always means the trolley is no longer engaged with the carriage. None of those four conclusions can be drawn from the driveway, but describing which one you hear decides what parts ride out to the appointment.

### The safety circuit is federal, and older than plenty of these garages

Openers manufactured on or after January 1, 1993 have to carry an external entrapment protection device, meaning photo eyes or a door-edge sensor, or else a constant-contact wall button that cannot be closed from a remote at all. The requirement traces back to the 1990 consumer product safety law that pulled UL 325 conformance in for openers built on or after January 1, 1991, plus a warning label at the wall control. That matters in a city where the 2000 census already counted 12,306 housing units built in the 1960s and 9,640 more in the 1970s. Plenty of those garages have had two or three heads bolted to the same ceiling joists, and a reversal problem on a mid-1990s unit is a different repair from one on a head installed last spring.

### Cold weather goes after the electronics and the grease

An attached Overland Park garage is not heated, and the 1991 to 2020 normals put 93.3 nights a year at or below 32 degrees with 26.4 in January and 22.3 in December. Chilled grease in the gear case and on the rail raises the effort the motor has to overcome, which is why low-temperature calcium greases are sold specifically for opener chains, bearings and gears. Battery backup packs lose capacity in the same conditions. Boards that get repeatedly cold and then warmed by a car's engine heat sit through condensation cycles, and a board that misreads its own sensor inputs after a night at 6 degrees is not a board that a force adjustment will fix.

### Turning the force up is not a repair

A close-force dial exists so an installer can match the opener to the door. It is not a way to overrule a door that is dragging. Raise it and the safety reversal that was telling you a roller has seized, a hinge is binding or the seal is frozen down goes quiet, while the same load keeps working on the drive gear and the top section. The correct order is the opposite: disconnect the carriage, move the door by hand through its whole travel, and see whether the resistance is mechanical. If it moves freely by hand and the eyes are clean and aligned, the fault is in the head. If it fights you, the head was never the problem.

### The red cord, and why you do not pull it with the door up

The emergency release drops the door off the opener, and with a healthy spring behind it that is a safe thing to do at the floor. With the door raised, or with a spring already broken, releasing the carriage hands the whole weight to gravity. Outages are the common trigger here: somebody pulls the cord to get a car out, then the trolley never re-engages and the motor runs a full cycle in an empty rail. Reconnecting it takes ten seconds, but the balance check comes first, because coupling an opener to an unbalanced door is how a good head becomes scrap.

### What the visit looks like

The technician starts at the door rather than the ceiling: hand travel, spring balance, roller and hinge condition, track alignment. Then the eyes get checked for alignment, lens film and a pinched wire staple, and the travel limits get read rather than assumed. Only then does the head come apart. A gear and sprocket kit, a capacitor, a logic board or a full replacement are all real outcomes, and so is the finding that the opener is fine and a spring or roller was making it look tired. Rail length, headroom and ceiling obstructions get measured before anything is ordered.

### What it costs, and when replacement is genuinely right

A gear kit or a sensor pair is a modest parts-and-labor repair. A full unit swap is a bigger number, and the range widens with drive type: chain, belt, and wall-mount jackshaft heads all price differently, with the wall-mount option earning its premium in a low-headroom 1960s garage where nothing wants to hang from the ceiling. Replacement makes sense when the head predates external entrapment protection, when parts are no longer made for it, or when a second failure would cost more than the difference. Replacement does not make sense as a way to avoid balancing a door. The pricing page lists what the metro is charging for each of those paths.

### Reading a crooked door before you touch it

Look at the gap along the top edge and along the floor. A door that is level at the header but sitting proud on one side at the slab has lost cable tension on that side. A door with one roller standing outside the track, usually at the radius where vertical track curves into horizontal, has a cracked roller or a spread track. A door with a visible crease across a section has been driven into or has been pulled against something solid. In all three cases the assembly is out of square, the remaining hardware is carrying more than its share, and nothing about the situation improves by pressing the button again.

### The three ways a door leaves its rail

Most off-track calls in Johnson County start one of three ways. A spring breaks, the door drops unevenly, and one cable unwraps its drum and takes up slack in a loose loop. Second, a roller stem wears or a nylon wheel cracks, the wheel stops turning, and it climbs out at the curve where the load changes direction. Third, a vehicle nudges the vertical track at the jamb, moving it a quarter inch, which is enough for a roller to pop out at the same point on every cycle until somebody finds the bend. Reversed order matters too: a bent track can break a cable, and a broken cable can bend a track.

### Ice, grit and the bottom seal

The Kansas City normals count 93.3 nights a year at or below 32 degrees, and December and January contribute nearly 50 of them. Meltwater runs under the door, refreezes at the slab, and grabs the seal. An opener that then pulls against a frozen seal can shear a roller or drag a cable off its drum in one cycle, which is why a door that will not break loose should be left alone rather than helped with the wall button. Driveway grit and ice melt tracked into the track channel does the slower version of the same damage, packing the rail and pitting the rollers until they stop rolling and start sliding.

### What not to attempt in the meantime

Do not lift or lever a door that has a slack cable or a roller out of the track. The counterbalance is no longer acting evenly and the door can come around on you. Do not park a car under a door hanging out of square. Do not clamp vise grips on the track and call it secured. Cut power at the outlet, keep the opening clear, and if the door is stuck partly open, keep the bay empty until it is properly supported. Photographs of the loose cable, the popped roller and the track bracket from a safe distance are far more useful than a hand on the door.

### The repair sequence

The door gets locked in place with clamps and blocking before anything is loosened, so it cannot travel while the cable is off. The failed roller, hinge or cable comes out, the vertical and horizontal track are checked for plumb, spacing and flare, and bent sections are straightened or replaced rather than persuaded. Cables are reset in the drums with matched tension so both sides lift together, and the counterbalance is checked because a broken spring is often the cause rather than a coincidence. Then the door is cycled by hand, the opener is reconnected, and travel and force are reset. A dented skin gets evaluated last, because a cosmetic dent matters far less than a bowed strut behind it.

Call the plans examiner of the day and settle the scope before a door is ordered, not on install morning.

### Insulation against a 6 degree design temperature

The city's own adopted design table assumes a 6 degree winter design temperature, severe weathering, and a required ice barrier underlayment. Against that, a single-skin steel door on an attached garage is a hole in the thermal envelope of the room above it. Insulated sandwich sections cost more, weigh more and need springs sized for the extra weight. They also change how the garage behaves: warmer air at the slab means less ice at the bottom seal, and less ice at the seal means fewer of the drag failures that put rollers and cables on the repair list. Ask for the stated R-value and the section thickness in writing, and ask what spring specification the new weight requires, because the old springs will not carry it.

### Wind, snow and the numbers to make the quote state

Overland Park's table lists a 115 mph wind speed with no special wind region and no windborne debris zone, so no impact-rated assembly is being forced on a residential garage here. Design pressure still applies. DASMA counts a garage door as cladding in the edge and corner zones, and at 115 mph with a 15 foot mean roof height its published guide puts a 16 by 7 foot door near 10 psf inward and 11 psf outward, with an open-exposure lot multiplying that by roughly 1.48. Ground snow load in the same table is 20 psf, which matters for the framing over the opening rather than the door leaf. A quote worth reading names the door's rated design pressure, the track gauge and radius, the strut count, and the reinforcement bracket for the opener arm.

### Measuring an older garage against a newer one

The opening width and height, the headroom above the header, the backroom depth, the sideroom at each jamb, and the ceiling framing all get measured. A late-1950s Nall Hills garage often has minimum headroom, which pushes a low-clearance track and sometimes a wall-mounted opener. A 1990s Deer Creek or LionsGate garage generally has room for standard radius track and a conventional head. Doors in the 66221 subdivisions built after 2000 tend to be the widest and heaviest of the group. The measurement also tells you whether the floor is level, because a slab that has settled at one end leaves a wedge of daylight no door will close out.

### What installation day looks like

The old door comes down section by section after the springs are unwound and the cables released, and the old track and hardware leave with it. The opening gets checked for square and for rot at the jamb, new track is set plumb, sections go up with hinges and rollers as they stack, the counterbalance is wound to the new door's weight, and the opener arm gets its reinforcement bracket. Then the door is balanced by hand, the opener travel and force are set, the reversal is tested against an object on the floor, and the old door is hauled away. A single-section swap is a shorter version of the same sequence with the same balance check at the end.

Insulation level, section style, window inserts, hardware grade and door width move the number most, and haul-away and disposal belong on the estimate as their own line. Spring cycle rating is a line worth paying up on, since a heavier insulated door works its counterbalance harder. Custom colors and discontinued sizes add lead time rather than only money. <h2>The equipment is not a bigger version of a house door</h2>
<p>Three families cover most of what is out there. Sectional steel doors on commercial track, which look residential and are not: heavier gauge, thicker end hinges, larger shafts. ## svc_garage_door_hinge_repair_lede

Hinges and brackets are the joints of a sectional door, and they carry every pound of it through every bend. When one cracks, the door stops tracking square, and the damage moves outward to the section, the roller and eventually the track.

## svc_garage_door_hinge_repair_body

<h2>The hardware is numbered for a reason</h2>
<p>A sectional door uses hinges stamped with a number, commonly one through five, and the number sets the offset that lets the sections stack correctly through the curve. A number three hinge fitted where a number two belongs changes the geometry between two panels by a fraction of an inch, which is enough to bind a roller at the radius and to load one hinge leaf in shear. That is why hinge replacement is a matching job rather than a generic one. It is also why a door that started binding after an earlier repair is worth re-checking against the stack order before anything else on the <a href="../services/">service list</a> gets blamed.</p>
<h2>End hinges, top brackets and bottom brackets do different jobs</h2>
<p>Center hinges only join sections. End hinges carry a roller stem as well, so they take both the joint load and the track load, and they are the ones that tear. The top bracket holds the top roller and the opener arm and sees the highest bending moment on the whole door. The bottom bracket is the one to leave alone: on most doors the lifting cable terminates there, which means it is under the full spring tension whenever the door is down. Loosening a bottom bracket on a closed door with a wound spring is one of the two or three genuinely dangerous mistakes available in a garage.</p>
<h2>Storm damage puts hinges in a different failure mode</h2>
<p>Ordinary wear cracks a hinge at the knuckle. A wind or hail event bends the section itself, and then the hinge is asked to hold two panels that no longer line up. The pressures involved here are not hypothetical: the city designs to a 115 mph basic wind speed, the weather service measured an 89 mph gust in Johnson County on July 14, 2023, and a confirmed EF-1 tornado on May 19, 2024 tracked from just northeast of Overland Park near 79th and Lamar with maximum winds near 95 mph. Hail from the March 2024 storms reached three inches in the western part of the county. A dented, creased or bowed section transfers all of that into the fasteners at its joints.</p>
<h2>Torn stiles are the failure that ends a repair</h2>
<p>Hinges are lagged into the vertical stile at each end of a section. On a wood door the screws pull out of softened stock; on a steel door the thin skin tears around the fastener and the hinge starts to rock in an oval hole. Once the stile is torn, a longer screw is not a repair, and neither is a second hole an inch away. The legitimate options are a reinforcement plate spanning the damage or replacing the section. Which one applies depends on how much sound material is left, and an honest inspection says which. Where several sections are torn, the arithmetic usually favors <a href="../garage-door-replacement/">replacing the door</a> over patching four panels.</p>
<h2>What a cracked hinge does downstream</h2>
<p>A hinge that has failed lets its section drop a few millimeters relative to its neighbor. The roller stem then rides at an angle, the wheel scuffs the track flange instead of rolling on it, and the flange wears a bright groove. Continue past that point and the roller leaves the track entirely, at which point the repair is no longer a hinge; it is the sequence described in <a href="../off-track-garage-door-repair/">off-track repair</a>, usually with a bent flange and a cable off its drum for company. Catching a cracked hinge early is the difference between a small parts job and half a day.</p>
<h2>How the repair is done properly</h2>
<p>The door is clamped or supported so no section carries load through the joint being opened, and on doors where the springs are involved the tension is dealt with first. Damaged hinges come off in matched sets rather than one at a time, because a single new hinge in a row of worn ones concentrates load. Fastener holes that have opened up get moved to sound material or backed with a plate. Rollers are inspected while the hinges are off, since they are already accessible. The door then gets run by hand through full travel to confirm nothing binds at the curve, and the balance is rechecked before the opener is reconnected.</p>

## svc_garage_door_insulation_lede

Insulation numbers on garage doors are advertised in a way that makes comparison difficult on purpose. R-value describes one spot in the middle of a panel. U-factor describes the whole installed door. Only one of them predicts what the garage will feel like in January.

## svc_garage_door_insulation_body

<h2>R-value and U-factor are not two names for the same thing</h2>
<p>The industry's own guidance is blunt about it. R-value is a calculation of thermal resistance through the center of a door section, and it deliberately ignores the rails, stiles, joints and the perimeter. U-factor is a measurement of heat transfer through the complete installed door, determined by a standardized test method, and it is what energy codes reference for garage doors. The two are not reciprocals of each other and cannot be converted. Lower U is better, and a door at U-0.20 loses heat at half the rate of a door at U-0.40. Anyone comparing quotes from the <a href="../services/">service list</a> should ask for the U-factor, because that is the number that includes the parts of the door that leak.</p>
<h2>What zone 4A means for a garage in this county</h2>
<p>Johnson County falls in climate zone 4 with a moist regime, the mixed-humid category, which means the garage is fighting winter heat loss and summer humidity in the same year. The city designs to a winter figure well below zero wind chill conditions and counts 93.3 nights a year at or below freezing, then turns around and delivers July normal highs of 87 to 89 degrees. An uninsulated steel door is a single sheet of metal between those conditions and the car. Where a bonus room or bedroom sits above the garage, as in a large share of the two-story plans built around LionsGate and Mills Farm, that door is effectively part of the thermal envelope of a living space.</p>
<h2>Retrofit kits versus an insulated door</h2>
<p>A retrofit kit is rigid polystyrene board or a faced batt cut to fit the inside of each panel, held by clips or friction. On a flat pan door with real depth it makes a measurable difference and costs little. Its limits are honest ones: it does nothing for the joints between sections, nothing for the perimeter, and nothing for a door with shallow panel embossing that leaves no cavity. A factory door with polyurethane foamed in place bonds to both skins, gives a much better whole-door number, and adds stiffness that matters on a wide double door. Polystyrene board inside a steel shell sits between the two on both performance and price.</p>
<h2>Weight changes the mechanics, and the springs have to know</h2>
<p>This is the step that gets skipped. Adding insulation adds weight, sometimes twenty or thirty pounds on a double door with a retrofit kit and considerably more when a single-layer door is replaced by a three-layer one. The counterbalance is sized to the door's actual weight, so the springs have to be recalculated: wire diameter, inside diameter, length and the number of turns. Leave the original springs under a heavier door and the door becomes bottom-heavy, the opener carries load it was never meant to carry, and the drive gear wears early. Any competent insulation job ends with a hand balance test, and if it does not, it is not finished. Spring specification detail sits with <a href="../garage-door-spring-repair/">spring repair</a>.</p>
<h2>The air film, and why the door alone is not the answer</h2>
<p>A closed door has a still-air film on each face worth roughly R-0.85 combined, most of it on the inside, and that number shrinks as soon as wind moves across the exterior. The practical lesson is that a good door in a leaky opening underperforms badly. Perimeter stops, a sound bottom astragal and a threshold that keeps standing water off the bottom section do as much for comfort as a jump of one insulation tier, and they cost far less. Sequence matters: seal the opening under <a href="../garage-door-weather-seal-replacement/">weather seal replacement</a> first, then decide how much door to buy.</p>
<h2>What to specify when the goal is a warm garage</h2>
<p>Ask for the U-factor determined by the recognized test method rather than a center-of-panel R number. Ask for the section construction: two skins with foamed polyurethane, two skins with board stock, or a single pan with a kit. Ask what the finished door weighs and what spring specification is going on it. Ask whether the perimeter stops and astragal are included, because a door installed against forty-year-old wood stops will not deliver its rating. If the room above the garage is the actual complaint, the ceiling and the attic hatch usually deserve attention at the same time, and no door alone will fix a garage with an uninsulated ceiling.</p>

<h2>The hardware families you will actually find</h2>
<p>Three arrangements cover nearly every door here. A T-handle through the center of a section, connected by two flat lock bars that shoot into slots in the vertical track on both sides. <h2>What gets replaced and what it costs</h2>
<p>Lock work is a parts-light, labor-light job in most cases: a handle assembly, a pair of lock bars, a spring, sometimes a track slot plate. It also frees the whole ceiling for storage, which is often the real reason people pick it.</p>
<h2>Reinforcement, rails and the parts that get skipped</h2>
<p>A sectional door's top section was not built to be pushed by an arm. Nearly every manufacturer requires a horizontal strut across that section before an opener attaches, and where one is missing the arm eventually bends the panel around its own bracket. Rail length has to match the door height, and an eight-foot door needs an extension kit rather than an optimistic install. Where headroom is short, a reduced-radius track or a low-headroom rail bracket keeps the door from binding at the curve. Skipping any of these produces a unit that works on the day it is fitted and a cracked top section two winters later, which is the most common avoidable failure on this job.</p>
<h2>Safety hardware is not an accessory</h2>
<p>Rules that took effect for openers manufactured on and after January 1, 1993 require either an external entrapment protection device, meaning photoelectric eyes or an edge sensor, or a constant-contact wall control that reverses on release and cannot close the door by remote. The eyes belong no higher than six inches above the floor and within six inches of the door's path. Fitting them at bumper height because the bracket lands there is a defeat of the whole point. The install also includes setting travel limits against the actual slab, setting close force to the door's real weight, and finishing with a reversal test against an object on the floor rather than a shrug.</p>
<h2>Battery backup earns its keep here</h2>
<p>A head with an integrated battery will cycle a door a limited number of times with no grid power, and in this county that is not a theoretical benefit. The July 14, 2023 wind event took down power poles a mile west of the city, and the same squall lines that dent doors also drop circuits for hours. Without a battery, the way out is the manual release and a hand lift, which requires an intact counterbalance and a door that is fully closed. Households with one exit through the garage tend to decide this question quickly once it has happened to them once, and the surcharge for a battery head is smaller than most expect.</p>
<h2>Permits, licensing and what happens after</h2>
<p>Swapping an opener on an existing door is service work on existing equipment, not a structural alteration, and the city publishes thirteen categories of work exempt from a building permit. Where an install requires a new circuit, the electrical side falls under the code package the city has adopted, and a building permit will only be issued to a contractor holding an active Johnson County contractor license. After commissioning, keep the number of enrolled transmitters small and deliberate, which is covered in <a href="../garage-door-remote-programming/">remote and keypad work</a>, and if phone control is part of the goal, decide that before the head is chosen rather than bolting on a <a href="../smart-garage-door-opener/">smart retrofit</a> afterward.</p>

## svc_garage_door_remote_programming_lede

Remotes and keypads fail on their own terms. Nothing is bent, nothing is under tension, and the door works perfectly from the wall button. What has broken is a radio conversation, and the fix is a pairing sequence, a battery, or a receiver that has stopped listening.

## svc_garage_door_remote_programming_body

<h2>Establish which half of the link died</h2>
<p>The wall button is hardwired, so it is the control group. If the door runs from the wall and ignores every handheld, the problem is on the radio side: transmitters, the antenna wire, or the receiver in the head. If one remote works and a second does not, the dead remote is almost certainly a battery or a lost pairing rather than a fault in the opener. If the outside keypad is the only thing not responding, suspect its own battery and its own enrollment, because a keypad is a separate transmitter with a separate memory slot. Everything else on the <a href="../services/">list of garage door repairs</a> starts with a mechanical inspection. This one starts by pressing three buttons in a fixed order.</p>
<h2>Rolling codes changed what pairing means</h2>
<p>Openers built through the 1980s used a bank of dip switches, and a matching pair of switch positions was the whole security model. Anything modern uses a rolling code: the transmitter and receiver both advance through a sequence after every press, so a captured signal is worthless a second later. The practical consequence is that you cannot copy a rolling-code remote by setting switches. It has to be enrolled at the head, usually by holding the learn button until an indicator lights and then pressing the remote once within thirty seconds. Common carrier frequencies sit near 315 and 390 megahertz depending on the generation, and a remote built for one will not talk to the other regardless of how the buttons are pressed.</p>
<h2>Memory slots fill, and old openers forget</h2>
<p>Most residential heads hold a limited number of transmitters, often between six and twenty depending on the model. A house on its second set of cars, with a keypad, two visor remotes, two key fobs and a car-integrated button, can quietly hit that ceiling, and the symptom is that enrolling a new remote silently drops the oldest one. Clearing the entire memory and re-enrolling every device in one sitting is the fix, and it is also the correct response when a used vehicle arrives with someone else's opener still programmed into its mirror. Deer Creek and LionsGate houses from the late 1990s and early 2000s are often on their second head, which means two receivers and two generations of remote may both be in the garage.</p>
<h2>Cold is what kills a keypad, not age</h2>
<p>An outside keypad is bolted to the jamb on the unheated side of the wall, and it runs on a coin cell or a nine-volt battery. Across 93.3 freezing nights a year, with 26.4 of them in January, alkaline chemistry loses a meaningful share of its usable capacity, and the first symptom is a keypad that works in the afternoon and does nothing at seven in the morning. Owners read that as a failed keypad. Usually it is a battery that has three months left in a warm room and three weeks on a north-facing wall. Backlight membranes also crack when they are pressed with a gloved thumb at ten degrees, and a cracked membrane lets in moisture that shorts the pad permanently.</p>
<h2>What interference looks like from the driveway</h2>
<p>Range loss is a distinct symptom from total failure. If the door only responds from ten feet away when it used to work from the corner of the street, something is either loading the antenna or crowding the band. The antenna is a bare wire hanging from the head, and it stops working when it is coiled, taped to the rail, or tucked behind a metal shelf. LED shop lights and some low-voltage transformers emit broadband noise across the same frequencies, and unplugging them one at a time is a legitimate diagnostic. A repeated pattern worth checking here: the range collapses the week after the garage lighting is changed.</p>
<h2>What gets replaced, and what should not</h2>
<p>A dead handheld is a battery, then a transmitter. A dead keypad is a battery, then a keypad. A head that will not enroll anything, or whose learn indicator does not respond, usually needs a receiver board, and on units past their parts life that decision folds into <a href="../garage-door-opener-installation/">installing a new opener</a> instead. What should not happen is replacing a working opener because the remotes stopped talking to it. If the goal is phone control rather than more handhelds, the retrofit path in <a href="../smart-garage-door-opener/">smart opener work</a> is usually the cheaper and more useful answer. Keep the number of enrolled transmitters deliberately small, and clear the memory whenever a car or a house changes hands.</p>

## svc_garage_door_safety_inspection_lede

A safety inspection is a documented pass or fail on the parts of a garage door that can hurt someone. It is a different service from a tune-up: nothing gets lubricated to make the report look better, and the deliverable is a list of what failed and why.

## svc_garage_door_safety_inspection_body

<h2>What the federal rule actually requires</h2>
<p>The law behind all of this is specific. Openers manufactured on and after January 1, 1991 were required to conform to the entrapment protection provisions of the applicable safety standard, and following final rules published in December 1992, units manufactured on and after January 1, 1993 had to meet the revised requirements. Compliance takes one of two forms: an external entrapment protection device, meaning photoelectric eyes or an edge sensor, or a constant-contact wall control that reverses on release and cannot close the door by remote. A warning label near the wall button is part of the requirement. So the first question an inspection answers is what the opener's date of manufacture is, which is on the label on the head, and what protection it is supposed to have.</p>
<h2>The tests, and the order they run in</h2>
<p>Reversal on contact comes first: a flat two-by-four laid on the floor in the door's path, the door run down, and the door must reverse on touching it. Then the entrapment device: an object passed through the beam at shin height during closing, with the door reversing to full open. Then beam geometry, which has a published target of no higher than six inches above the floor and within six inches of the door's path. Then the manual release, tested with the door fully closed. Then a hand balance test through full travel. Then the mechanical audit: springs, cables at both drums, containment cables on extension systems, hinges, stiles, rollers, track alignment, bearing plates and fastener torque. Everything on the <a href="../services/">repair list</a> traces back to one of those findings.</p>
<h2>Why the interval is monthly, not annual</h2>
<p>The agency that wrote the opener rules recommends testing the reversing feature every month, and the reason is the death toll that produced the rule: 54 children between the ages of two and fourteen died in garage door incidents in the decade before the standard took effect. A reversal test is thirty seconds and needs no tools. What an annual professional inspection adds is the parts nobody can see from the floor, cable strands lifting at the drum, a hairline crack at a spring cone, a torn stile behind a hinge leaf, and a written record. A tune-up covers the maintenance side of the same visit and is described under <a href="../garage-door-tune-up/">tune-up work</a>.</p>
<h2>The findings that come up most often on older doors</h2>
<p>In a city where the median housing unit dates to 1989 and 12,306 units were finished in the 1960s, the pattern is consistent. Openers predating the 1993 requirement, still running with no photo eyes. Extension springs with no containment cable. Sensors mounted at bumper height because that is where the bracket landed. Down limits set so far past the slab that the door bottoms out and rebounds. Close force turned up to defeat a reversal that was warning about a mechanical bind. And bottom brackets that a previous owner unbolted while the spring was wound, which is the finding that ends an inspection on the spot.</p>
<h2>What this inspection is not</h2>
<p>It is not a city inspection and it does not substitute for one. Overland Park's own inspections are requested through the city, scheduled for the next business day when the request comes in before four in the afternoon, and the city does not accept third-party inspections in place of its own. A garage door safety inspection is a private mechanical report, useful in a rental turnover, before a sale, or when a household has small children and an opener of unknown age. It has no code standing, and anybody presenting one as a municipal sign-off is misrepresenting it.</p>
<h2>The report, and what to do with it</h2>
<p>A usable report names each test, the result, and the specific part behind any failure, with the opener's model and date of manufacture recorded. Findings sort into three groups: take the door out of service now, repair on a schedule, and monitor. A broken containment cable, a lifting cable strand or a failed reversal belongs in the first group, and the honest recommendation there is to disconnect the opener and leave the door down until it is fixed. Where the failure is on the sensing side, <a href="../garage-door-sensor-repair/">photo eye repair</a> is usually a same-visit fix; where it is an opener too old to comply at all, replacement is the only route to a passing result.</p>

## svc_garage_door_sensor_repair_lede

A door that starts down, drops a foot, then climbs straight back up has not lost a spring or a motor. It is being told to stop by two photo eyes mounted six inches off the slab, and in Overland Park those brackets spend January standing in meltwater.

## svc_garage_door_sensor_repair_body

<h2>A reversal at the floor is the circuit working</h2>
<p>Nothing about this failure is a broken part in the usual sense. The opener sends the door down, the beam between the two eyes breaks or goes intermittent, and the logic board does exactly what federal rule requires of it: reverse to full open. Most owners read that as the opener dying and start pricing new heads. It is worth ruling the cheap thing out first. The rest of the repairs behind the <a href="../services/">services index</a> involve tension, weight or steel; this one involves a lens, a bracket and about twenty feet of two-conductor wire. The tell is that the door goes up perfectly every time and only fails on the way down.</p>
<h2>Six inches off the floor, and within six inches of the door</h2>
<p>The mounting position is not a matter of taste. DASMA's guidance on photoelectric sensor location states that each eye lens, or the lowest lenses where several sets are used, is to emit a beam no higher than six inches above the garage floor, and that the sensors should sit within six inches of the door's path measured perpendicular to the door. They are usually placed up to twenty feet apart. That geometry is what puts a child's leg inside the protected zone rather than above it. It also means the hardware lives at the exact height where a car tire throws slush, where a rake handle gets leaned, and where a snow shovel swings.</p>
<h2>What a Johnson County winter does to a bracket at ankle height</h2>
<p>The Kansas City normals count 93.3 nights a year at or below freezing, with 26.4 of them in January. Meltwater from a warm engine bay runs down the slope of the slab, pools at the opening, and refreezes overnight against the two lowest brackets in the building. Add the grit and ice melt tracked in off the driveway and the bracket screws corrode, the sheet-metal tab flexes, and the beam wanders out of alignment by a degree or two. That is enough. A sensor pair that worked all autumn starts failing on cold mornings and working again by noon, which is why this fault so often gets described as intermittent rather than broken.</p>
<h2>Wiring in a garage that has outlived three openers</h2>
<p>The city counted 12,306 housing units built in the 1960s and 9,640 more in the 1970s, and the garages under those roofs are on their third or fourth opener head. Each swap left staples in the jamb, and the small-gauge bell wire that feeds the eyes gets pinched under one of them, nicked by a shelf bracket, or spliced with a twist and a wrap of tape behind the door track. In a 1956 Nall Hills garage with low headroom, that wire often runs across the header where the door itself brushes it. An intermittent short there behaves identically to a misaligned lens, and the only way to separate the two is to read the sending unit's indicator and then check continuity along the run.</p>
<h2>The sequence that actually finds it</h2>
<p>Start with the lenses: fog, cobweb, a film of exhaust residue. Then read the two LEDs. Most units light the sender steadily and blink the receiver when the beam is lost, and the pattern narrows it to power, alignment or wire before anything is unbolted. Loosen the wing nut, sight the receiver at the sender, retighten, and confirm the beam holds with the door in motion rather than at rest. Wire faults get found by ohming the pair from the head down. The finish is a live test: a two-by-four laid flat on the floor under the door, and a hand-held object at shin height on a second pass. If the door reverses on both, the circuit is honest again.</p>
<h2>When it is not the eyes at all</h2>
<p>Two other faults imitate this one closely enough to matter. A travel limit set too far down makes the door hit the slab and rebound, which looks like a reversal but happens with the eyes reading clear; that belongs with <a href="../garage-door-opener-repair/">opener repair</a>. A door that binds on a seized roller partway down also trips the force sensor and reverses, and the way to tell is to pull the release and move it by hand. If the descent is uneven or stiff, read <a href="../garage-door-wont-close/">why a door refuses to close</a> before buying sensors. A sensor pair is inexpensive; a sensor pair fitted to a door with a mechanical problem is a wasted visit.</p>

## svc_garage_door_tune_up_lede

A tune-up is not a cosmetic service. It is a measurement session with a wrench, and the measurement that matters is whether the counterbalance still holds the door at waist height with the opener disconnected.

## svc_garage_door_tune_up_body

<h2>The one number the whole visit turns on</h2>
<p>A residential door system carries a cycle rating, and ANSI/DASMA 102 sets a minimum of ten thousand cycles for the door and its hardware. One cycle is one open and one close. A household that leaves twice a day and comes back twice a day burns roughly fifteen hundred cycles a year, which puts a builder-grade spring at the end of its designed life somewhere around year seven. That arithmetic is why a tune-up has a schedule rather than a trigger: by the time a door sounds wrong, it has usually already spent its margin. The rest of the <a href="../services/">service list</a> is what happens when the number runs out unattended.</p>
<h2>What gets measured, in order</h2>
<p>Balance first, with the trolley disconnected: the door should stay roughly where it is left through the middle of its travel. Then spring condition, read off the coils, the cones and the wind on the shaft rather than guessed. Then cable condition at both drums, looking for the strand that has begun to lift. Then roller stems and wheel play, hinge pin wear, and track alignment at the curve. Then fastener torque across every hinge, bracket and bearing plate. Then the opener: chain or belt deflection, the down limit against the slab, close force, and finally the reversal test with an object on the floor. Nothing on that list is optional, and the results are worth writing down so next year's visit has a baseline.</p>
<h2>Lubrication is a materials decision, not a spray can</h2>
<p>Hinge pins, roller stems, spring coils and end bearings take a light lithium or silicone grease, applied thin. The track face takes nothing. Penetrating oil belongs nowhere on the assembly, because it displaces what little lubricant remains in a sealed bearing and then evaporates. Locally this matters more than it sounds. Across 93.3 nights a year at or below freezing, grease thickens and the door leaves the slab against real added drag; then July normals of 87 to 89 degrees in a mixed-humid climate thin the same grease and let it run off the coils. A door here gets worked from both ends of the year, and the correct lubricant is the one that stays put through both.</p>
<h2>The reversal test belongs to the homeowner too</h2>
<p>Federal safety rules that took effect for openers manufactured on and after January 1, 1993 require an external entrapment protection device or a constant-contact wall control, and the agency behind them advises testing the reversing feature every month. That is a household task, not a technician's annual task. The method is a flat two-by-four laid on the floor in the door's path; the door should touch it and reverse. If it does not, the door stays disconnected until it does. The same guidance came with a warning label requirement near the wall button, and on a garage that has been through several openers, that label is often the only original piece left.</p>
<h2>Doors that need the visit sooner than others</h2>
<p>Wide double doors take more of the load and show it first, and the wind exposure here is real: the city designs to a 115 mph basic wind speed, which sets the bar for what a large unsupported panel is expected to survive. Doors on 1960s and 1970s hardware, which describes a large share of the 12,306 units built in that first decade of expansion, often still run extension springs and stamped rollers, and those benefit most from attention. Insulated doors ordered after 2000 in neighborhoods like Mills Farm weigh more per section than the doors they resemble, so their springs and their opener gears wear on a shorter schedule than the sticker suggests.</p>
<h2>What the visit does not cover</h2>
<p>A tune-up is maintenance, not a rebuild. If a spring has already broken, a cable has parted, or a section is cracked around a hinge, those are separate repairs and get quoted separately rather than folded into an hourly visit. A tune-up also is not a code inspection; it is a mechanical service. When the goal is a documented pass or fail on the safety systems, in a rental turnover or a sale, ask for a <a href="../garage-door-safety-inspection/">safety inspection</a> instead, and if the complaint that prompted the call was sound rather than function, start with <a href="../noisy-garage-door-repair/">noise diagnosis</a> so the right parts are on the truck.</p>

## svc_garage_door_weather_seal_replacement_lede

The rubber strip under the door is the cheapest part on the assembly and the one that decides whether water, grit and cold air get into the garage. When it fails, the damage shows up somewhere else: a rusting bottom section, a frozen door, a nest behind the drywall.

## svc_garage_door_weather_seal_replacement_body

<h2>Four separate seals, four separate failures</h2>
<p>People say weather seal and mean the bottom strip, but a sectional door has four. The astragal is the flexible bulb or T-shaped strip in the retainer along the bottom edge. The perimeter stop moulding runs up both jambs and across the header and seals the door against the frame. The threshold is a separate adhesive ramp bonded to the slab, used where the concrete has settled or slopes the wrong way. And between each pair of sections there is an interlocking joint seal that is part of the panel profile. Each one fails on its own schedule, so the first job on any visit through the <a href="../services/">service list</a> is naming which of the four is actually leaking.</p>
<h2>The bottom strip and the slab it lands on</h2>
<p>An astragal is held by an aluminum retainer with one or two channels, and it fails by tearing at the lip, going stiff and losing its memory, or pulling out of the retainer at one end. What matters locally is what happens next. Meltwater and rain that should be shed at the opening instead run under the door and stand against the lowest section, and Johnson County sits in a mixed-humid climate with July precipitation normals near 4.58 inches. Steel bottom sections are the least protected part of a door, and a bottom edge that spends the year damp corrodes from the inside of the hem outward, which is not repairable once it has perforated.</p>
<h2>Freeze-thaw is what turns a seal into a service call</h2>
<p>Cold alone does not tear rubber. The cycle does. On any of the 26.4 January nights at or below freezing, water trapped between a stiff astragal and the concrete freezes, bonds the strip to the slab, and then gets torn free the next morning by an opener pulling with its full close force. Do that a dozen times and the lip is shredded on one side. The related failure is worse: a ridge of ice remains under the door, the door will not seat, the opener trips its force sensor and reverses, and if it does not, it drags a cable off the drum. That whole sequence is described under <a href="../garage-door-wont-close/">a door that will not close</a>, and a healthy seal with a proper threshold removes the cause.</p>
<h2>Older openings need the perimeter stops, not just the strip</h2>
<p>In garages from the 1960s and 1970s, and there were 12,306 units built in that decade alone, the door frame is usually wood with a nailed-on wooden stop, and forty years of sun and water have left it split and pulled loose. Replacing the bottom strip on a door whose jamb stops are gone accomplishes very little, because the daylight gap is up the sides. The correct repair replaces the stop moulding with a flexible-lipped vinyl or a new wood stop with a separate seal, set to touch the door face without dragging on it. Where the jamb itself has rotted at the bottom six inches, that gets cut out and replaced first, since no seal fastens to soft wood.</p>
<h2>Getting the threshold decision right</h2>
<p>A bonded threshold is the right answer when the slab has settled away from the opening or was poured with a low spot, and it is the wrong answer when the driveway drains toward the garage. In that second case a threshold turns the garage into a shallow basin the first time it holds water on the outside. The test is a hose and five minutes of watching. Where the slope is genuinely against the building, the durable fix is drainage or a saw-cut channel outside the opening, not a rubber dam. Installers who skip that test produce a garage that is dry in a drizzle and floods in the kind of downpour that a July here supplies routinely.</p>
<h2>Cost, timing and what it protects</h2>
<p>This is a low-cost job in parts and a moderate one in labor, since the retainer often has to be cleaned out end to end and the door lifted to work the strip in. Doing it before the bottom section rusts is the whole economic argument, because a corroded section becomes a panel order. Rodent entry is the other reason people finally call: a quarter-inch gap at a corner is an open door in November. If the garage is heated or shares a wall with living space, a new seal only matters in combination with the door's actual thermal rating, which is the subject of <a href="../garage-door-insulation/">insulation and U-factor work</a>.</p>

## svc_garage_door_wont_close_lede

This is the most common call of the year and it is a diagnosis, not a part. A door that opens fine and refuses to close is being stopped by one of about six things, and four of them cost nothing to check standing in your own garage.

## svc_garage_door_wont_close_body

<h2>Watch what the opener does, precisely</h2>
<p>The behavior narrows the cause faster than any guess. If the light bulbs flash a set number of times and the door never leaves the top, the head has detected a fault in its safety circuit and is telling you the count. If the door travels down and reverses immediately at the floor, it either found an obstruction or its down limit is set past the slab. If the motor hums and nothing moves, the trolley may be disconnected from the carriage. If the wall button closes the door while the remote only opens it, you are looking at a radio fault, not a closing fault. Those four outcomes lead to four different pages under the <a href="../services/">repair list</a>, so the observation is worth thirty seconds.</p>
<h2>Start with the two things you can see from where you are standing</h2>
<p>First, the manual lock. Every older door here has a slide bolt or a T-handle with a side latch, and a bolt that has drifted into the track leaves the opener straining against a locked door. Second, the photo eyes. They belong no higher than six inches above the floor and within six inches of the door's path, which means they are the two most easily knocked components in the building. A bicycle wheel, a recycling bin or a bag of mulch left in the beam produces exactly this symptom. So does a lens fogged by exhaust film. Both checks are free and together they account for a large share of these calls.</p>
<h2>January in this county produces its own version of this fault</h2>
<p>A door frozen to the slab will not close for a reason that has nothing to do with sensors. Meltwater runs off a warm car, pools at the low edge of the opening, and refreezes overnight; on one of the 26.4 January nights at or below freezing, the vinyl astragal bonds to the concrete. What owners usually see is the reverse case: the door comes up, breaking the ice, and then will not seat because a ridge of ice remains under the seal. Pressing the button again is the expensive choice. The opener will pull the door onto that ridge, trip the force sensor, and reverse, and in the worse version it drags a cable off its drum before it gives up.</p>
<h2>Force and limit adjustments are not a repair</h2>
<p>Every head has a close-force setting and a down-limit setting, and both exist so an installer can match the unit to a specific door. Turning the force up to defeat a reversal removes the only warning you were getting. The load that tripped it, a seized roller, a bent track flange, a frozen seal, a spring that has lost torque, keeps working on the drive gear and the top section instead. Setting the down limit too low is the same mistake in the other direction: the door bottoms out, the opener keeps driving, the sections compress, and the safety reversal fires. The correct order is always to disconnect the carriage first and move the door by hand.</p>
<h2>The hand test tells you which half of the system is at fault</h2>
<p>With the door fully closed, pull the emergency release, then lift the door by hand through its whole travel. It should move with modest effort and stay put around waist height when you let go. If it drops or races upward, the counterbalance is out and the opener was covering for it. If it binds at a particular point, mark that point; a roller, a hinge or a track flange lives there. If it moves freely and holds, the mechanical side is sound and the fault is electrical: eyes, wiring, limits or the board. Never pull the release with the door raised, because the weight it hands back to you is whatever the spring is no longer carrying.</p>
<h2>What gets fixed, and where it goes from here</h2>
<p>The outcomes in rough order of frequency are an obstructed or misaligned beam, a fogged lens, a frozen or damaged bottom seal, a corroded sensor wire, a down limit out of adjustment, an engaged manual lock, and a mechanical bind. Only the last two demand parts on the truck. If the beam is the problem, the detail sits in <a href="../garage-door-sensor-repair/">sensor repair</a>. If ice at the slab is the annual event, the durable answer is <a href="../garage-door-weather-seal-replacement/">replacing the weather seal and threshold</a> so water stops standing where the door lands. Until it is sorted, leave the door open and clear rather than parking a car under something that has already refused to seat once.</p>

## svc_noisy_garage_door_repair_lede

A door that has grown loud did not get loud overnight, and the noise is specific enough to name the part. Grinding, squealing, popping and rumbling come from four different places, and the one you can hear from the bedroom above the garage is rarely the one that is failing.

## svc_noisy_garage_door_repair_body

<h2>Sort the sound before touching anything</h2>
<p>Noise diagnosis works because each component fails with its own signature. A rhythmic rumble that rises and falls with travel speed is rollers. A single sharp bang near the top of the opening is usually a hinge or a strut fastener releasing under load. A metallic squeal that continues for a second after the door stops is the torsion bar turning in a dry end bearing. A grinding sound from the ceiling with the chain motionless is the drive gear inside the head, which belongs with the opener rather than the door. Each of the four leads somewhere different in the <a href="../services/">service list</a>, and mistaking one for another is how people buy a lubricant kit for a problem that needed a bearing plate.</p>
<h2>Rollers are the usual answer in an older garage</h2>
<p>The stamped-steel rollers that came on 1970s doors have no bearing race worth the name, and the Oak Park association's older subdivisions, with Perry Lane houses dating to 1970, still carry plenty of them. Once the wheel stops spinning it starts sliding, and a sliding roller polishes a bright stripe inside the track while transmitting every irregularity into the framing. Nylon rollers with sealed bearings cut the noise substantially and last longer, and they are cheap enough per door that replacing all ten or twelve at once is the sensible move rather than swapping the two that squeal. The stems get checked at the same time, because a worn stem lets the wheel cock in the track and undoes the benefit.</p>
<h2>Grit and grease behave differently across the year</h2>
<p>Two local conditions work on the same hardware from opposite directions. Through winter, with 93.3 nights a year at or below freezing, factory grease in the drums and end bearings stiffens, and the door leaves the floor against measurably more drag. The ice melt and driveway sand that ride in on the tires get packed into the track channel and act as grinding compound on the roller wheels. Then summer arrives, with normal July highs of 87 to 89 degrees and a mixed-humid climate zone, and the same grease thins and runs while condensation sits on bare steel. The result is that a door here rarely wears smoothly. It wears in two directions and gets noisy sooner than a published maintenance interval would suggest.</p>
<h2>The wrong lubricant makes it worse for a month</h2>
<p>Spray penetrating oil is not a lubricant for this hardware. It thins whatever is left in the bearing, quiets the door for a few weeks, then leaves the race drier than before and collects dust. The correct materials are a light lithium or silicone grease on the hinge pins, roller stems, spring coils and end bearings, applied sparingly, with nothing at all on the track face. Grease inside the track is the single most common self-inflicted noise complaint, because it turns a running surface into a paste that holds sand. If the door was recently sprayed and got louder, that is the sequence, and the fix is cleaning the channel rather than adding more.</p>
<h2>Hardware that has loosened rather than worn</h2>
<p>A sectional door is held together by hinges numbered by position and by lag screws driven into wood stiles. Roughly ten thousand cycles of load reversal walks those fasteners loose, and a loose hinge leaf makes a flat popping sound each time it takes the bend at the curve. The bearing plates at each end of the torsion shaft are bolted through the header and do the same thing. Retorquing the whole assembly, in order, with an eye for stripped holes that need to be moved rather than driven harder, is what a proper visit includes. Where the wood is torn out around a hinge, the honest answer is <a href="../garage-door-hinge-repair/">hinge and bracket repair</a>, not a longer screw.</p>
<h2>What a quiet door costs, and what keeps it quiet</h2>
<p>Rollers, hinge pins, bearings, a chain or belt adjustment and a full retorque is a labor-led job with a fairly tight range, and it usually returns a door to something close to its original sound level. What moves the number is how many rollers, whether the end bearings and bearing plates are going too, and whether a section has already cracked around a hinge. Doors above about 112 square feet carry heavier hardware and more of it. Once quiet, staying quiet is a matter of scheduled attention rather than luck, which is the whole argument for a <a href="../garage-door-tune-up/">tune-up on a set interval</a> instead of waiting for the next noise.</p>

## svc_smart_garage_door_opener_lede

Phone control comes two ways: a new head with a radio built in, or a small controller wired to an existing opener. Both work, they fail differently, and neither one adds a single piece of safety hardware to the door.

## svc_smart_garage_door_opener_body

<h2>Retrofit or replace, decided by the head you already own</h2>
<p>A retrofit controller wires into the existing wall-button terminals and adds a door-position sensor, so a working opener of almost any age can gain app control for the price of a small module and an hour of labor. That is the right call when the head is sound and the door is balanced. Replacement makes more sense when the opener is already noisy, the drive gear is worn, or the unit predates the safety rules that took effect for openers manufactured on and after January 1, 1993, because a retrofit on an opener with no photo eyes gives you remote closing on a door with no external entrapment protection. The rest of the <a href="../services/">service list</a> covers the mechanical half; this page is only about control.</p>
<h2>Unattended closing is the part worth thinking about</h2>
<p>The point of the app is closing the door from somewhere else, and that removes the person who used to be standing there watching. Manufacturers address it with an audible and visual warning before an unattended close begins, and the reason that exists is the failure mode: something in the opening that the eyes do not see, a bicycle leaning against the jamb, a pet under the path near the wall. Testing the reversal before enabling remote closing is not optional in practice. A two-by-four laid flat on the floor, a door that touches and reverses, and only then the app. The agency behind the federal opener rules recommends that same test monthly, and it matters far more once the door can close with nobody home.</p>
<h2>Wi-Fi in an attached garage is a real engineering problem</h2>
<p>The garage is usually the far corner of the house, behind a firewall assembly and often with a steel door between it and the router. Signal there is routinely twenty decibels down from the kitchen, and a marginal link produces exactly the complaint people report: the app works most of the time and fails when it is needed. A mesh node or an access point in the garage fixes it more reliably than moving the opener. Some controllers also run their own bridge on a proprietary radio, which sidesteps Wi-Fi entirely at the cost of another box to power. Where the house is a late-1990s two-story in Deer Creek with the router on the opposite end, plan the network before ordering the opener.</p>
<h2>Cold, batteries and the sensor on the door</h2>
<p>Most retrofit kits use a battery-powered tilt or contact sensor mounted on the top section, and that sensor lives on the unheated side of the wall through 93.3 freezing nights a year, 26.4 of them in January. Coin cells give up a large share of their capacity at those temperatures, and a sensor that has gone quiet makes the app report the door's state incorrectly, which is worse than no app at all. Checking that battery on a schedule, and treating a stale status reading as a battery symptom rather than a software bug, saves most of the support calls. Wall-mounted keypads on the same wall behave the same way for the same reason.</p>
<h2>What to ask before buying anything</h2>
<p>Four questions settle most of it. Does the controller support the existing opener's terminal type and its safety-reversing logic, or does it require a compatible-brand head. Does it need a dedicated hub. Does the manufacturer charge for notifications, guest access or camera storage after the first year. And can the door still be operated from the wall button, a handheld and the manual release when the internet is down, which it will be. A control layer that becomes the only way to open the door is a design mistake, not a feature.</p>
<h2>Where this fits with the rest of the work</h2>
<p>The honest order is mechanical first, control second. Balance the door, replace worn rollers and confirm the reversal, then add the radio. A smart controller bolted to a door that binds at the curve simply gives you a phone notification about a door that was already failing. If the head itself is at the end of its life, do the <a href="../garage-door-opener-installation/">new opener</a> and get the radio built in rather than layering a module onto a unit with a worn gear. And if handheld remotes and the outside keypad are the actual complaint, that is <a href="../garage-door-remote-programming/">remote and keypad work</a> and costs a fraction of this.</p>

## svc_torsion_spring_conversion_lede

Converting a door from extension springs to a torsion shaft is not a repair, it is a redesign of the counterbalance. It is worth doing on a specific set of doors and a waste of money on others, and headroom above the opening decides most of it.

## svc_torsion_spring_conversion_body

<h2>Two ways to counterbalance the same door</h2>
<p>An extension spring system uses a pair of long springs stretched along the horizontal tracks, one per side, each pulling through a pulley and cable arrangement. A torsion system uses one or two springs wound around a shaft mounted above the opening, delivering torque to cable drums at each end. Extension hardware was cheaper to build and is what most builders used through the 1960s and 1970s, which covers the 12,306 units finished here in that first decade and the 9,640 that followed in the next. Torsion has become standard because it controls the door on both sides from a single shaft. Both are legitimate systems when maintained; the <a href="../services/">service list</a> covers repair of either.</p>
<h2>Why conversion is considered at all</h2>
<p>Three reasons hold up. First, balance: a torsion shaft applies matched torque to both cable drums, so a wide door is less prone to racking than one pulled by two independent springs that have aged differently. Second, containment: an extension spring that breaks under tension becomes a projectile unless it is threaded with a containment cable, and a torsion spring that breaks stays on its shaft. Third, service life: torsion springs are ordered by cycle rating, so a 20,000-cycle pair can be specified where a builder fitted the minimum. What does not hold up is converting because a single extension spring broke. That is a spring replacement, and a much smaller one.</p>
<h2>Containment cable is not optional on the system you already have</h2>
<p>If the decision is to keep extension springs, the safety requirement is explicit in the manufacturers' own instructions: each extension spring must have a containment cable running through it, anchored at both ends. One cable per spring, not one for the pair. On a large share of the older doors in this city that cable is either missing entirely or was cut out during a past repair and never reinstalled. Checking for it takes ten seconds with a flashlight and it is the single highest-value thing a homeowner can look at on a door of that vintage. Retrofitting the cables is inexpensive and does not require touching the springs themselves.</p>
<h2>Headroom is what makes or breaks the conversion</h2>
<p>A torsion assembly needs vertical space above the door opening for the shaft, the bearing plates and the spring, typically around twelve inches for a standard setup. Many of the mid-century garages here do not have it. A 1956 Nall Hills garage, in a neighborhood organized around 64 original houses and now covering roughly 1,271 homes between 95th Street and Indian Creek, frequently has a header and a low ceiling that leave four or five inches. Low-headroom torsion kits exist, mounting the shaft on the rear wall or the ceiling with an idler arrangement, and they cost more and require framing that can carry the load. Where neither fits, the correct answer is to keep and properly maintain the extension system.</p>
<h2>What the work actually involves</h2>
<p>The extension springs are unhooked and removed with the door blocked open, along with the pulleys, the safety cables and the S-hooks. A torsion tube is cut to the opening width, cable drums are fitted at each end, end bearing plates and a center bearing bracket are lagged into solid framing above the header, and the spring or springs are sized to the door's measured weight, height and drum diameter. New lift cables run from the bottom brackets to the drums. Then the springs are wound to the calculated number of quarter turns and the door is balanced by hand. Winding is done with proper winding bars, and a screwdriver is not one.</p>
<h2>Framing, permits and honest scope</h2>
<p>The bearing plates transfer the door's entire weight into whatever they are bolted to, so a header with only sheathing behind it needs backing. Adding blocking or a new header is where this stops being service work: the city publishes thirteen categories of work exempt from a building permit, and structural framing is not among them, with permits issued only to contractors holding an active Johnson County contractor license. Most conversions in existing framing need no permit at all. Before committing, price it against the alternatives, because on a tired 1970s door the money often belongs in <a href="../garage-door-replacement/">a new insulated door</a> with torsion hardware included, and where the existing torsion springs are simply worn out, this is a <a href="../garage-door-spring-repair/">spring replacement</a> instead.</p>

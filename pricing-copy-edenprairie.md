## pricing_lede

Below are the ranges garage door jobs actually occupy in Eden Prairie, the fees the city's adopted schedule and Minnesota statute attach to permitted work, and the winter conditions that decide where inside a range a particular door lands.

## pricing_body

### Cold is the variable that shapes this market

The National Weather Service office for the Twin Cities puts current winter averages at 19 days with lows at or below zero and 55 days with highs at or below freezing. A garage door is the largest moving assembly on a house, and it spends that entire stretch cycling between a cold garage and colder outside air. Steel contracts, grease stiffens, torsion springs sit at their least forgiving, and a spring already near the end of its cycle life breaks on the coldest morning of the year rather than on a mild one. That is why the low end of the spring row above belongs to a scheduled weekday visit and the high end belongs to a door that failed at six in the morning in January with a car sealed inside.

The second cold effect is the bottom seal. Water runs off a car, freezes to the slab overnight, and welds the astragal to the floor. The opener then pulls against a door that is stuck, senses excess force, and either reverses or strips a gear. What arrives as an opener complaint is often a frozen seal, a bent bottom bracket, or both, and the honest scope depends on which of those a technician actually finds.

Third, the concrete moves. Frost action lifts and settles slabs through the winter, and a heaved slab leaves a door that no longer meets the floor evenly across its width. Correcting that means adjusting cable tension, shimming a track, or fitting a thicker seal.

### The fees, and what they actually control

Eden Prairie's fee schedule for the administration of official controls, adopted in Chapter 25 of the city code, prices a building permit from valuation: $40.00 where valuation runs from $1 to $500, and $105.25 for the first $2,000 plus $19.75 for each additional $1,000 in the $2,001 to $25,000 band. Plan checking is 65 percent of the building permit fee where plans are reviewed, and a reinspection is $50.00 per hour. On top of the city fee sits the state's construction surcharge under Minnesota Statutes section 326B.148: one-half mill, that is 0.0005 of the permit fee or $1, on fixed-fee permits, and 0.0005 of valuation on permits based on valuation.

The city accepts building permit applications through its paperless ePermits portal and states that permits are required for work regulated by the Minnesota State Building Code, including structural alterations and repairs to permanent weather-resistive surfaces, with all permitted work subject to inspection. In practice, spring, cable, roller and opener replacement on an existing opening is service work. Altering framing, changing an opening, or adding a circuit is the point at which the schedule above and the inspection calendar both apply.

One code detail worth knowing before anybody sells insulation as an energy fix: Minnesota's residential energy code treats a garage as unconditioned space, and requires a nonconditioned space that is altered to become conditioned to comply fully with the code. An insulated door in an attached garage buys a milder garage and a quieter door. It does not by itself make that garage a conditioned room, and any pitch that implies otherwise is selling something the code does not recognize.

### What belongs on a written quote

Springs are specified by four numbers, and a quote that omits them has not measured anything: wire diameter, inside diameter, length, and wind direction, plus the drum series matched to the door's weight and height. Cables get a diameter and a length. Rollers get a stem length and a bearing description, and in a market this cold, sealed bearings are worth their small premium because grease in an open bearing goes stiff.

The rest is arithmetic that should be visible. Trip or diagnostic labor listed separately from repair labor, with a plain statement of whether one credits against the other. Parts named individually rather than as a hardware allowance. On a full replacement: model, steel gauge, insulation value, track configuration, whether the existing opener and rail are being reused, new perimeter weatherstripping and bottom seal, disposal of the old sections, and the permit and surcharge if framing or wiring is involved.

### The trap, stated plainly

Two of them run in this market. The first is the opener sale on a spring problem. A door out of balance overloads the motor, and the symptoms look electronic: hesitation, reversal, a grinding gear. Replace the opener and leave the balance alone, and the new unit fails the same way. The one-minute check is to release the opener, raise the door by hand to about waist height, and let go. A balanced door holds position. A door that drops or climbs is telling you the springs are the problem.

The second is a full-door replacement proposed for cosmetic damage. Steel dents easily, and a dented bottom section on a straight door with sound hardware is a section replacement, not a project. Ask which parts are condemned, ask what measurement condemned each one, and ask whether the door balances once new springs are on it. Where a section genuinely cannot be matched because the profile has been discontinued, the whole-door conversation is legitimate. Where it can be matched, it is not.

### What the online estimate ignores

The averages published nationally price a door and a nominal installation. They omit the state surcharge and the city permit fee on permitted work, the reinspection charge when a first inspection fails, a low-headroom track kit in a garage framed tight to the ceiling, the reinforcement strut a heavier opener needs across the top section, replacing a bottom bracket that corroded from road salt and meltwater, and cold-weather labor on a driveway that has to be cleared before a truck can back into it. On repairs they omit the second worn part that surfaces once the first is off, which on a door that has run through twenty Minnesota winters is the expected outcome rather than a surprise.

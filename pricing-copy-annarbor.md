## pricing_lede

This page lays out what garage door work actually costs in the Ann Arbor market, what the city collects when a job needs a permit, and which features of a Washtenaw County garage push a repair to the upper end of its range.

## pricing_body

### Where the spread inside one row comes from

Three variables decide where any single job lands: which component failed, how much of the door has to be dismantled to reach it, and what hour of the week the truck arrives. A torsion spring on a straight shaft with sound bearing plates is a contained piece of work. The same spring on an Old West Side garage with a shortened header, a shaft that has worn its plates out of round, and winding cones frozen onto it takes considerably longer, and the invoice tracks the hour hand rather than the part number.

Corrosion is the local multiplier. The city treats 346 lane miles of priority streets down to bare pavement through the winter, and every vehicle that pulls in carries that brine over the threshold. Bottom brackets, cable ends, the lower two feet of vertical track and the fasteners buried in the bottom section absorb it first. Swapping a pair of lift cables on clean hardware is quick; swapping them where the bracket bolts shear off inside their holes turns into a bracket and track job too. A range that pretends otherwise gets rewritten in the driveway.

The third variable is approval. Near campus, the person who finds a door stuck halfway is often a tenant while the person who authorizes spending is a manager reached the next morning. That gap changes whether the work happens on a first visit or a second one, and second trips are billable.

### Why this metro prices where it does

Federal wage data explains most of the labor side. Median pay in the general maintenance and repair occupation across the Ann Arbor metro is $23.64 an hour in the May 2025 Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics release. Once a truck, a stocked inventory of springs in several wire sizes, liability coverage, drive time from a shop out on the edge of the city and unproductive hours are loaded on top, the billed figure sits at a multiple of the wage. That is arithmetic, not markup, and it is why an hour saved on access shows up directly in the total.

The permit side is narrow but real. Rehanging a door, replacing springs, cables, rollers or an opener normally stays outside the permit process. Changing the size of the opening, altering the header or running a new circuit for an opener does not. The City of Ann Arbor building permit fee sheet, revised July 2025, prices a permit from the estimated project cost, with $100 covering the first tier and one rough plus one final inspection, and a nonrefundable $15 base application fee attached to every permit. Each additional inspection added to an open permit costs $35. Small money in isolation, but it sets a schedule, and schedules are what stretch a two-hour installation across two calendar days.

One more Ann Arbor step deserves a mention before anybody orders a door. Exterior work inside a local historic district needs an approved Certificate of Appropriateness before it starts, and that review is about how the door looks from the street. Owners in Old Fourth Ward, Water Hill and the streets around them should settle the district question before choosing a panel design, not after the deposit is placed.

### Reading a quote line by line

A quote worth acting on names the wire size, inside diameter and length of the spring going on the shaft, because those three numbers are what make a spring correct for a door. It states the door's weight or drum size rather than calling the door heavy, and separates the diagnostic visit from repair labor with a plain note on whether one credits against the other. It lists hardware individually: rollers by stem length and bearing count, hinges by number, cable by diameter. On a replacement it names the manufacturer, model, gauge of steel, insulation value, track configuration, whether the existing opener is being reused, and who hauls the old sections away.

Two lines get skipped and should not: the reinforcement strut across the top section when a heavy opener is fitted, and the bearing plates. Both are cheap while the door is apart.

### The upsell that runs in this trade

The pressure point is the leap from one broken spring to a full replacement door. A snapped spring produces a dramatic noise and a door that will not budge, which is the moment a homeowner is most likely to agree to whatever is proposed. Sometimes the door genuinely is finished, with rust perforating the bottom section and hinges tearing out of the stiles. Often it is a twenty-dollar bearing and a pair of springs on an otherwise straight door. Ask which specific parts are condemned and what condition each one is in, and ask whether the door still balances at mid-travel once new springs are on it.

The smaller version of the same trap is single-spring thinking. A two-car door running on one spring is not an economy; it is a shorter service life for both the opener and the remaining spring, and the second visit arrives sooner than anyone plans.

### What the figure people find online leaves out

National averages price parts and a flat labor allowance. They leave out the low-headroom track kit an older detached garage often needs, the haul-away of two hundred pounds of steel sections, the after-hours premium on a door frozen open in February, the second trip when a landlord signs off a day later, the extra inspection fee if a permitted job fails the first look, and the wait for historic review on a street where that applies. They also leave out matching: sourcing one section in the color and profile of a discontinued door can cost more than the section is worth, which is how a panel repair becomes a whole-door decision on the numbers alone.
